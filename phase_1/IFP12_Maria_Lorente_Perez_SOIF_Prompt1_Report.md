## Theme 1: The Incipient Network — Choosing "Hub" Over "Network" as a Deliberate Identity

### Summary
The Spanish initiative for future generations deliberately avoids calling itself a "network," choosing instead the term "hub." This is not casual: it reflects a conscious reflection, prompted by SOIF/SWAF, that a "network" implies a more established, structured entity with clearer boundaries and obligations, while a "hub" signals a freer, more open space for convergence and action. The distinction matters because the term shapes expectations for membership, commitment, and governance.

### Detailed Explanation
Maria explains that the choice of terminology came relatively late in the process, prompted by a SOIF-assigned facilitator who asked the group to reflect on what they wanted to be. Until then, terms were used interchangeably "as you would expect." The reflection revealed that "when we speak of network, people seem to understand something that is much more established already, with kind of a clear structure." The group realised they were "not yet at that stage. And maybe we never wanted to be at that stage."

The hub is defined as "more of a convergent place. Not just a meeting place. It's a place where action can happen." Giovanni offers a synthesis that Maria validates: the hub seems to guarantee "at the same time focus and freedom. So you are more free to be yourself, but you focus on what you can put together in order to increase the impact." Maria refines this: the hub is "impact oriented more than a network. The network seems more functioning oriented… it focuses on how we function in democratic terms. And the hub seems: we have a common goal among all the other different goals, and we want to focus on that and increase the impact."

### Specific Elements
- **SWAF/SOIF facilitator**: The person who prompted the reflection on naming
- **Time of reflection**: Came when funds needed to be allocated and a legal recipient was required
- **Key distinction**: Network = established structure, clearer boundaries; Hub = freer, action-oriented, impact-focused
- **Current composition**: ~17 organisations, ~31 individuals (some orgs have 2-3 people involved)
- **Implicit**: The name also serves to avoid scaring away potential members who cannot commit to a formal network

### Key Quotations
- "When we speak of network, people seem to understand something that is much more established already, with kind of a clear structure. And that we were not yet at that stage. And maybe we never wanted to be at that stage."
- "A hub is a place that feels freer. And where you can come in and explore. And see how you can participate, contribute. And also receive if you want from it."
- "It's a convergent place. Not just a meeting place. It's a place where action can happen."

## Theme 2: SOIF/SWAF as Incubator — The Role of an External Accelerator

### Summary
SOIF/SWAF played a critical role in catalysing the Spanish hub, providing not only financial resources but also strategic support, access to Sophie Howe (former Welsh Future Generations Commissioner), facilitation, a shared digital workspace, and a structured approach. The relationship evolved from a "Pledge Network" (low commitment) to more intensive support, functioning as an accelerator that connected existing initiatives rather than creating something artificial.

### Detailed Explanation
Maria describes a two-tier system within SOIF's network: a "Pioneers Network" for more advanced groups and a "Pledge Network" for less advanced ones — where the Spanish group initially belonged. After a period of strategic rethinking at SOIF, the support intensified: "when they had their strategy clear, that eventually they will merge it, and they will try to push everything in one direction, that's when we got a lot of support."

The support was multi-dimensional: presence of Sophie Howe to inspire and lend credibility ("if you needed Sophie to come, or any resources, she will come"), some financial support for events, monthly knowledge-exchange conversations, a dedicated shared workspace (hosted in Google Drive through SOIF), funding for a website, and the assignment of a dedicated person for four months "helping us shape things and structure things." This person prompted the crucial reflection on naming and identity.

Giovanni identifies a pattern: SOIF "intercepts and intervenes" — it does not create networks from scratch in countries where nothing exists, but connects with and accelerates existing initiatives. Maria confirms this model: SOIF's role is to "bring together," "amplify impacts," and "help kick this off properly."

### Specific Elements
- **SOIF/SWAF**: School of International Futures / SWAF — the incubator organisation
- **Sophie Howe**: Former Welsh Commissioner for Future Generations — provided inspiration and institutional credibility
- **Pledge Network vs Pioneers Network**: Two-tier system within SOIF's network
- **Dedicated facilitator**: Person assigned for 4 months to guide strategic reflection
- **Resources**: Website funding, event funding, shared workspace, monthly knowledge exchange
- **Relationship**: Not financial dependence but a nurturing/accelerating role
- **Key events**: Sophie Howe's visit to Spain (~1.5 years ago), first online gathering of all groups

### Key Quotations

## Theme 3: The Dual-Track Origin — Advocacy Core and Expanding Hub

### Summary
The Spanish hub developed from two parallel tracks that eventually converged. A small advocacy core (Greenpeace, Political Watch, REVO) worked on legislative proposals for a Future Generations Commissioner, building direct relationships with the Ministry of Youth. In parallel, a wider group of organisations interested in future generations was assembled with SOIF's support. The two tracks merged approximately 1.5 years ago, creating a hub structure where the advocacy core handles institutional relationships while the broader hub provides legitimacy, diversity, and collective direction.

### Detailed Explanation
Maria traces the origins to Neus Casajuana (president of REVO, a Catalan association for sustainable prosperity) who was "adamant that we needed to do something for future generations." Neus connected with Political Watch and Greenpeace to create a manifesto — a "ten-principle decalogue" for protecting future generations — and successfully met with the youth minister to present it. This small group "holds the relationship with the Ministry of Youth" because "the Ministry of Youth prefers to talk to a very big organization like Greenpeace."

Simultaneously, SOIF-supported conversations gathered a broader set of organisations "that in some shape or form were touching the space of working for future generations." When the two groups converged in the first online gathering of all parties, the structure was proposed: a larger hub (all members, meeting periodically) and a smaller working group (self-selected volunteers) that "crafts proposals, brings them back to the hub for approval, and then puts them in motion." Crucially, the working group is not identical to the original advocacy core — it includes some original members and some newcomers, deliberately avoiding a "founding members" hierarchy.

### Specific Elements
- **REVO**: Neus Casajuana's association — the initial driver
- **Political Watch**: Advocacy organisation — co-wrote legislative proposals
- **Greenpeace Spain**: Large NGO — lent institutional weight for government meetings
- **Ministry of Youth**: Target of advocacy, receptive to meetings
- **Manifesto/Decalogue**: 10 principles for protecting future generations
- **First convergence**: Online meeting ~1.5 years ago bringing both tracks together
- **Working group**: 6 self-selected volunteers (3 from Catalan/Mallorcan-speaking regions)
- **Key structural choice**: Working group ≠ founding members — open to all who volunteer

### Key Quotations
- "We had two different small groups… This small group is the one that held the relationship with the Ministry of Youth. But then when we have come together, it was the entire people."
- "The working group was of people… whoever did want to put their hand up and offer time, they did. And no one was said no you can't."
- "Some people who were in these other groups, and some who were never on those groups. So that identity is not there."

## Theme 4: Internal Governance — Implicit Sociocracy, Dialogic Process, and the Weight of Facilitation

### Summary
The hub operates through an implicit sociocratic approach: decisions aim for consent (everyone "can live with it") rather than full consensus, with explicit invitation for objections and alternative proposals. This is not a formalised procedure but an emergent practice driven by the facilitator's skills and values. Maria acknowledges the constant tension between the desire for efficiency and the need for genuine participatory process — and insists that the latter must prevail even when it feels burdensome.

### Detailed Explanation
Maria describes the decision-making approach as trying "to have conversations where we can really listen to each other constantly." The working method is "to agree by consent, like in sociocracy. So if you can live with it, even if you don't like it 100%, that's great. But if you have an objection, you need to say what the objection is and propose a way forward." This is not formally adopted but is "the way we started to operate — always try to be dialogic."

Giovanni asks about the "weight" of facilitation: whether members sometimes feel that participatory procedures are unnecessary within a group that already shares democratic values. Maria confirms this happens "every day" and strongly disagrees with the shortcut: "because we think we're democratic and because we think we're participatory… that doesn't hold. It never does. Especially if there is something super important to you that is being discussed. The chances of going off participatory approaches, democratic approaches are very high." She cites the proverb: "If you want to go quick, go alone. If you want to go far, go together."

The facilitator role is performed by Maria herself, who brings professional facilitation skills as a consultant. She acknowledges this may not be sustainable as the group grows but sees it as an appropriate contribution at this stage. The group has not yet discussed formalising governance rules, professional facilitation, or rotation of roles.

### Specific Elements
- **Approach**: Implicit consent-based decision making (sociocratic principles)
- **Objection handling**: Must state objection + propose alternative
- **Facilitator**: Maria herself (professional facilitator, voluntary contribution)
- **Tension**: Efficiency vs. participation — "every day" someone wants to skip steps
- **Not yet discussed**: Formal rotation, professional facilitation for larger group
- **Quote source**: "If you want to go quick, go alone. If you want to go far, go together."
- **Implicit risk**: Dependency on Maria's facilitation skills — not yet institutionalised

### Key Quotations
- "We try to have conversations where we can really listen to each other constantly."
- "Because we think we're democratic and because we think we're participatory… that doesn't hold. It never does."
- "If you want to go quick, go alone. If you want to go far, go together."
- "The easiest is to lower the pace, even if it becomes very itchy for people."

## Theme 5: Legitimacy, Legal Form, and the Psychological Contract

### Summary
The hub's lack of a legal form creates practical challenges for receiving and managing funds, but also raises deeper questions about legitimacy — both external (credibility with institutions) and internal (members' sense of belonging and ownership). Maria's aspiration is for a model of "distributed leadership" where "the one who knows best leads," but she acknowledges this is "very difficult with human beings."

### Detailed Explanation
When the hub received its first funding, the question arose: who will receive the funds? Currently, funds are channeled through one of the member organisations — a pragmatic arrangement that "has been transparent and agreed." However, Maria is wary of formalisation: she previously closed an organisation because of bureaucratic burden ("in Spain, it's a pain"). Her preference is "limiting bureaucracy to the minimum."

Giovanni introduces a deeper dimension: the legal form is not just a technical instrument for receiving funds but also shapes the "psychological aspect that allows belonging." The form communicates to current and potential members what kind of entity they are joining. Maria agrees and notes that the conversation about legal form is "just coming" — prompted by having money to receive rather than by strategic design. She dreams of incubation by a larger organisation as an alternative to full formalisation.

Linked to legitimacy is the question of transparency: as the hub becomes visible, it must demonstrate that "there is no obscure hand moving things." The working group is aware that it must maintain "hyper transparency" for both internal members and external observers.

### Specific Elements
- **Current arrangement**: Funds channeled through member organisations (pragmatic)
- **Maria's experience**: Previously closed an organisation due to bureaucracy
- **Aspiration**: Distributed leadership, minimal bureaucracy
- **Hypothesis**: Incubation by larger organisation as alternative to formalisation
- **Legitimacy dimensions**: External (credibility with institutions) and internal (belonging)
- **Transparency**: Hyper-transparency as precondition for trust
- **Current status**: No decision on legal form yet — discussion just beginning

### Key Quotations
- "My dream will be that we have distributed leadership, always. And the one who knows best leads. Period. That seems to be very difficult with human beings."
- "We remain doing the worst. We've decided: no more of the legal part."
- "The legal form can determine the perception of legitimacy of the people who want or not to participate in the battle." (Giovanni's insight, validated by Maria)

## Theme 6: Bipartisanship as a Deliberate Strategy — Avoiding Ideological Hijacking

### Summary
The hub operates with an intentional commitment to bipartisanship: it must not be "hijacked by any ideology." This is not a naive neutrality but a strategic choice to enable broad participation across the political spectrum. In the polarised Spanish context, the hub carefully crafts its messages to focus on unifying themes (e.g., "we all want a good place for people to live") while acknowledging that its very focus on future generations places it in the "progressive" space in most people's perception.

### Detailed Explanation
Maria describes a conscious effort to remain accessible across political divides: "the messages are carefully crafted, not to alienate anyone, but to do the opposite, to actually say, yeah, this is a hub. You're welcome. Everyone is welcome." The strategy is to find "what matters to them that is linked to the overall common goal" — approaching conservative actors through the issues that unite rather than divide.

However, she acknowledges the inherent tension: "in Spain, most likely, just by saying that you're working to protect the rights of present and future generations, you're already placed in the progressive part." This means the hub must engage in "targeted relationship building" with right-wing administrations, making "incursions in the other field" rather than expecting them to come naturally.

Giovanni identifies this as a pattern that will likely be common across democratic networks: the need for "affirmative action or positive discrimination with the right-wing" — a deliberate, asymmetrical effort to reach across the political divide that organisations on the left must make because their very vocabulary creates automatic barriers.

### Specific Elements
- **Strategy**: Find common ground with conservative actors through shared values (e.g., healthy environment for future generations)
- **Message crafting**: Careful language to avoid alienating any political orientation
- **Acknowledged reality**: The hub is perceived as progressive despite bipartisan intention
- **Targeted approach**: Specific outreach to right-wing administrations
- **Giovanni's framing**: "Affirmative action with the right-wing" — deliberate asymmetrical effort
- **Spanish context**: Regional differences (Catalonia, Basque Country) add complexity

### Key Quotations
- "This movement needs to be totally bipartisan. It cannot be hijacked by any ideology."
- "Everyone has something to offer and contribute that we need as a society."
- "The messages are carefully crafted, not to alienate anyone, but to do the opposite."

## Theme 7: Diversity and Its Challenges — Composition, Representation, and Blind Spots

### Summary
The hub's current membership (17 organisations) was built through existing relationships rather than strategic outreach, resulting in limited diversity. Gender as a dimension has not yet been discussed in 1.5 years of operation — a significant gap that Maria acknowledges. The working group is aware that diversity should increase but has not yet developed a strategy for it, partly because internal diversity challenges (e.g., between regions, between experienced and new members) are still being navigated.

### Detailed Explanation
When asked about diversity, Maria is candid: "is there diversity in the 17 organizations? Yes. Is it representative of the society? So is it a fractal of the society? No. Because it was created more on 'who do we know that might be doing something around this.'" The hub grew through personal networks, creating an "opportunistic, relationship-based" composition rather than a strategically designed one.

On gender, the conversation reveals a notable gap. Asked whether gender has been discussed, Maria responds: "We haven't thought about it yet." She reflects on the observation that in many voluntary organisations, "women [are the majority of workers] and are still led by men, mostly." She references Yayo Herrero, a Spanish ecofeminist thinker, and the idea that "abstraction is a privilege" while women tend to "the everyday life things." But no feminist organisation is currently part of the hub, and gender as a governance dimension has not been addressed.

The regional dimension adds another layer: three of the six working group members come from Catalan or Mallorcan-speaking backgrounds, but this has not created tension because "in general, in Spain, both the Basque Country and Catalonia tend to be more innovative." The hub's approach to regional diversity is to "establish the relationships as human beings and individuals before we establish relationships with organizations" — building bonds that transcend potential language or cultural barriers.

## Theme 8: The Evidence Challenge — Observatory, Legitimacy, and Sense-Making

### Summary
The hub plans to establish an observatory to gather and synthesise evidence on future generations, drawing from existing sources (academia, official statistics, research institutes) rather than producing original research. This role — sense-making and translating complex evidence into accessible language — is seen as a core function. The challenge is ensuring the evidence base is legitimate and perceived as bipartisan, avoiding the trap of becoming "just another report producer."

### Detailed Explanation
Giovanni asks about the "highly technical" dimension of evaluation when working on future generations — how the hub will ensure evidence-based credibility. Maria describes a plan for "an observatory where we can actually bring evidence." The hub would not produce its own evidence but would "take it from the different places" and "make sense of it." The goal is to "make it available, and accessible, and understandable" — translating between academic language and the needs of civil society and policymakers.

This function is directly linked to legitimacy: "we really need to make sure that whatever is there is legitimate. And it also doesn't become bipartisan." The observatory is meant to serve as a platform for evidence-based advocacy without being captured by any political orientation.

Maria draws a clear boundary: "if it is already done, why are we going to replicate it? And also, if it will be done by somebody else with more resources in a better way, let them do it." This positions the hub as a curator and translator of evidence rather than a primary producer — a pragmatic division of labour in a resource-constrained environment.
