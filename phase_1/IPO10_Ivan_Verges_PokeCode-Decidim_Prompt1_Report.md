## Theme 1: Contribution Ethics vs. Rhetorical Claims in the Decidim Community

### Summary
Ivan draws a sharp distinction between actors who *speak* about being central to the Decidim community and actors who *demonstrate* centrality through code contributions. He argues that several organisations, especially those that complain about not being heard, contribute nothing to the open-source codebase while generating significant revenue. PokeCode, by contrast, makes multiple contributions per week despite being a small company. For Ivan, contribution is the legitimate currency of belonging.

### Detailed Explanation
The interview opens with a disagreement: the interviewer suggests that the Catalan sub-community is peripheral within Decidim. Ivan rejects this framing. He acknowledges a Catalan linguistic and cultural dimension — Decidim was designed from the start with small-language communities in mind, unlike Consul (Madrid), which resisted Catalan translations. But his main argument is different: the Catalan technical community *contributes* while others do not.

Ivan names one company in Finland as an exception with genuine technical capacity, but claims that almost all other providers — he mentions Octree explicitly — have contributed "zero" to the open-source code. He contrasts this with PokeCode, which sends "several contributions a week." He extends this critique to Open Source Politics (OSP), which he says has now shut down after being acquired. According to Ivan, OSP "made millions" but never contributed meaningful code.

His logic is unambiguous: claiming a voice in the community must be earned through technical contribution. "If all you do is ask and give nothing in return, I don't know… once you contribute, then you're part of the conversation. Otherwise, forget it."

### Specific Elements Mentioned
- **Actors:** Octree (non-contributing company), Open Source Politics / OSP (acquired, no contributions), a company in Finland (competent contributor), Platonic (Ivan's former employer).
- **Practices:** Weekly code contributions as a proof of belonging; open-source contribution as the measure of community legitimacy.
- **Problems/Opportunities:** The community lacks technical depth; non-contributing actors claim centrality on rhetorical grounds; OSP's closure attributed to being "overwhelmed" without a strong technical base.
- **Tensions/Contradictions:** Ivan's insistence on meritocratic contribution as the gate to influence sits uneasily with the participatory and inclusive ethos of the Decidim project itself. The tension between open-source contribution ethics and discursive community participation is a recurring undercurrent.

### Key Quotations
> "If you look at the bare reality, which is that they contribute to the code, you'll see that these companies contribute nothing, zero. And then you look at us and we're full of contributions. We make several a week."

> "For me, you first have to show that you're doing things, and then you'll get any attention. If all you do is ask and give nothing in return, I don't know, once you contribute, then you're part of the conversation. Otherwise, forget it."

## Theme 2: The Hybrid Nature of the Decidim Community — Between Theory and Technology

### Summary
Ivan characterises Decidim as a rare hybrid: a community built simultaneously around a concrete technological object (the software platform) and an abstract theoretical debate (what democratic participation should be). This dual nature creates a unique dynamic, different from purely code-driven open-source communities (like Linux) and purely idea-driven networks (like academic forums). The challenge is to keep both dimensions in productive tension.

### Detailed Explanation
When the interviewer asks whether a network built around a "physical object" (the software) is different from networks built around ideas alone, Ivan agrees and elaborates. He distinguishes three types of communities: (1) purely physical/technical communities like Linux, where contribution *is* membership and theoretical discussion is minimal; (2) idea-driven communities (forums, universities, research), which are easier to enter without technical knowledge; and (3) hybrid communities like Decidim, which must simultaneously manage philosophical debates about participation and the practical constraints of software implementation.

Ivan describes how the theoretical part decides *what should be done*, but the technical part encounters "the limitations of the physical world." The two constantly interact. This hybridity is both a strength — it grounds abstract ideas in concrete tools — and a source of tension, because actors with only theoretical or only technical competence struggle to communicate across the divide.

### Specific Elements Mentioned
- **Comparisons:** Linux (pure technical, contribution = membership), academic/research networks (pure ideas, low entry barrier).
- **Practices:** Translating democratic theory into software features; balancing philosophical ideals with technical feasibility.
- **Problems/Opportunities:** The hybrid nature creates entry barriers for non-technical actors who want influence; but it also ensures that theory does not float free from practical implementation.

### Key Quotations
> "I would say there's the philosophical, theoretical part… about what participation should be, and the practical, technical part. That is, we're going to implement it with the tools that exist… So, you run into the limitations of the physical world."

> "It's a community where if you don't contribute, you're not part of the community. Period."

## Theme 3: PokeCode's Identity — Technical Excellence with a Political Conscience

### Summary
PokeCode was founded on the premise that the Decidim ecosystem had an oversupply of theoretical actors and an undersupply of technically excellent ones. Ivan positions the company as the benchmark for Decidim technical development, but insists that technical work must serve political and democratic values. The company operates as a small team (five people), growing organically from the "bottom up," and sets explicit ethical boundaries in client contracts.

### Detailed Explanation
Ivan traces his personal journey: from a background in mathematics and music, to freelance web development, to working for the crowdfunding platform Goteo, to joining Platonic and working on European Horizon 2020 projects implementing Decidim. When Platonic's management became unsupportive of the Decidim team's growing prominence, Ivan and his colleague Pau left to found PokeCode (previously called Platonic, later rebranded).

The founding idea was to create a company that "specializes in the technical aspects, but with political ideas." Ivan insists that the technical side "must, at the very least, live up to your philosophical promises. In other words, it absolutely has to work. And work well." He describes the company as starting "from the bottom" rather than "from the top" — a deliberate anti-elitist approach.

PokeCode's ethical stance is operationalised through explicit client boundaries: they refuse to build features they consider politically harmful (e.g., disclosing how individuals voted), and they act as "guarantors" of democratic integrity even when clients want otherwise. However, Ivan acknowledges nuance — they did build a pre-screening word-filter for the Italian Green Party (decidiamo.org), even though they advised against it, and the feature was ultimately not used after public controversy.

### Specific Elements Mentioned
- **Actors:** Pau (co-founder), Platonic (former employer), Goteo (crowdfunding platform), Barcelona City Council, Lucerne City Council (first client).
- **Places/Contexts:** Switzerland (first client), Catalonia, Basque Country, New York (potential), Mozambique.
- **Practices:** Ethical clauses in contracts ("we have to be guarantors of certain things"); refusing unethical requests regardless of payment; bottom-up growth strategy.
- **Problems/Opportunities:** The tension between ethical ideals and pragmatic client work (the censorship filter case); the challenge of maintaining principles while growing a business.

### Key Quotations
> "We see that in this world there are many people focused on the theoretical side and very few on the technical side. So we said, let's create a company that specializes in the technical aspects, but with political ideas."

> "The technical aspects must, at the very least, live up to your philosophical promises. In other words, it absolutely has to work. And work well."

> "If someone asks us to conduct a pre-screening process, we may disagree, but we can accept the task and carry it out. However, if a political party is holding a vote and asks us how this person voted, we will say no. And they can pay us whatever they want. There's an ethical limit there."

## Theme 4: The Big Tech Dilemma — Pragmatic Agnosticism vs. Ethical Purity

### Summary
Ivan addresses the tension between using Big Tech infrastructure (WhatsApp, Instagram) and the democratic/ethical values that PokeCode holds. His answer is pragmatic: you cannot escape Big Tech platforms because that is where citizens are, but you can build technical architectures that minimise dependency. The solution is platform-agnostic design with interchangeable connectors.

### Detailed Explanation
The interviewer raises a dilemma: PokeCode worked on a project in Mozambique that used WhatsApp as a participation channel. WhatsApp is owned by Meta (a Big Tech corporation), which raises data sovereignty and ethical concerns. How does a company born from a "philosophical, ethical" environment navigate this?

Ivan's response is fundamentally pragmatic: "Do it however you can. You have to have your ideal world and your practical world, and you navigate between the two." He critiques Octree's WhatsApp integration as technically poor — "a mess" — and explains that PokeCode's approach is to build an interface layer with connectors: one connector for WhatsApp, another for Telegram, another for SMS. This makes the system agnostic and replaceable.

He extends the argument to Instagram: even if he hates Instagram, he must have an account because that is where people are. The same logic applies to the platform architecture. The goal is not purity but *openness* — to ensure that content submitted via WhatsApp remains accessible on the Decidim platform regardless of the channel used.

Ivan also shares an observation about the digital divide in Mozambique: the real gap is not between WhatsApp users and internet users, but between smartphone owners and feature-phone owners. Many people use SMS, not WhatsApp. The "access culture" differs because many African countries skipped the desktop era entirely and went straight to mobile.

### Specific Elements Mentioned
- **Actors:** Octree (WhatsApp integration attempt), Meta (WhatsApp), Mozambican users.
- **Places/Contexts:** Mozambique (Maputo, training of technical staff), Africa generally.
- **Practices:** Connector-based architecture for platform agnosticism; training local technical teams.
- **Problems/Opportunities:** Dependence on Big Tech is unavoidable but can be managed architecturally; the digital divide is more about smartphone access and cultural patterns than about WhatsApp specifically.
- **Tensions/Contradictions:** The interview presses Ivan on whether working with Big Tech forces ethical compromise; Ivan navigates this by distinguishing between *using* a platform and being *dependent* on it.

### Key Quotations
> "I'd say do it however you can. That is, you have to have your ideal world and your practical world, and you navigate between the two."

> "Use Big Tech in the best way possible, because in the end you can't escape it. If I have Instagram, nobody will know I exist. Even if I hate Instagram, I'd have to have an Instagram account."

> "We need to keep opening it up, opening it up as much as possible."

## Theme 5: The Multi-Tenant Debate — Free Access vs. Quality and Autonomy

### Summary
Provincial and regional governments in Catalonia (Barcelona, Girona, Navarre) offer free multi-tenant Decidim instances to municipalities. Ivan sees both positive and negative consequences: free access lowers the barrier to entry, but it also creates a ceiling on ambition. Municipalities that want to go further in their participatory practices eventually outgrow the multi-tenant model.

### Detailed Explanation
The interviewer asks about the impact of provincial multi-tenant systems (Diputació de Barcelona's "311 Participants", the Girona system, and a new one in Navarre) on the market for Decidim services. Ivan gives a balanced assessment.

**Negative side:** As a company, PokeCode loses potential clients because municipalities that would have hired them now get a free service. Moreover, because the service is free, municipalities do not value it properly. There is no dedicated support — "I called you this morning, how do I do this? I can't remember where the button is" — and no customisation. The quality is "not very good."

**Positive side:** Ivan doubts that cost is the real barrier. "A city council can spend some money. It's not a problem for them to spend €10,000, €5,000 a year on having a participation website. They spend thousands of euros on lights, on nonsense." The multi-tenant model attracts municipalities that want to "comply with the law" minimally, while municipalities that are genuinely motivated invest in their own instance.

PokeCode's client Reus City Council, for example, deliberately chose a standalone instance rather than the multi-tenant. Why? Because they want the autonomy to add custom modules (like anonymous proposals) and run participatory budgeting differently. In a multi-tenant system, "if we make it available to you, we have to make it available to everyone."

Ivan suggests that the ideal pathway for a motivated municipality is to start with the free multi-tenant, and when they outgrow it, migrate to a dedicated instance. But he admits this rarely happens in practice.

### Specific Elements Mentioned
- **Actors:** Diputació de Barcelona (311 Participants), Diputació de Girona, Gobierno de Navarra (PokeCode is supplier), Reus City Council, Olesa City Council.
- **Places/Contexts:** Catalonia, Navarre, Portugal (multi-tenant need, failed project).
- **Practices:** Multi-tenant as entry-level participation; standalone instances for committed municipalities; the support gap in free services.
- **Problems/Opportunities:** Free provision reduces willingness to invest; lack of customisation limits participation depth; multi-tenant creates a two-tier system of "compliant" vs. "committed" municipalities.

### Key Quotations
> "As a company, you think, damn, potential towns that could hire you aren't going to hire you for the free service. And it's true, it happens. But what else happens? Because it's free, they don't appreciate it. And because it's free, there's no support service either."

> "If you offer it for free, all they see is that they can invest a little money. The people who hire you want to do it for real."

> "People who want to get more involved don't join the multi-team."

## Theme 6: Governance Tensions and Political-Cultural Friction within Decidim

### Summary
Ivan describes several concrete governance conflicts within the Decidim community that reveal underlying political and cultural differences. The most striking is a dispute with Finnish contributors who wanted administrators to be able to edit citizens' content — a feature Ivan's team rejected as "blatant manipulation." He links this to Nordic vs. Mediterranean cultural assumptions about trust in authority.

### Detailed Explanation
Ivan identifies two interconnected governance issues. First, a technical governance dispute: Finnish contributors wanted administrators to have the ability to edit any citizen's proposals directly. From Ivan's Mediterranean perspective, this is unacceptable — the equivalent of a public official rewriting a citizen's submission. From the Finnish perspective (as Ivan interprets it), administrators are trusted to improve content quality. Ivan connects this to a broader cultural pattern: "Nordic culture has this implicit acceptance that those who govern will do a good job… from here, a more Mediterranean perspective is out of the question."

The second is about the broader governance model of the Decidim association. Ivan criticises the association for not providing clear enough protocols for how external contributors should engage. "I would also criticize the Decidim association for providing guidelines so that people can do things better. Sometimes that part is missing too, to be honest."

He also touches on the Finland-Palestine controversy, noting that the Finnish contributor was "strictly on the side of Israel" but that this did not escalate seriously because "the software itself is agnostic." However, he adds that the Finnish technical lead also has a personal style he dislikes: "He doesn't read your messages, he doesn't reply, he doesn't accept your messages, nothing. I just don't like him."

Ivan estimates the core Decidim technical community at only 4–8 people. In such a small group, every conflict is amplified: "when someone makes a scene or has a few of these issues, it's very noticeable." If there were 200 contributors, these tensions would dissolve.

### Specific Elements Mentioned
- **Actors:** Finnish contributors (Mainio Tech / the technically capable Finnish company), Decidim association, Open Source Politics (OSP, former partner that provided funding), Platonic (never became a partner).
- **Practices:** Governance proposals and debates within the Decidim community; the partnership model for funding.
- **Problems/Opportunities:** Cultural differences in democratic trust (Nordic vs. Mediterranean); small community size amplifies conflicts; lack of clear contribution protocols from the association; some actors disagree with the partnership model itself.
- **Tensions/Contradictions:** Ivan criticises others for not contributing while also criticising the association for not providing enough guidance — the relationship between contribution and governance remains unclear.

### Key Quotations
> "In Finland, they initially wanted administrators to be able to edit people's content… from our perspective, doing that is blatant manipulation."

> "Nordic culture has this implicit acceptance that those who govern will do a good job… from here, a more Mediterranean perspective is out of the question."

> "There are only 4 or 5 of us. 5, 8, or 1 makes a difference. And if there were 200 of us, it wouldn't be a problem."

## Theme 7: The Realities of the Participation Market — Low Engagement and the Paradox of Institutional Willingness

### Summary
Ivan offers a sobering assessment of democratic participation software as a market: most citizens are simply not interested in participating. The paradox is that institutions pay for a platform that may generate criticism against themselves. The market is small, and the motivational structure of participation platforms fundamentally differs from social media.

### Detailed Explanation
The interviewer suggests that if Decidim were proprietary software, the market would likely be larger. Ivan disagrees: the money involved is not "spectacular." The deeper issue is that participation is not a natural human drive for most people. He uses the example of Som Energía, the largest energy cooperative in Europe (based in Girona), which uses Decidim for its internal democratic processes. Only 0.3% of members participate in elections. His explanation: people care about the service (green energy), not about the governance.

He contrasts this with Decidiamo (the Italian Green Party's instance), which has very high participation — but that is because party members are already politically sensitised. For the general public, creating a user account on a participation platform offers none of the ego gratification of social media. "Why would I want to create a user account if no one's going to boost my ego?"

Ivan highlights a fundamental paradox: the institution pays for the platform, but the participation it enables will often be *against* its own interests. "The institution that wants to participate pays, and often that participation will be detrimental to its own interests. Because people are going to complain, they're going to call him a legend, corrupt, or whatever. But even so, the politician has to create this platform and pay for it."

### Specific Elements Mentioned
- **Actors:** Som Energía (energy cooperative), Decidiamo (Italian Green Party), Reus City Council, Barcelona en Comú.
- **Places/Contexts:** Catalonia, Italy (Decidiamo).
- **Practices:** Internal party democracy (high engagement); cooperative governance (low engagement); social media vs. participation platform comparison.
- **Problems/Opportunities:** Structural lack of citizen interest; the paradox of institutional clients paying for criticism; small overall market size.

### Key Quotations
> "It's simply the kind of product that brings democracy into people's daily lives. Most people aren't interested."

> "Why would I want to create a user account if no one's going to boost my ego? That's the point. Because that's how other social media platforms end up."

> "The institution that wants to participate pays, and often that participation will be detrimental to its own interests."

## Theme 8: Digital Voting — Distrust, Security, and the Limits of Technology

### Summary
Ivan is deeply sceptical about digital voting for large-scale binding elections. He distinguishes between low-stakes internal organisational votes (acceptable) and high-stakes general elections (impossible to secure properly). The core problem is that no technical system can verify that the person voting is who they claim to be, given that the voter's own device may be compromised.

### Detailed Explanation
The interviewer asks about creative voting systems — the Borda count and other preferential voting methods. Ivan confirms that PokeCode is developing a Borda system for Barcelona en Comú. But the real obstacle is not algorithmic complexity; it is political fear. Politicians fear that citizens will perceive complex voting systems as "black boxes" designed to cheat them.

Ivan goes further: he doubts digital voting will ever be implemented for large-scale binding elections. The fundamental problem is endpoint security: "How do I know it's you voting and not someone using your information on your behalf? That's impossible to verify." He contrasts this with in-person voting, where physical presence verifies identity. Digital voting works for "pronouncements" and internal organisational elections (like Barcelona's district council elections) because the stakes are lower and the scale is manageable.

He extends this to AI: "If they're good at it now, I'm sure they'll be even better at it in the future with AI." The only scenario where digital voting could be secure is under "brutal surveillance" — a condition that is itself anti-democratic.

### Specific Elements Mentioned
- **Actors:** Barcelona en Comú, Italian Green Party.
- **Places/Contexts:** Barcelona (district council elections), Italy, Portugal (vote-buying cases detected through IP address monitoring).
- **Practices:** Borda count voting system development; alarm systems for detecting coordinated voting fraud.
- **Problems/Opportunities:** Endpoint security is the unsolvable problem for digital voting; complex voting systems face political fear of being seen as manipulative; practical fraud (family voting, vote buying) is detectable but not preventable.

### Key Quotations
> "How do I know it's you voting and not someone using your information on your behalf? That's impossible to verify."

> "I highly doubt we'll ever implement a digital voting system… Maybe one day we'll have a brutal surveillance system and then they'll be sure it's us. But private corporations will also be involved."

## Theme 9: AI as a Learning Accelerator — Strategy for Small-Team Capacity Building

### Summary
Ivan views AI not as a replacement for developers but as a tool for learning and acceleration. He actively encourages his team to use AI to understand code, question its solutions, and expand their skills. His goal is to make himself increasingly unnecessary by building a self-sufficient technical team.

### Detailed Explanation
When asked whether AI is changing PokeCode's operations, Ivan describes a positive but nuanced impact. As an experienced programmer, he uses AI to quickly search for solutions and understand new approaches: "It helps me look things up quickly." For his team, especially junior members, the value is different. He cites the example of Elvia, an employee who joined without programming knowledge three years ago and has now published a technical article.

Ivan's philosophy is that AI should be used to *learn*, not to *shirk*. He challenges his team: when AI gives you a solution, ask it *why* it chose that approach. "If he doesn't use AI-coding, he won't learn anything with us and he won't progress." The risk is that AI becomes a crutch that replaces understanding.

His ultimate aspiration is revealing: "My goal would be for me to no longer be needed. Making myself unnecessary would be my objective." This is consistent with the company's bottom-up, anti-hierarchical ethos.

### Specific Elements Mentioned
- **Actors:** Elvia (PokeCode employee, started without programming knowledge, now publishes).
- **Practices:** Using AI to ask "why this solution?"; AI as a teaching tool; publishing articles about the learning journey.
- **Problems/Opportunities:** Risk of AI replacing understanding vs. opportunity to accelerate skill development; AI as a force multiplier for small teams.

### Key Quotations
> "If my workers use the AI to learn and communicate, then I have… Use AI to learn and we'll increase our capacity tenfold. If it's used to shirk work, it's useless to me."

> "My goal would be for me to no longer be needed. Making myself unnecessary would be my objective."

## Theme 10: Implicit Theme — The Size and Fragility of the Decidim Technical Ecosystem

### Summary
Throughout the interview, Ivan repeatedly emphasises how small the Decidim technical community really is. This smallness has consequences: every conflict is amplified, the market is fragile, and the ecosystem depends heavily on a handful of actors. It also means that political and technical disputes become personal quickly.

### Detailed Explanation
This theme is not stated explicitly as a single argument, but it emerges from multiple points in the interview. Ivan describes the core technical community as "4 or 5" or "5, 8" people. He notes that when someone leaves or causes friction, "it's very noticeable" — a sharp contrast with a hypothetical larger community of 200 where individual conflicts would dilute.

The small size also explains the governance problems: there are not enough contributors to sustain a healthy division of labour between technical work, governance, and community management. The Decidim association struggles to provide adequate protocols because it too is resource-constrained. The market itself is small — "there isn't that much of a market for equity participation" — so the ecosystem cannot easily grow.

The interview contains an implicit warning: the Decidim ecosystem is a small, fragile system where the loss of one or two key actors (PokeCode included) would be significant. The OSP closure is presented as a case study of what happens when technical capacity is absent.

### Specific Elements Mentioned
- **Actors:** PokeCode, Octree, OSP, Finnish contributors, Decidim association.
- **Practices:** Self-reinforcing smallness (few contributors → high visibility of conflict → discouragement of new contributors).
- **Problems/Opportunities:** The ecosystem may not survive the loss of key technical actors; small size limits governance capacity.

### Key Quotations
> "There are only 4 or 5 of us. 5, 8, or 1 makes a difference. And if there were 200 of us, it wouldn't be a problem. But we're not there yet."

> "There isn't that much of a market for equity participation, to be honest. At least not at the moment."

## Synthesis: Cross-Cutting Observations

Several threads run across multiple themes in Ivan Vergés's interview:

1. **Contribution as legitimacy:** The most consistent position Ivan takes is that legitimacy in the Decidim ecosystem must be earned through concrete contributions — especially code. This applies to companies, to the Decidim association, and to individual contributors.

2. **The technical deficit:** Ivan repeatedly notes that the Decidim ecosystem has too many theoretical/political actors and too few technically competent ones. He positions PokeCode as filling this gap, but the imbalance remains a structural weakness.

3. **Pragmatic idealism:** Despite strong ethical positions, Ivan shows consistent pragmatism — using Big Tech, building censorship filters when asked, accepting the multi-tenant trade-offs. His idealism is operationalised through architectural choices (connectors), contractual boundaries (red lines), and team development (AI as learning tool), not through purity.

4. **Scale as a constraint and an explanation:** Again and again, Ivan explains problems — conflicts, governance gaps, market limitations — by reference to the small size of the community. The implication is that without growth, these problems will persist.

5. **The multi-level governance gap:** The interview illustrates a disconnect between local (municipal), regional (provincial), and European levels of democratic participation. The multi-tenant system enables compliance but not intensification. European projects fund development but do not create sustainable local ecosystems.
