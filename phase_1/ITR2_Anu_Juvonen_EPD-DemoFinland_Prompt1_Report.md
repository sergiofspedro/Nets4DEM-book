## Theme 1: EPD as a Network of Organisations – Evolution and the Third Pillar

### Summary
Juvonen describes EPD as a network of organisations (not a network of networks) that evolved from a dual focus on advocacy and EU funding programming into a three-pillar structure with the addition of research through the Democracy Hub. She highlights the diversity of members—geographic and thematic—as EPD's core strength, and notes the constant stream of membership applications the network receives.

### Detailed Explanation
Juvonen became executive director of Demo Finland in 2016 and has been on EPD's board since its formation. She characterises EPD's early work as "very much focused on programming… content information on EU calls but also on advocacy." The secretariat provided a collective voice in Brussels that many members lacked individually.

The addition of research as a third pillar came when the network identified "a gap in our work… to provide evidence-based information for decision makers and for us members." This created the Democracy Hub, which Juvonen considers "a success." She highlights recent collaborative research—"the China report together with them on China's soft power in global south"—produced with Carnegie.

She emphasises that "the strength of EPD lies in its diversity of members," and the network is "receiving a lot of member applications constantly" while balancing "a geographical footprint but also the thematic focusses." This balance is an ongoing conversation.

### Specific Elements
- **EPD structure**: Network of organisations (not network of networks)
- **Three pillars**: Advocacy, programming, research (Democracy Hub)
- **Democracy Hub**: Evidence-based research, collaboration with Carnegie
- **Membership growth**: From founding few to 21 members
- **Diversity**: Geographical and thematic balance is a constant negotiation
- **Key report**: China's soft power in global south (January 2025)
- **Juvonen's roles**: ED of Demo Finland (2016–), EPD board president

### Key Quotations
- "The strength of EPD lies in its diversity of members."
- "We felt that there is a gap in our work… to provide evidence-based information for decision makers."

## Theme 2: The Value of In-Person Connection – Retreats and Peer Culture

### Summary
Juvonen strongly values face-to-face meetings as essential for building trust and strategic alignment within EPD. She reinstated the board retreat to create space for "in-depth reflections" beyond formal board meetings. The peer-to-peer culture among executive directors of member organisations is described as a rare and valuable space for mutual support.

### Detailed Explanation
EPD's board meetings and Annual General Meetings are "always in person… at least twice a year" by rule. Juvonen explains: "you can't take part through technology for various reasons" but primarily because "it's encouraging people to come physically… these meetings are so fruitful." The informal exchange leads to concrete cooperation: "we also cooperate outside of EPD… as a Nordic member with Oslo Centre and DIPD the Danish one, we are currently preparing a programme or two together."

As board president, Juvonen introduced the retreat—"first time last end of August"—because she felt "we have such great minds in the board as well as the secretariat representatives." The retreat provides "in-depth reflections" that formal board meetings cannot accommodate: "when we have a full day of the board we don't necessarily have time for in-depth reflections." The first retreat was 2.5 days; the current one is 2 days.

The peer dimension is crucial for Juvonen personally: "as an executive director, it's not so simple to maybe find your… peer groups." EPD provides "an important peer group as a director as well because we are having similar issues… whether it's about funding or staff or whatever." This has led to genuine friendships.

Beyond EPD, she participates in the Nordic Meeting (yearly, informal) and the Political Party Peer Network (loose, unregistered), which brings together democracy foundations across Europe.

### Specific Elements
- **EPD board**: In-person only, twice yearly
- **Retreat**: Introduced by Juvonen; 2–2.5 days for strategic depth
- **Peer value**: Directors share challenges on funding, staffing, strategy
- **Nordic cooperation**: Informal yearly meeting of Nordic EPD members
- **Political Party Peer Network**: Unregistered, rotating chair (Warsaw 2025)
- **Technology**: Useful but cannot replace face-to-face for trust-building

### Key Quotations
- "I mean they are so fruitful these meetings."
- "It's an important peer group as a director as well because we are having similar issues."
- "The retreat… is really to ensure key people have time for in-depth discussions."

## Theme 3: Membership Governance – The Balance Between Openness and Selectivity

### Summary
EPD manages membership through formal criteria (international work required, domestic-only organisations excluded), thematic and geographic balance, and a rigorous assessment process involving interviews and board-level Q&A. Rejected applicants do not receive detailed feedback. Juvonen frames selectivity as necessary for the network's coherence and added value.

### Detailed Explanation
The formal rule is that "the organisation needs to have international work." A domestic-only organisation cannot join: "if it's a purely Italian democracy NGO that works only in Italy, then sorry no." Geographic balance matters—the network currently lacks Baltic representation, which is "a pity."

Thematic balance is also important: "it's important to keep the thematic balance also because… there's a different angle from different organisations, different added value." The process involves an assessment format, interviews, and if there is "even a lighter green light," the organisation presents to the board in person for Q&A.

On rejections: "our rules also say that we do not need to give feedback why they are not selected." In practice, "we of course give some feedback," but "we don't need to go into detail." The process is governed by Chatham House rules.

### Specific Elements
- **Membership criteria**: International work required; domestic-only excluded
- **Geography**: Need for Baltic representation identified as gap
- **Thematic balance**: Deliberate curation of complementary specialisations
- **Process**: Assessment → interview → board presentation (in person)
- **Transparency**: No obligation to detail rejection reasons
- **Current applications**: Constant stream; selectivity increases as network grows

### Key Quotations
- "If it's a domestic like… purely works in Italy, then sorry no."
- "We need to think of the network itself, of what brings added value."

## Theme 4: Equality Among Members – The A/B Category Reform

### Summary
Juvonen recounts her role in reforming EPD's membership fee structure to ensure fairness. Previously, fees were flat amounts that disproportionately burdened smaller organisations. She proposed a percentage-of-budget model so that all members—large and small—pay proportionally and are valued equally. The reform addressed a perception that larger members had disproportionate influence.

### Detailed Explanation
The A/B membership categories determine board eligibility (A members can serve on the board, B members cannot). But the financial side was problematic: "there wasn't a clear figure of how much of your annual budget the membership fee would be." Demo Finland was paying about €5,000 on a €2 million budget, while a larger member paid €15,000 on a much larger budget—meaning Demo Finland paid a higher percentage.

Juvonen raised this: "I was like well if you look at the overall budget percentage, why we're actually paying more." The reform linked fees to a percentage of annual budget, making contributions proportional: "we are all kind of equal in that term… we are as important even if it's only €5,000."

The board size also had to evolve: there was "a set number of board members that could be board members," but as the network grew, this became too restrictive. The limit was lifted, though this created "a bit more competition" for board seats—Juvonen recalls one year having to "campaign… say a couple of words why it would be so very important."

### Specific Elements
- **A members**: Pay higher fee, eligible for board; based on annual budget percentage
- **B members**: Lower fee, not board-eligible; can evolve to A status
- **Reform**: Proposed by Juvonen ~6 years ago; fee linked to budget percentage
- **Impact**: More equitable; small organisations valued equally
- **Board evolution**: Size limit lifted as network grew; more competition for seats

### Key Quotations
- "We are all kind of equal in that term… we are as important even if it's only €5,000."
- "The rules and regulations are evolving… responding to the needs of the day."

## Theme 5: The Political Challenge – Autocratisation and the Democratic Playbook

### Summary
Juvonen identifies the political scenery—rising autocratisation, shrinking civic space, and democratic backsliding within the EU—as EPD's most critical challenge. She cites specific data (V-Dem, civic space reports) and examples (Slovakia, France, UK, Finland's collapse in academic freedom). Her prescription is a "democrats' playbook" to counter the systematic "autocratisers' playbook."

### Detailed Explanation
Juvonen is deeply informed on democratic trends: "the rise of the autocratisation… according to the V-Dem report in 74 percent or whatever it is." Civic space is contracting: "Italy no longer open civic space, UK, France… liberal democracies are in decrease." Slovakia is in the "top 10 of world's quickest autocratisers."

She notes how autocratisation works incrementally: "little corners are being cut here and there." One particularly alarming example is Finland: "Finland of all the bloody countries dropped down about 40 points within one year on academic freedom." She calls this "breaking news on the front pages" that went largely unreported.

The response must be systematic: "we need to strengthen the democrats' playbook and really analyse how the autocratisation is taking place because there are similar trends." She highlights cross-border learning: "I learned that the Slovakian civil society is engaging with the Czech civil society where you also see deterioration of democracy."

### Specific Elements
- **V-Dem data**: 74% of countries autocratising
- **EU problem cases**: Slovakia (top 10 fastest autocratisers), France, UK
- **Italy**: No longer classified as open civic space
- **Finland**: 40-point drop in academic freedom in one year
- **Mechanism**: "Autocratisers' playbook" is systematic; democrats need an equivalent
- **Cross-border learning**: Slovak and Czech civil societies cooperating on democratic defence
- **Funding risk**: Some want to defund advocacy/LGBTIQ+ organisations

### Key Quotations
- "There are similar trends… the autocratisers' playbook is valid."
- "Finland of all the bloody countries dropped down about 40 points within one year on academic freedom."
- "There are so many political levels that we should really try to be stronger in our collective action."

## Theme 6: Self-Censorship and the Veto – Navigating Hot Topics

### Summary
EPD's rule that a single member can veto any external statement creates a structural dynamic around controversial issues. Juvonen describes how the network navigates this through case-by-case dialogue, distinguishing between the network's position and individual members' positions. The Israel-Palestine issue is the most prominent example of a topic where consensus has not been possible.

### Detailed Explanation
When asked about self-censorship on hot topics, Juvonen acknowledges the dynamic: "EPD is very regularly invited to come on board campaigns or statements." The network's stance is: "we are not an activist network… it's a practitioners' network." This framing allows EPD to be selective about which campaigns it joins.

The veto rule is clear: "if one member opposes, then EPD is not signing." This gives each member "a quite a strong veto power," but Juvonen believes "people use that wisely and they really explain why they don't want EPD to sign a certain thing."

The result is a subsidiarity-like arrangement: "in some cases the members individually join these campaigns whereas EPD not." Members are free to act individually when the network cannot reach consensus. Juvonen frames this as healthy: "there's not a flat line you can draw because world changes, politics changes, positions change."

The interviewer connects this to self-censorship, noting that fear of unbalancing the network on Palestine/Israel has led to silence. Juvonen implicitly acknowledges this dynamic without naming the specific case that Berman described (the failed Gaza statement).

### Specific Elements
- **Veto rule**: Single member blocks EPD from signing external statements
- **Network identity**: "Not an activist network" – practitioners first
- **Subsidiarity**: Members act individually when network cannot agree
- **Case-by-case**: No flat rule; depends on topic, context, political moment
- **Dialogue**: Explaining veto reasons is expected and practiced
- **Self-censorship**: Acknowledged as a risk without explicit confirmation

### Key Quotations
- "If one member opposes, then EPD is not signing."
- "We are not an activist network however you would define it, it's a practitioners' network."
- "There's not a flat line you can draw because world changes, politics changes, positions change."

## Theme 7: Demo Finland – Unique Dual Mandate and Inclusivity Pioneer

### Summary
Demo Finland is distinctive among EPD members for having a domestic mandate to work within Finland alongside international programming. Founded 20 years ago by the Finnish Parliament and Foreign Ministry, it was a pioneer in focusing on underrepresented groups (youth, women, persons with disabilities) from day one. Juvonen takes pride in this track record and the deep inclusivity expertise her organisation has built.

### Detailed Explanation
Demo Finland's uniqueness lies in its dual mandate: "we work in eight countries including Finland… we have a mandate to work in Finland." Domestically, Demo Finland works with "youth wings, women's wings, and the party" on "different capacity building, different dialogue programmes." This is rare among democracy support organisations.

Internationally, Demo Finland does not set up country offices but works through careful partner selection: "it's a national civil society organisation, impartial political party-wise." The emphasis is on partnership, not donor-recipient relationships: "working with us is very different because we are really the partners, we are not donors."

Juvonen is particularly proud of Demo Finland's inclusivity track record: "since day one, 20 years ago, the focus was on underrepresented groups in politics: youth and women." More recently, "persons with disabilities… have more agency as policymakers rather than objects of policies." She criticises superficial gender mainstreaming: "people often look at the data from IPU on women in parliaments… but they don't fall from the sky to the parliaments, they grow from civil society."

### Specific Elements
- **Founded**: 20 years ago by Finnish Parliament and Foreign Ministry
- **Dual mandate**: Domestic (Finland) + international (7 other countries)
- **International model**: No country offices; partner-led implementation
- **Inclusivity**: Youth, women from day one; persons with disabilities added later
- **Budget**: Small (~€2M) but high-impact per dollar
- **Results framework**: Intensive monitoring and evaluation to demonstrate impact
- **Key insight**: Women in parliament come from local-level and civil society pipelines

### Key Quotations
- "Since day one, 20 years ago, the focus was on underrepresented groups in politics: youth and women."
- "Working with us is very different because we are really the partners, we are not donors."
- "They don't fall from the sky to the parliaments, they grow from civil society, from local council level."

## Theme 8: Global South Engagement – Partnership as Anti-Colonial Praxis

### Summary
Demo Finland's approach to working in the global South is built on partner-led design, long-term commitment, and careful selection. Programmes are co-created with local organisations based on local needs, political context, and culture. Juvonen—trained as an anthropologist—emphasises listening, flexibility, and responsiveness over imposing external agendas.

### Detailed Explanation
Demo Finland works in "numerous African countries as well as in Myanmar, Thailand, and Kosovo." The methodology is deliberate: "partner selection is done extremely carefully; all the programmes are planned together with the leadership of the local needs, local culture, local political setup." A political economy analysis is conducted for each country.

The organisation prides itself on flexibility: "we change plans if need be if the elections are changed… our colleagues on the ground from our partner organisation inform us about a gap that should be addressed." This responsiveness is confirmed by external evaluations: "we are thanked for in our evaluations often that working with us is very different because we are really the partners."

Juvonen's anthropology background informs her approach: "I'm very aware of this… it matters." The multi-year strategy (2025–2029) was developed through "a lot of consultations and partner meeting here with extensive discussions." Local partners are treated as "key stakeholders," not implementers of predetermined plans.

### Specific Elements
- **Countries**: Multiple in Africa, plus Myanmar, Thailand, Kosovo
- **Method**: Partner selection → political economy analysis → co-created programmes
- **Flexibility**: Programmes adjusted on partner feedback, political changes
- **Evaluation feedback**: Partners highlight non-donor, partnership approach
- **Strategy 2025–2029**: Co-developed with extensive partner consultations
- **Juvonen's background**: Anthropology degree informs approach

### Key Quotations
- "All the programmes are planned together with the leadership of the local needs, local culture, local political setup."
- "We change plans if need be… we are really the partners, we are not donors."
- "Working with us is very different… we do not go and tell what is to be done."

## Theme 9: Democratic Resilience and the Risk of Securitisation

### Summary
Juvonen introduces a critical perspective on the concept of "democratic resilience": while useful, it risks being co-opted into a narrow securitisation agenda. She warns that civil society should not be reduced to "service providers of comprehensive security," as this distorts the diverse roles that civil society organisations play. The democratic leg of resilience—improving democratic quality—must not be forgotten.

### Detailed Explanation
Juvonen agrees the concept is "blurred" but sees a more fundamental risk: "the whole security and democracy as a security asset… we have to be extremely careful and vigilant… that this securitisation… is very much of a term that's been implied everywhere." In Finland, the discourse has shifted to "defence and preparedness," with NGOs being asked to frame their work in security terms to access funding and visibility.

Her concern is that this "reduces" civil society: "not every civil society is dealing with security… what about environmental human rights defenders… LGBTIQ, child rights, mental health associations?" The securitisation lens threatens to narrow funding, policy attention, and the very definition of civil society's role.

She argues for a more balanced approach: "we need to put more emphasis on the internal security risks as well… it's very externally defined at the moment." She points to internal forces—"anti-rights, anti-gender civil society organisations that are aiming at destabilising"—that are overlooked when the focus is exclusively on external threats.

She also warns against diminishing civil society's role in policymaking: "this is not the time to do that, this is the time to increase that." The European Centre for Democratic Resilience must be "more responsive for civil society," because "not all the wisdom is at the headquarters."

### Specific Elements
- **Concept**: Democratic resilience is useful but blurred
- **Risk**: Securitisation narrows civil society to "comprehensive security providers"
- **Examples**: Environmental, LGBTIQ, child rights, mental health—all marginalised by security framing
- **Internal threats**: Anti-rights and anti-gender organisations destabilise from within
- **Response**: Need to strengthen democratic quality alongside protection
- **European Centre for Democratic Resilience**: Needs more civil society responsiveness
- **Key demand**: Not the time to reduce civil society consultations—time to increase them

### Key Quotations
- "We have to be extremely careful that the civil society is not put into the narrow corner of securitisation."
- "This is not the time to diminish the role of civil society… this is the time to increase that."
- "Not all the wisdom is at the headquarters."

## Theme 10: Commoning Inside and Outside the Network

### Summary
Juvonen strongly affirms the value of EPD as a common good, both for members and for broader democracy support. The network facilitates cooperation, knowledge-sharing, and capacity-building that extends beyond formal EPD activities into bilateral member-to-member partnerships. She values the permeability of the network—its openness to external experts, researchers, and individual contributors.

### Detailed Explanation
For Juvonen, EPD is "extremely useful, it's very cooperative within the network through EPD work what we do together, but also what we do individually." She gives the example of cooperation "with Westminster Foundation for Democracy in Kenya" as member-to-member programming that happens outside EPD's direct coordination.

The network also creates permeable spaces for individual contributions. Juvonen describes Demo Finland's "Democracy Hour"—a one-hour webinar with keynotes and conversation. Through EPD connections, she brought Joe Brinker from NATO's Centre for Democratic Resilience to meet Finnish parliamentarians and MFA officials: "this was just through meeting him and having a conversation with him and realising the added value for both."

She also highlights cooperation with academics: "we cooperate a lot with Finnish academics in various ways." Demo Finland was paid to organise a workshop connecting researchers' findings with politicians, "tailor-making" and further developing the research together.

This permeability—the ability to bring in external expertise and create ad hoc spaces for collaboration—is a key feature of how the network functions as a common good.

### Specific Elements
- **Internal commoning**: Bilateral member-to-member cooperation (e.g., with Westminster Foundation in Kenya)
- **External permeability**: Experts, researchers, advisors brought into network spaces
- **Democracy Hour**: Demo Finland's open webinar format with external keynotes
- **NATO connection**: EPD contacts enabled Finnish parliamentary engagement on democratic resilience
- **Academic cooperation**: Researchers pay Demo Finland to facilitate politician workshops
- **Value**: Network creates opportunities that individual members could not access alone

### Key Quotations
- "It's extremely useful, it's very cooperative… we do a lot of cooperation outside like member-to-member."
- "We are having great results even if our budgets are small… we always ensure it's sustainable."
- "There are numerous ways… experts outside of our member organisations delivering fresh data, fresh insights."
