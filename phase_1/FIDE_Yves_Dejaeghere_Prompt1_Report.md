## Theme 1: The Founding Gap — When National Legal Form Frustrates International Ambition

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
FIDE was born from a structural mismatch: its precursor organisation, the Belgian think tank G1000, was doing internationally recognised work on deliberative democracy but was trapped by its Belgian legal status. International funders could not support a Belgian association, even when its work was European in scope. Simultaneously, the European Commission and Council of Europe were searching for a reliable interlocutor in the deliberative democracy space as the "deliberative wave" accelerated — but found only a fragmented field of independent actors. The convergence of these push and pull factors created the founding moment for FIDE.

**Detailed explanation:**  
Yves begins by describing G1000, the Belgian think tank on the "liberty of democracy" where he worked. After the Belgian G1000 project, he was "asked left, right and centre to either give advice, come talk, et cetera" — but when the organisation itself sought funding, it was treated as a domestic actor. "When we would talk to a Dutch funder, they would go like, ah, that's great. If you would be an international organization, we could fund you. Or if you would be a Dutch organization, we could fund you on our national stream, but you're a Belgian organization." This legal-national container created a persistent gap between the scope of the work and the organisational form.

On the demand side, Guy Tanke-Kathniul, a European Commission official preparing the Conference on the Future of Europe, faced the reverse of Kissinger's famous quip: "If I want to call Europe, which phone do I call? The reverse was true for the commission. If I want to get information about the deliberative democracy, who do I call? You had loads of independent actors." The Council of Europe was similarly moving into the space without a clear partner.

A third factor compounded the urgency: the risk of unqualified advice filling the vacuum. Because the field lacked connection, institutions "would fall upon the first person claiming to be the expert in deliberative democracy" — sometimes for-profit actors, sometimes individuals with idiosyncratic but strongly held methodologies. Yves cites Marcin Gerwin in Poland (2016–2020) as a benign example of a single person shaping multiple cities' approach to deliberative democracy. The broader point is structural: "You don't get like 700 times to try a new model in a space where risk-averse politicians are trying something new." Each failed or suboptimal experiment consumes political capital that the field cannot afford to waste.

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: G1000 (Belgian think tank), Guy Tanke-Kathniul (European Commission), Council of Europe, Marcin Gerwin (Poland).
- Contexts: Belgium, European Commission (Conference on the Future of Europe), Poland.
- Practices: International nonprofit registration requiring Belgian royal decree; ad hoc advice via Zoom calls with Commission officials.
- Problems: National legal status constraining international work; absence of a European-level coordinator; risk of for-profit actors or methodological "gurus" shaping national policy without peer oversight.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**  
*"If I want to call Europe, which phone do I call? The reverse was true for the commission. If I want to get information about the deliberative democracy, who do I call?"*

## Theme 2: The Name "FIDE" — Practical Constraints Disguised as Branding Strategy

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
The name "FIDE" was originally an acronym for "Foundation for Innovation in Democracy in Europe." Belgian law prohibited "foundation" for the organisation's legal form, forcing a last-minute adjustment. "Federation" was chosen simply to preserve the acronym. The Latin meaning of "fide" (trust) was a happy accident discovered after the fact, not a deliberate branding choice. Yves treats this story with disarming honesty, using it to contrast the romantic narratives institutions construct about their origins with the mundane reality of legal constraints and bureaucratic compromise.

**Detailed explanation:**  
When asked about the choice of "federation" — a term heavy with connotations of subsidiarity, unity-in-diversity, and convergence — Yves refuses the opportunity for retrospective myth-making. The original name was modelled on the New Democracy Foundation, but the Belgian Department of Justice rejected "foundation" because it is a separate legal form in Belgium and would confuse the public. The organisation had already been using the acronym "FIDE" in its tracing and early communications. To preserve it, they changed the last word to "federation." His tone is wry: "I could now give it a beautiful backstory… but the piece is the first song, so I'm just being honest."

The Latin meaning of "fide" (trust) was discovered only after the name was fixed. Had the alternative name "Hub for Innovation in Democracy" been chosen, the acronym would have been "HIDE" — "which doesn't really sound well in English." The episode illustrates a broader observation about organisational branding: philosophical meaning is often retrojected onto decisions that were originally pragmatic. Yves redirects the conversation firmly: "I think it's more interesting to talk about how we had discussions about the governance rather than the words."

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Belgian Department of Justice, New Democracy Foundation (reference model).
- Practices: International nonprofit registration in Belgium (royal decree required); acronym preservation strategy.
- Problems: Legal constraints on naming; tension between branding aspirations and legal reality; retrospective myth-making in institutional narratives.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**  
*"The name federation was chosen because the name foundation was proven not legal in Belgium because we weren't a foundation."*  
*"Fidey, as all people realize, is a beautiful play on words because in Latin, the words Fidey means trust."*  
*"I think it's more interesting to talk about how we had discussions about the governance rather than the words."*

## Theme 3: Non-Membership as an Ethical Safeguard — The Structural Conflict of Interest in Advisory Organisations

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
FIDE deliberately rejected the membership model despite calling itself a "federation." The reasoning is ethical and structural: if FIDE advises governments on deliberative processes while counting as members organisations that bid on the same government contracts, a conflict of interest arises — analogous to a car industry association setting emissions standards while Volkswagen sits on its board. FIDE operates instead as a standard NGO with an executive board (quarterly meetings, approving budget and strategy) and a largely symbolic advisory board of respected figures.

**Detailed explanation:**  
This theme is the most analytically developed in the interview. Yves sketches the deliberative democracy field's heterogeneity — ranging from "the most anarcho-syndicalist people trying new models of self-governance" to "Deloitte [offering] cities consulting on how to involve your citizens" — and observes that many competent practitioners work as for-profits because "people need to eat somehow and send their kids to university." A membership organisation in this field would have to define criteria for inclusion and exclusion, with immediate ethical consequences.

The core problem emerges when a membership organisation also gives advice: "If I consult with governments and my members would include these… then some of my members then are the ones that get the project and make money from it." He makes the comparison explicit and damning: "Replace us with the car industry association and then Volkswagen… the car association giving advice on car standards and then one of the members of the car industry association being Volkswagen, getting money from the commission. You'd be a bit like, eh, this is a bit weird."

Restricting membership to nonprofits would not solve the problem — it would exclude many competent actors while still not preventing conflicts when nonprofit members tender for the same projects FIDE advises on. During the Conference on the Future of Europe period, when several million euros were at stake, this was not a theoretical concern.

FIDE's solution is structural: it is not a membership organisation at all. Its governance consists of an executive board that meets quarterly, approving the budget and strategic choices — "so I'm not just an individual doing whatever I want" — and an advisory board, which Yves describes as "a collection of respectable figures in this space" whose role is more symbolic than operational. The advisory board functions as a "high-profile volunteer" network: "call on me if you need me, but I'm really fine if you don't." He illustrates the ethos with Jane Souther: she "will not call me at the 31st of December going like, you didn't call on me this year. I was waiting by the phone. But if I ask her to come and help for a school in Barcelona, she will come."

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Deloitte (hypothetical), for-profit consultants, ECPR, we-do-democracy, Mission Publiques, IFORC, Jane Souther.
- Contexts: Conference on the Future of Europe (EU), Barcelona (school example).
- Practices: Regular NGO structure; executive board (quarterly); advisory board (by invitation, symbolic); ad hoc expert convening.
- Problems: Conflict of interest in membership organisations that also advise; impossibility of neutral membership criteria in a heterogeneous field; risk of ethical drift when organisational form and function misalign.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**  
*"If I'm giving advice to the mayor of the city on how to design a participatory project, and then some of my members then are the ones that get the project and make money from it… that sounds a bit fishy."*  
*"A good ship means at some point you could change a bit and the course of the ship wouldn't change dramatically. That would be actually a good definition for me of good governance."*

## Theme 4: Quality Before Quantity — The Anti-Oracle Philosophy

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
Yves states FIDE's core philosophy succinctly: "increase the quality and quantity, but always in that order." Rather than positioning any single person as the authoritative voice on deliberative democracy, FIDE's model is to convene leading practitioners collectively. When advice is needed, a group of experts responds rather than a single oracle. This guards against the risk of an individual — however well-intentioned — shaping a country's deliberative practice through idiosyncratic methodology.

**Detailed explanation:**  
The philosophy was embedded in FIDE's founding pitch. The opening slides proposed "bring together the leading actors in Europe. So if somebody wanted advice, we would bring Marcin Gerwin from Poland, the guy from Ireland, and et cetera, and have them work together rather than have one person be a sort of oracle in this space." This collective model is contrasted with approaches that prioritise volume: "Some people were like, let's do as many as possible and everywhere. And at some point in what way, whatever, as long as we can get them done."

Yves applies the same anti-oracle logic to his own role. "Fide would also have worked if it wasn't me, but somebody else… Camille Doublé could have done my job and it would probably have been the same." He defines good governance as interchangeability: "The litmus test for Fide would have been that you could probably exchange at least one or two people in our work. And it shouldn't suddenly have made a dramatic difference." This is not false modesty — it is a design principle for institutional resilience. An organisation that depends on a single person is not an institution; it is a persona with a bank account.

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Marcin Gerwin (Poland), Camille Doublé (hypothetical successor), unnamed Irish practitioner.
- Practices: Collective expert convening; non-oracular model; interchangeability as governance criterion.
- Problems: Risk of single-actor dominance in national deliberative practice; tension between quality-first and quantity-first approaches; institutional fragility when key individuals are not substitutable.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**  
*"We wanted to increase the quality and quantity, but always in that order."*

## Theme 5: The Three Criteria — How a €350,000 Budget Forces Strategic Discipline

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
With a budget of €350,000 and minimal staff — "six bullets in my gun" — Yves applies three rigorous criteria before accepting any project: (1) it must be connected to real-world policy impact, not pure experimentation; (2) it must have "lighthouse" potential — its success or failure would significantly affect how deliberative democracy is perceived in that region; (3) FIDE's involvement must be genuinely necessary, not duplicating existing expertise. These criteria produce a highly selective portfolio and function as a defence against mission drift.

**Detailed explanation:**  
Yves articulates the three criteria through concrete cases, revealing a structured decision-making process that is unusual in its explicitness.

*Criterion 1 — Policy connection.* "90% of the time, they have to be connected to some real world impact with a government." He declined the Democratic Odyssey precisely on this ground: "It wasn't really connected… it was an experiment, a trial." Experimentation, he argues, is the proper domain of the 700 academics in the ECPR Democratic Innovation Group. FIDE's scarce resources should go where policy outcomes are at stake.

*Criterion 2 — Lighthouse potential.* The project must be a potential "beacon." A run-of-the-mill German city doing a mobility assembly does not qualify — "Germany has dozens of city assemblies." But the first assembly in Moldova, or the first at city level in Bosnia in an inter-ethnic context (Mustar), or sustained work in Ukraine — these are cases where "if this succeeds, this will become a sort of beacon where other people can refer to."

*Criterion 3 — Genuine need.* Even when the first two criteria are met, if the project's line-up is already strong, FIDE steps back. "If I looked at the line up, I would go like, great, you guys do that, success, good luck, but I'm going to do something else because you're already good."

A concrete example illustrates all three criteria in action: three small Ukrainian cities reached out for advice. The Council of Europe contributed less than €10,000 for guidance spanning nine weekends across three cities. Yves notes: "If I was a for profit, I would be losing money on this. So basically we invest in those projects." His colleague Kyle Redman has been the main practitioner on these engagements. The imbalance between investment and compensation is not a bug — it is the point: FIDE treats certain projects as strategic investments in the field's development.

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Kyle Redman (FIDE), Kami, Calypso, Nicolom (Democratic Odyssey participants), ECPR Democratic Innovation Group.
- Contexts: Ukraine (multiple small cities), Bosnia (Mustar, inter-ethnic context), Moldova, Democratic Odyssey (declined), Paris (ad hoc advice), Belgium.
- Practices: Three-criteria project triage; investment model (below-cost delivery for high-impact cases); board consultation on strategic choices.
- Problems: Resource scarcity; risk of funder-driven mission drift; distinguishing experimentation from policy-connected work; underinvestment in high-impact but low-resource contexts.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**  
*"Would I have done this if this project didn't exist?"*  
*"The Lloyd wouldn't come out of the elevator for that money."*  
*"If this succeeds, this will become a sort of beacon where other people can refer to."*

## Theme 6: The Surplus Value Test — A Critique of Network Saturation and the Same-Faces Problem

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
Yves offers a sustained and unusually candid critique of the networking industry in deliberative democracy. He observes that "there's more money for networking and workshops than for citizen assemblies" and that the same people appear in every network, creating redundancy without diversity. FIDE's response is strategic abstinence: it is the only Nets4Dem partner not returning for the next project. His "litmus test" — "would I do this if the project didn't exist?" — functions as a discipline mechanism against the gravitational pull of available funding and social expectation.

**Detailed explanation:**  
The critique develops across several dimensions. First, the fungibility problem: "You could spend your day going from workshops to workshops to nets for them to scale them to feed them… and never work on democracy because you're so busy networking." The paradox is structural — networking has become an industry with its own funding stream, often better resourced than the actual practice it is meant to support. "There's more money for networking and workshops than for citizen assemblies." He illustrates with a stark comparison: a Moldovan organisation had €20,000 to run a two-week citizen assembly; a three-day networking meeting in Prague might cost €50,000 all-in.

Second, the same-faces phenomenon. "At some point, you go to meetings and I meet you, I meet Jacob, and Hega, and we all go, hey, how are you doing? How are the kids?… there is not enough diversity and we're just duplicating stuff." The implication is that the network field has become an echo chamber of the already-convinced, consuming resources that could fund practice in underexposed regions.

Third, the fiduciary responsibility argument. "Funding being so limited, especially now that OSF is gone and USAID is gone… I think we should have a responsibility because the cake is not getting bigger." Every euro spent on a networking meeting is a euro not spent on an actual deliberative process.

FIDE's response is not to participate less but to be radically selective. Yves's "litmus test" — "would I have done this if this project didn't exist?" — is designed to detect the subtle corruption of mission drift. When a funder proposed a project on border areas, Yves and the board concluded that border areas were not something FIDE would have pursued organically. "We decided, no, this is like something this foundation is interested in. And we would be putting our little speed boat and going towards that bow just because there's money in there."

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Nets4Dem, OSF (Open Society Foundations), USAID, Jacob, Hega (recurring network participants).
- Contexts: Prague (networking meeting), Moldova, Lisbon, Vienna.
- Practices: Strategic network abstinence; litmus test for project selection; resource fiduciary responsibility.
- Problems: Network saturation; participant duplication; fungible networking funding vs. scarce project funding; post-OSF/USAID funding contraction; social pressure to participate.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**  
*"There's more money for networking and workshops than for actual citizen assemblies."*  
*"We should reflect a bit like how big do these things need to be? Does everybody need to be in the boat?"*  
*"The whole thing for me is a bit, what is the surplus value we are still delivering?"*

## Theme 7: The Civil Servant Network — Targeted, Invitation-Only, and Designed for Redundancy

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
FIDE created a small network (40–50 people) for civil servants working on deliberative democracy at regional or national level. It is by invitation only, funded with Bertelsmann, and deliberately kept small to avoid governance bloat. The logic is strategic: civil servants — often isolated single points of expertise in their institutions — have "a crucial impact on how tenders are written" and on the advice politicians receive. The network's ultimate goal is its own obsolescence: peers connect directly, and FIDE becomes unnecessary.

**Detailed explanation:**  
The network was conceived with Dominic Hileman in the aftermath of the Conference on the Future of Europe. Yves and Hileman identified a gap: while the Conference had created temporary meeting spaces in Brussels, civil servants outside the Brussels bubble — those working at regional or national level on deliberative democracy — had few opportunities for peer exchange. Yet these individuals are strategically critical: they influence tender specifications, advise ministers, and often operate in isolation.

Yves illustrates the isolation with the Bundestag example: "It's one lead person and two or three people max for a country of 80 million." A civil servant in the Polish parliament faces a similar situation — one person, no participation service, carrying institutional memory alone.

The network avoids open membership because "networks that suddenly have 300, 400 people become a governance in themselves." Instead, invitations are extended only to those in contexts where political will exists. "For the moment we don't have the Bulgarian parliament in that network. There's no real thing happening. There's no incentive. There's no political will. So why take up civil servants who at the moment have no… it's like training somebody to become a car mechanic and in the next 10 years you can't see a car coming by their hands."

The network's design incorporates planned redundancy. The goal is that members develop direct peer relationships — "this person can then contact their peers and we could even be pulled out of the equation Fede at that moment." Current membership includes civil servants from Ireland, Belgium, the Bundestag, and the Polish parliament (following the government change that created new political space). The network is funded by Bertelsmann. Its working name — "European Network on Citizen Participation and Deliberative Democracy" — Yves acknowledges is imperfect.

- Actors: Dominic Hileman (co-conceiver), Bertelsmann (funder), civil servants from Ireland, Belgium, Germany (Bundestag), Poland, potentially Hungary.
- Contexts: Conference on the Future of Europe (aftermath), European Policy Center (pre-existing meetings).

## Theme 8: The Social Cost of Refusal — Why Saying No Is Half the Job

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
Yves reveals that "half of my job is saying no to people and justifying myself." The deliberative democracy field operates through relationships, invitations, and collegiality — creating a social economy in which refusing participation requires repeated justification. He frames this as a form of "self-sensorship to be agreeable," drawing on Foucault, and acknowledges that his seniority and reputation make it possible for him to absorb the social cost where smaller or newer organisations might not.

**Detailed explanation:**  
Beyond the formal project-selection criteria lies a less visible but equally consequential dimension of strategic work: the interpersonal management of refusal. "The easiest thing is to go like, oh yeah, that's super interesting. But as soon as you go like, no, Fide will stay out of this. You will have a follow up like, oh, why?" The pressure is not abstract. When declining a major European project, Yves found himself having to justify the decision repeatedly. At a coffee meeting, someone told him he "looked edgy" — "it was like I had to justify for the fifth time. Why are you not a part? Why are you saying no? It's almost like saying to a friend, why are you not coming to my party?"

He connects this to a Foucauldian observation about self-governance in the field: "There's a form of self-sensorship to be nice towards each other." The collective desire to be agreeable creates a subtle but powerful gravitational pull toward participation. Organisations dilute their strategic focus not because they mistakenly believe in a project's value, but because they cannot face the social cost of refusing.

Yves is candid that his ability to resist this pressure is partly a function of position and seniority: "Because I am who I am and I have a bit of a name, I can stand that." The corollary is uncomfortable: organisations without established reputations — newer, smaller, or more precariously funded — face greater pressure to accept every invitation, further straining their limited resources. He acknowledges the personal cost: "You could also be very critical of me… I sometimes hear that I'm difficult."

## Theme 9: The Interchangeability Principle — Institutional Resilience Through Substitutability

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
Across multiple themes, Yves returns to a distinctive design principle: a healthy institution should be able to survive the replacement of any individual, including its founder. He applies this to his own role ("Fide would also have worked if it wasn't me"), to the advisory board ("you should be able to change a bit and the course of the ship wouldn't change dramatically"), and to network participation ("Nets4Dem works fine without me"). This principle counterbalances the person-centric nature of the deliberative democracy field, where networks often revolve around charismatic individuals.

**Detailed explanation:**  
The interchangeability principle is the thread that connects several otherwise distinct themes. It is the governance counterpart to the "anti-oracle" philosophy articulated in Theme 4: just as FIDE rejects the model of a single expert oracle, it also rejects the model of a single indispensable leader. "Fide would also have worked if it wasn't me, but somebody else" is not humility; it is an institutional design criterion.

The advisory board embodies the same logic: its members should be such that any one could be replaced without changing the organisation's fundamental direction. "A good ship means at some point you could change a bit and the course of the ship wouldn't change dramatically."

Applied to network participation, the principle produces the surprising conclusion that FIDE's absence from networks like Nets4Dem is not a loss: "Nets4Dem works fine without me." The assumption that every network needs every actor is, Yves suggests, a form of collective narcissism that the field can ill afford.

The principle is also implicitly self-protective. By designing for his own substitutability, Yves insulates FIDE against the fragility that afflicts organisations built around a single founder — the risk that when the founder leaves, the organisation collapses or loses its identity.
