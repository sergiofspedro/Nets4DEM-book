# IFP11 – Lorena Zarate (HIC / Global Platform for the Right to the City) – Prompt 1 Report

## Theme 1: Complexity as Both Richness and Challenge

### Summary
Zarate opens her interview by identifying a central paradox: the complexity of networks like HIC (Habitat International Coalition) and the Global Platform for the Right to the City—diversity of actors, scales, topics, and contexts—is simultaneously their greatest strength and their most persistent challenge. The ability to hold this complexity together is what makes these networks unique, but it also demands constant negotiation.

### Detailed Explanation
Zarate frames complexity as multi-dimensional. First, diversity of actors: "civil society organisations, so NGOs, so professional-based institutions, then grassroots organisations or community-based organisations that can be thousands of people, and broader social movements, and also academics and researchers." The Platform adds another layer: "not just civil society organisations, but local and regional governments, representatives and in particular networks of local and regional governments" like UCLG.

Second, complexity of topic: "housing, habitats, city, and the different dimensions of that and entry points." The urban question intersects with climate, care, migration, land rights, gender, and racial justice.

Third, complexity of scale: "trying to address the local, the national, the regional and international, more or less at the same time." Working across these scales requires "aligning priorities, visions, narratives, strategies."

The paradox is that this complexity makes the network "unique and strong in a way—that ability." But it also means that every decision, every joint initiative, requires navigating vastly different organisational cultures, political contexts, and material realities.

### Specific Elements
- **Actor diversity**: NGOs, grassroots, social movements, academics, local governments
- **Topic breadth**: Housing, habitat, city, land, care, climate
- **Scale range**: Local → national → regional → international
- **Strength**: Unique capacity for multi-actor, multi-scale coordination
- **Challenge**: Constant alignment work, patience, trust-building
- **Key skill**: Learned by doing, not formally trained

### Key Quotations
- "Complexity is at the same time the richness of these kind of institutions, but it's also a big challenge."
- "That ability of combining the different scales and actions at the different scales."
- "That requires a lot of patience, a lot of abilities and those abilities, you really learn those by doing them."

## Theme 2: The Sandwich Strategy – Bypassing National Governments

### Summary
Zarate articulates one of the most explicit strategic frameworks in the interview corpus: her networks work primarily at the local and multilateral levels, deliberately bypassing the national level. National governments are described as the most difficult to engage—bureaucratic, slow, vulnerable to electoral disruption, and removed from everyday realities. Local and translocal action, combined with multilateral advocacy, creates a "sandwich" that squeezes the national level from both sides.

### Detailed Explanation
The interviewer proposes the "sandwich" metaphor: "the two extremes of the pyramids, then in the middle with national governments and regions." Zarate agrees emphatically: "Yes. Yes. That's kind of an explicit approach. And I've been talking about that for a while now with colleagues."

She elaborates that national-level engagement is plagued by "bureaucratic barriers" that are "very, very heavy. The inertia at the national level is very difficult to overcome." Even when networks have members in different countries collaborating at national level, "it's difficult to maintain that level of advocacy and open dialogue."

Electoral changes compound the problem: "you need to start from zero again." National government participation spaces tend to be "more formal, but at the same time, not necessarily very effective and not very dynamic."

The local level, by contrast, offers "more flexible engagement, more open, more systematic." The multilateral level (UN agencies, special rapporteurs, global agendas) provides a space for "innovation," "testing new ideas," and "creating new narratives." By working both ends, the network creates pressure that the national level cannot ignore.

### Specific Elements
- **Explicit strategy**: Work local and multilateral; bypass national
- **National barriers**: Bureaucracy, inertia, electoral disruption
- **Local advantage**: Flexibility, openness, systematic engagement
- **Multilateral opportunity**: Innovation, narrative creation, standard-setting
- **Metaphor**: Sandwich—squeezing the national from both sides
- **Evidence**: Care policy emerged from local experimentation, not national agendas

### Key Quotations
- "The sandwich—because the center of the sandwich is the most difficult to reach in reality."
- "The bureaucratic barriers at the national level, they're very, very heavy."
- "Electoral changes and then you need to start from zero again."
- "More flexible engagement at the local level can be more productive for us."

## Theme 3: The Global Platform as an Anti-Competitive Structure

### Summary
The Global Platform for the Right to the City was created explicitly to overcome fragmentation and competition among housing and habitat networks. Zarate describes her frustration with organisations competing for "membership, funding, voice, and political advocacy." The Platform was designed as a non-competitive space—it has no legal status, no independent funding stream, no staff—relying instead on voluntary commitment from member organisations. This structure is both its weakness and its potential.

### Detailed Explanation
Zarate traces the origin: "I was really upset with that. And I realized that many years ago… we need to create something that is bigger than HIC and is multi-network, but it's not another organization. It's not something that is going to compete with HIC or any of those networks."

The solution was radical: no legal registration. "The Global Platform doesn't have a legal status. The Global Platform is an articulation of existing organizations and networks. And then the funding goes to those organizations and networks, and then they commit to do something as part of the platform."

This structure required a different kind of commitment. Zarate and Nelson (co-coordinator) work without guaranteed salary: "we're doing this because of commitment and conviction. So sometimes we have funding, sometimes we don't have funding and we just do it."

The Platform fills "a gap" that would otherwise remain empty. Member organisations recognise that "if it weren't for the platform, there would be no other network of networks or a space of articulation of networks doing that in the way we do it." The voluntary, non-legal structure is a deliberate choice to maintain the anti-competitive, collaborative spirit.

### Specific Elements
- **Motivation**: Frustration with competition among similar networks
- **Design principle**: "Bigger than HIC, multi-network, but not another organization"
- **Legal status**: None—intentional choice
- **Funding model**: Goes through member organisations, not the Platform
- **Staff commitment**: Based on conviction, not salary
- **Gap-filling role**: Unique articulation space in the housing/habitat ecosystem

### Key Quotations
- "We're kind of competing for attention, membership, funding, voice, and political advocacy. That makes no sense."
- "The Global Platform is an articulation of existing organizations and networks."
- "We're doing this because of commitment and conviction."
- "If it weren't for the platform, there would be no other space of articulation of networks doing that."

## Theme 4: Power Relations, Race, and Governance Within Networks

### Summary
The pandemic-era Black Lives Matter protests provoked intense internal debates within HIC and similar networks about racial dynamics, decision-making power, and the politics of language. Zarate argues that these issues go deeper than "governance" as a technical category—they touch on fundamental power relations within international coalitions, including how English as a working language homogenises political culture and excludes diverse knowledge systems.

### Detailed Explanation
Zarate describes three interlocking dimensions of internal power relations. First, racial dynamics: the George Floyd killing "brought to HIC and some other networks a wave of internal discussions around the racial issues and tensions around racial issues and decision making and power imbalances." This was not only about geographic representation ("how much representation gets Latin America vis-à-vis Africa") but about the multiple layers of racial hierarchy within regions.

Second, language: "of course, usually English is the language people choose to work in. But that's also a fact of discrimination of a lot of people and a lot of knowledges." Choosing English is not neutral—"it puts into a homogenisation process, homogenising the political culture. When you choose English, you're kind of also changing your mindset in political terms." Diversifying languages "has financial implications" that smaller organisations cannot absorb.

Third, the paid/unpaid divide: organisations have "paid staff… employees who have a salary and obligations to fulfil grants" and "political bodies" of elected representatives who receive no salary. Navigating this tension "is also difficult to navigate in more formal organisations."

### Specific Elements
- **Trigger**: George Floyd killing and global BLM protests
- **Racial dimension**: Geographic representation and intra-regional hierarchy
- **Language politics**: English as homogenising force
- **Knowledge exclusion**: Languages carry political cultures
- **Paid/unpaid divide**: Staff vs. elected representatives
- **Regional cultural battles**: LGBT rights vs. traditional gender frameworks

### Key Quotations
- "When you choose English, you're kind of also changing your mindset, in political terms."
- "I think we lose a lot of things and a lot of knowledge and political knowledge when choosing to work in English."
- "Those tensions are also difficult to navigate in more like formal organisations."

## Theme 5: The Triple Sustainability Crisis – Intergenerational, Financial, Leadership

### Summary
Zarate identifies three interconnected dimensions of sustainability crisis facing long-standing networks like HIC: intergenerational dialogue (and failure of leadership renewal), financial resourcing (short-term project funding undermining long-term commitment), and updating of working methods. These three crises reinforce each other: precarious funding discourages young people, who are needed for leadership renewal, who would bring new working methods.

### Detailed Explanation
Intergenerational dialogue is "a weakness" in most organisations Zarate knows: "I'm not sure we are doing enough in terms of promoting and taking intergenerational dialogue seriously, like explicitly as part of the strategy." This is linked to "leadership renewal"—the people who built these networks 30-50 years ago are still leading them.

Financial sustainability is contradictory: "if you look at the websites and social media of different philanthropies and foundations, you can find a change towards more systemic support… But then when you look at what's going on on the ground, you don't find those things." Funding remains "very short-term projects for very specific outcomes" rather than "long-standing commitments like we used to have 20 years ago."

The interviewer connects these: precarisation prevents generational renewal because "young generation will choose other things to do than militating in this type of movement." Zarate agrees: the lack of stable funding means networks cannot offer young people decent livelihoods, so the next generation goes elsewhere.

The third dimension is methods: intergenerational renewal requires "updating the ways of working, the tools we use for working and engaging in different ways, understanding the political organising in different ways." Older leadership styles—bureaucratic, meeting-heavy, English-dominant—do not attract younger activists who organise differently.

### Specific Elements
- **Intergenerational**: Not taken seriously as explicit strategy
- **Leadership renewal**: Failure to transition from founding generation
- **Funding gap**: Narrative of systemic support ≠ reality of short-term projects
- **Precarious livelihoods**: Drive young people away
- **Method update**: Need to adapt working and organising styles
- **Vicious circle**: Each crisis reinforces the others

### Key Quotations
- "Many of these organisations have more than three decades now. I'm not sure we are doing enough in terms of intergenerational dialogue."
- "If you look at the websites, you can find a change towards more systemic support. But then when you look at what's going on on the ground, you don't find those things."
- "Young generation will choose other things to do than militating in this type of movement."

## Theme 6: Far-Right Appropriation of Progressive Tools and Narratives

### Summary
Zarate offers a chilling analysis: the far right is systematically appropriating the language, strategies, and tools that progressive movements developed—human rights discourse, occupation tactics, decentralised organising—and weaponising them against progressive agendas. This forces progressive networks into a defensive posture while simultaneously having to defend the very tools they created against their own critique.

### Detailed Explanation
The appropriation operates at multiple levels. Narratively, "when we discuss human rights now, it's like in Argentina—they want to frame that also as the human rights of the military." The language of rights is being inverted.

Strategically, the far right uses the same tactics: "In Ottawa, they were occupying streets with trucks, several streets, downtown Ottawa. They were doing the same things you could see during the Occupy movement 10, 15 years ago—occupying the streets, having parties, having kids."

The critique of globalisation is another example: "You go back to Seattle and we were all against globalisation, the neoliberal globalisation, and now you get Trump talking against globalisation. We have to be careful in that kind of appropriation by the far right."

The result is a double bind. Progressive networks must "counter and resist explicit attacks on knowledge and academic and scientific knowledge" while simultaneously "questioning that knowledge" and insisting on Indigenous and other non-Western knowledge systems. The far right exploits this ambiguity: "you criticise science because it's not enough. Now we can do the same, but now we destroy everything."

### Specific Elements
- **Narrative appropriation**: Human rights language used against progressive causes
- **Tactic appropriation**: Occupation and decentralised organising copied by the far right
- **Critique appropriation**: Anti-globalisation discourse turned against progressive internationalism
- **Knowledge dilemma**: Defend science while questioning whose science counts
- **Double bind**: Progressives must fight on two fronts simultaneously
- **Escalation**: Beyond denial to "pedagogy of cruelty"

### Key Quotations
- "The far-right movements have been appropriating both the narrative and the strategies and the tools that were used by more transformative, progressive movements."
- "It's more than just denying. It's really what we can call the pedagogy of cruelty."
- "You criticise science because it's not enough. Now the far right can do the same, but now they destroy everything."

## Theme 7: Counter-Data and Epistemic Resistance

### Summary
In an era of fake news, data suppression, and attacks on knowledge, Zarate's networks have long practiced counter-data production—generating their own evidence about evictions, housing precarity, and informal settlements that official statistics ignore. This practice has become more strategically important as the media and public seek reliable information in information vacuums created by government inaction.

### Detailed Explanation
The interviewer describes the current moment as one where "environmental deniers are also social deniers"—denying problems to avoid having to solve them. Data on evictions and housing precarity is systematically underproduced by governments: "The special rapporteur on the right to housing has been asking for these for the past 20 years. Governments don't do that."

Zarate's networks fill this vacuum. HIC has a long practice of "producing your own data" because informal neighbourhood residents "are not really on the official maps, they don't count on the official statistics." Counter-mapping and eviction monitoring are core activities.

The media response is revealing: "when you produce your own data, even a very specific scenario with a reduced number of cases and not necessarily a strong methodology, the media actually pays attention to that—if you're trying to fill a vacuum on information." HIC's pandemic-era eviction monitoring in Mexico became "a huge thing."

But Zarate injects a caution about epistemic politics: networks must navigate between defending science against far-right attacks while simultaneously questioning whose knowledge counts as scientific. "We need Indigenous peoples, Indigenous knowledge, hand in hand with the more academic and formal and Western kind of knowledge, working together." This is a balancing act that requires constant attention.

### Specific Elements
- **Data vacuum**: Governments do not count evictions or informal settlements
- **Counter-practice**: HIC produces its own eviction data and counter-maps
- **Media impact**: Filling a vacuum attracts attention disproportionate to resources
- **Pandemic example**: HIC Latin America monitored eviction threats, gained media traction
- **Epistemic balancing**: Defend science while decolonising knowledge
- **IPCC example**: Indigenous knowledge increasingly recognised in climate reports

### Key Quotations
- "Producing your own data was always important because those people are not really on the official maps."
- "When you produce your own data, the media actually pays attention to that."
- "We need Indigenous knowledge hand in hand with the more academic and formal Western knowledge, working together."

## Theme 8: Taboo Topics and the Limits of Democratic Inclusion

### Summary
Zarate confronts the most difficult governance challenge facing international coalitions: how to handle topics on which member organisations from different cultures and regions hold irreconcilable positions. She uses the example of LGBT rights versus traditional gender frameworks, which erupted in conflict within HIC's General Assembly. Her analysis connects these tensions to broader cultural battles, generational differences, and the challenge of maintaining inclusion without sacrificing core values.

### Detailed Explanation
The interviewer describes witnessing a conflict within HIC between women's organisations that "didn't want to rotate in the commission with the LGBT issues" and those defending LGBT inclusion. Zarate acknowledges this is "huge" and frames it as "dynamics that are much broader and they're playing out at the national level or the regional level."

She identifies two intersecting forces. First, regional cultural battles: organisations representing regions with conservative social norms bring those positions into the international coalition. Second, generational differences: younger activists "have it already incorporated in a way that our generations don't have it." At a Montreal housing summit, "the majority of participants were young, below 35, and the situation is completely different both in terms of the narratives and how careful people is in terms of language."

The traditional feminist movement sometimes falls into "the same trap" as the far right—a "zero sum kind of game" where recognising trans rights is seen as diminishing women's rights. Zarate sees this as a fundamental failure of solidarity.

Her conclusion is honest: housing movements "need to do much more in order to explicitly address and discuss some of these agendas." Feminist and environmental movements "are doing a much better work on that than the housing movements." The challenge is systematic, not incidental.

### Specific Elements
- **Concrete example**: HIC General Assembly conflict between women's and LGBT organisations
- **Root cause**: Broader cultural battles imported into coalition
- **Generational dimension**: Younger activists have inclusion as first-layer political training
- **Zero-sum trap**: Defensive politics mimicking far-right arguments
- **Sector comparison**: Housing movements lag behind feminist and environmental ones
- **Aspiration**: Need systematic, not pilot-level, engagement

### Key Quotations
- "It's reflecting dynamics that are much broader and they're playing out at the national level."
- "They are falling into the same trap [as the far right]—if we allow these groups to have more rights, that means we're getting less."
- "We need to do much more in order to explicitly address and discuss some of these agendas."

## Theme 9: Europe Learning from the Global South

### Summary
Zarate argues that the European housing crisis creates an opportunity for South-North knowledge transfer. European actors are discovering the right-to-housing framework and community-led housing models that Global South movements have developed over decades. The Global Platform has facilitated dialogues connecting European housing debates with expertise from other regions, challenging the unidirectional model of Europe teaching the world.

### Detailed Explanation
The interviewer notes that the book's focus on European democratic erosion makes South-North learning relevant. Zarate observes that despite HIC and the Platform being weaker in Europe than in other regions, "in the past few years… a lot of different actors coming together" around housing. These include networks of community land trusts, Housing Europe, House Europe, and municipalities for housing.

Crucially, these actors "are discussing housing issues from a human rights approach or from a community-led housing approach"—aligning with narratives that HIC and the Platform have promoted for decades. But "only a few of them actually have international connections." The opportunity is for HIC and the Platform to facilitate "inter-regional dialogues and trans-local dialogues."

The Platform organised four online dialogues (November–March) focused on the European affordable housing plan but bringing "people from other regions and cases and examples from other regions." The purpose was explicit: "to broaden up the imagination and contributions—not just in the sense that Europe can teach other regions things, but also Europe can learn from other regions things."

Zarate sees the mainstreaming of previously marginalised ideas as a positive development: "20 years ago we were discussing about the need for the public sector to play a bigger role and to regulate the market. We were the crazy ones. Now that's the common sense discourse." But mainstreaming carries the risk of hijacking: "you cannot protest if they are really mainstreamed."

### Specific Elements
- **European awakening**: New actors discovering right-to-housing frameworks
- **Alignment**: European narratives increasingly match Global South perspectives
- **Missing connection**: Few European actors have international links
- **Platform's role**: Facilitating inter-regional dialogue
- **Mainstreaming**: Success and risk of concept hijacking
- **Twenty-year arc**: From marginalised "crazy ones" to common sense

### Key Quotations
- "The idea was to broaden up the imagination—not just that Europe can teach other regions things, but also Europe can learn from other regions."
- "20 years ago we were the crazy ones. Now that's the common sense discourse."
- "You do an effort to mainstream some topics. Then you cannot protest if they are really mainstreamed."

## Theme 10: Language, Naming, and the Meaning of Organisational Forms

### Summary
Zarate reflects on the terminology of collective action—coalition, alliance, platform, hub, front, network—and argues that while some terms carry intentional political meaning, many are used interchangeably. The choice of "platform" for the Global Platform was deliberate: it signalled a non-competitive, non-legal space for coordination among existing organisations rather than a new entity. "Front" carries wartime connotations. "Hub" reflects a fashion cycle. The diversity of terms reflects the diversity of intentions, but also a certain conceptual confusion.

### Detailed Explanation
Asked whether the terms have distinguishable meanings, Zarate says "both"—sometimes they are intentionally different, sometimes interchangeable. "Hubs" were fashionable: "at some point, hubs were like the whole thing, and everyone wanted to create a hub."

"Fronts" carry a specific political genealogy: "they're more like taking, being conscious about the political lineage of those words, and they're more like politically aligned with progressive left-wing proposals." Zarate acknowledges the current resurgence of "front" reflects the sense of crisis: "we need to unite again… because the right wing is now the far right wing." But she personally dislikes "the language related to war."

"Coalitions" in theory are temporary, campaign-oriented, but HIC is a coalition that has persisted for decades. "Alliances" and "partnerships" were "many times just used as the same thing."

The Platform's choice of "platform" was most deliberate: "it was intentional in the sense of not to create a new organisation, not to compete with what was already there, but to create a space for coordination among existing actors." The word signals a technological metaphor—a place where things are deposited and shared—rather than an organisational actor.

### Specific Elements
- **Hub**: Fashionable term, practice-oriented, topic-specific
- **Front**: Political lineage, wartime connotations, left-wing solidarity
- **Coalition**: Theoretically temporary, often permanent in practice
- **Platform**: Deliberate non-competitive, coordination space
- **Alliance/Partnership**: Interchangeable with coalition
- **Zarate's position**: Prefers non-military language
- **Intention vs. practice**: Many organisations use terms interchangeably despite different intentions

### Key Quotations
- "At some point, hubs were like the whole thing, and everyone wanted to create a hub."
- "I personally don't like the language related to war."
- "Choosing the term platform was intentional—not to create a new organisation, not to compete."
- "In theory, coalitions are more like a campaign thing. But it's not the case with HIC."
