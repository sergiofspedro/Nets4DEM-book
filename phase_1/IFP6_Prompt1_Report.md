## Theme 1: The Name Debate — Observatory, Network, and the Critique of "Participatory"

**Theme summary:** Fernando Pindado is the most analytically critical interviewee regarding OIDP's name. He states bluntly: *"There are two things I don't like about the OIDP: its name and its surname. I don't even like the name 'observatory,' because it isn't one."* He also critiques the adjective "participatory," arguing that "democracy can only be participatory; otherwise, it wouldn't be democracy." He would prefer terms like "democratic quality" or "democratic innovation." Despite his role as Secretary General for four years, he used the name because it was institutionalised. He suggests that a 25-year anniversary is an appropriate moment to reconsider the name, comparing it to people who "change their name after 25 years, they even change their gender. That shouldn't be a problem."

**Detailed explanation:** Fernando traces the name's origin to the EU project context — "it was a project within a European Union project. Local observatories were created to analyze the state of democracy and participation." He acknowledges that observatories are "traditionally very Latin structures" that do not carry the same meaning in English or Anglo-Saxon contexts. The name no longer corresponds to the organisation's actual function: "OIDP isn't a place for analyzing reality; it's more of a space, a network for exchanging experiences and knowledge, and it doesn't aim to create a permanent manifesto." His critique of "participatory" is rooted in democratic theory — he sees it as redundant with a proper understanding of democracy, and prefers language that foregrounds "democratic quality" and "democratic innovation."

**Specific elements:**
- Actors: Fernando Pindado, OIDP General Assembly, Barcelona City Council.
- Practices: Naming as institutional identity marker; the difficulty of renaming a 25-year-old entity.
- Contradiction: Fernando used the name he disliked for four years as Secretary General.

**Key quotations:** *"There are two things I don't like about the OIDP: its name and its surname."* *"I was the Secretary General for four years and I had to use my name and surname."*

## Theme 2: Governance Critique — The Democratic Deficit of Barcelona's Dominance

**Theme summary:** Fernando was highly critical of the governance structure he inherited as Secretary General. The position was tied to Barcelona City Council's role as primary funder: *"I also held the position of Secretary General of OIDP. It was a position that was linked to other responsibilities, and that seemed a bit strange to me because, frankly, it wasn't democratic."* The 2018 Barcelona Assembly was a watershed moment — OIDP adopted a Local Democracy Agenda and began introducing rotation for secretarial roles, with other cities contributing financially beyond Barcelona. This reform directly addressed the democratic deficit Fernando identified.

**Detailed explanation:** When Fernando joined Barcelona City Council in 2015, OIDP was, in his subjective assessment, "in a moment of crisis, of uncertainty about its direction." The network was "sustained by the financial resources provided by the Barcelona City Council, which gave the City Council a... dominant position." The Secretary General position was automatically linked to Barcelona's participation department head — a structural arrangement that concentrated power in the funder. Fernando's reform agenda involved two elements: (1) more strongly linking OIDP to UCLG, so that "OIDP would become the specialized arm... of UCLG on matters of democratic quality and citizen participation"; and (2) introducing rotation and diversification of financial contributions, so Barcelona was not the sole funder. The 2018 Assembly crystallised these changes.

**Specific elements:**
- Events: 2018 Barcelona Assembly (watershed reform), Krakow meeting (2026, upcoming).
- Documents: Local Democracy Agenda (2018).
- Actors: Fernando Pindado, Adrià Duarte (successor), Emilia Sáenz (UCLG Secretary), Barcelona City Council.
- Problems: Democratic deficit created by funder-dominance; solved partially through UCLG integration and governance rotation.

**Key quotations:** *"Frankly, it wasn't democratic. It meant that the City Council, the city that contributes the most resources, was the one that was in charge of the General Secretariat."*

## Theme 3: The Individual-Institution Paradox and the "Waste of Experience"

**Theme summary:** Fernando delivers the most analytically precise formulation of the network's central paradox: *"The structures that are networks of legal entities... often have more presence of the person acting on behalf of them than of the institution they claim to represent."* He argues that OIDP should formally recognize and incorporate individuals — not just cities — into its working structures: *"What I believe could be beneficial is that, while cities remain the central structure, the process incorporates individuals, groups, and entities — things other than cities."* He also advocates creating a space for former mayors and officials, whom he describes as victims of "the waste of experience" — knowledge that is lost when individuals leave institutional roles.

**Detailed explanation:** Fernando observes that "when a mayor leaves office, they disappear from the municipal sphere" — and thus from OIDP. He contrasts this with national-level political systems, where former presidents and prime ministers retain titles and often remain active in public life. At the local level, no such mechanism exists. "OIDP should facilitate a space where those who are no longer mayors can remain involved if they wish... a sort of group of retirees... who possess knowledge that could be utilized." He provides himself as an example: after leaving his institutional roles, he still does work for Spanish municipalities, but is no longer formally connected to OIDP. The interviewer's observation resonates: "When I changed schools, I brought the new school with me and the old one didn't stay." Fernando replies: "That's it."

**Specific elements:**
- Actors: Fernando Pindado (post-office trajectory), former mayors, ex-officials.
- Practices: Proposed alumni/retiree space for OIDP; individual incorporation into working groups.
- Problems: Knowledge loss when individuals leave institutional roles; no formal mechanism for retention.

**Key quotations:** *"The structures that are networks of legal entities... often have more presence of the person acting on behalf of them than of the institution they claim to represent."* *"It's a shame to lose that cultural heritage; that's a loss of energy we can't afford."*

## Theme 4: The UCLG Umbrella — Strategic and Reputational Asset

**Theme summary:** Fernando was the primary architect of OIDP's deeper integration with UCLG. He frames UCLG as essential to OIDP's identity and sustainability: *"What gives it consistency and a positive image is UCLG... It is a reputational asset, an unwritten asset that cannot be lost."* He strongly cautions against merging OIDP with other networks if that would compromise the UCLG umbrella. Instead, he advocates programmatic collaboration: *"I would advocate more for joint programs... More out of practical coincidence than organizational consolidation, in a marriage of convenience that could end badly. Marriages rarely end well."*

**Detailed explanation:** During his tenure, Fernando worked with Emilia Sáenz (UCLG Secretary) to forge the integration strategy. The rationale was twofold: (1) administrative — UCLG could provide the back-office functions a small network needed; (2) political — UCLG's global institutional presence and membership base (much larger than OIDP's) would give OIDP scale and visibility it could not achieve alone. Fernando acknowledges he doesn't follow UCLG's current standing closely, but maintains his conviction that the UCLG umbrella is OIDP's most valuable asset. Regarding the recent convergence of OIDP coordination with the UCLG Committee, he sees it positively: "But they have the same umbrella." He is pragmatic about mergers: programmatic collaboration preserves autonomy while enabling cooperation.

**Specific elements:**
- Actors: Fernando Pindado, Emilia Sáenz (UCLG Secretary), Barcelona City Council.
- Practices: OIDP-UCLG integration; joint programmes over institutional merger.
- Problems: Merger risks losing UCLG umbrella benefits and institutional identity.

**Key quotations:** *"What gives it consistency and a positive image is UCLG... It is a reputational asset, an unwritten asset that cannot be lost."*

## Theme 5: The Democratic Defense Mission

**Theme summary:** Fernando articulates an explicitly political mission for OIDP grounded in the defence of democracy. Drawing on V-DEM data ("a devastating report" on global democratic decline), he argues that "cities have an important role to play there, and UCLG and OIDP, as part of UCLG, have an important role to play." He invokes the medieval principle "the air of the city will set you free" to express the idea that cities remain the primary space where democracy must be defended. The 2018 Local Democracy Agenda is described as one of the elements that "served as a theoretical reference or conceptual framework" for this mission.

**Detailed explanation:** Fernando is the most politically explicit of the OIDP interviewees about the network's role in democratic defence. He follows V-DEM's annual democracy reports and expresses deep concern about "the growing percentage of autocracies and the decline of democratic systems on the planet." He references current political dangers in Spain as a concrete example. The role he envisions for OIDP is to be the specialised instrument of the municipalist movement — channelled through UCLG — for defending democratic quality at the local level.

**Specific elements:**
- Actors: V-DEM (University of Gothenburg), David Allman, UCLG.
- Documents: V-DEM annual democracy reports, Local Democracy Agenda (2018).
- Contexts: Spain (current political risks), global autocratisation trends.
- Problems: The mission is clear but under-resourced relative to the scale of the challenge.

**Key quotations:** *"In times of crisis like the ones we are experiencing now, in politics and democracy, cities have to play an important role in defending that polis, that democracy that was born in the city."*
