## Theme 1: Entry and Early ALDA – From Balkan Project to European Organisation

### Summary
Boaria joined ALDA in 2003 when it was still a small, Balkans-focused organisation with only four employees. He witnessed and contributed to the transformation from a Council of Europe programme continuation into a geographically diversified European network. His entry was accidental—his social cooperative shared office space with ALDA—and he brought experience in youth training that helped shape ALDA's programme expansion.

### Detailed Explanation
Boaria describes the ALDA he joined as "totally different from the ALDA of today." In 2003 there were perhaps five employees: "a core of four people including Antonella, a communications officer, a PM, a financial officer and two consultants." The organisation was "solely and exclusively focused on the continuation of the program that had given rise to ALDA—that of the local democracy agencies of the Balkans in the 90s."

ALDA at that time was "heavily focused on the Balkans, with a strong Italian membership," driven by "a lot of solidarity with the Balkans stemming from the conflict." His entry was serendipitous: he was president of a social cooperative managing youth training centres that crossed paths with ALDA when it needed office space. The cooperative was reducing activities, and they transferred ALDA to their space.

Boaria came from a youth training background, having worked with the European Youth in Action programme. This background became important as ALDA expanded: "my hiring was part of a program defined by ALDA to expand ALDA's scope of action from the Balkans."

### Specific Elements
- **Year joined**: 2003
- **ALDA size**: ~5 employees, Balkans-only focus
- **Entry path**: Shared office space with his social cooperative
- **Background**: Youth training, European Youth in Action programme
- **First projects**: CARDS programme (Balkans), Interreg, Adriatic
- **Early innovation**: EurAction project (2006) – one of 4 EU pilot projects testing citizen panel methodology
- **Youth projects**: Right Direction youth – 3-year project, pre-dating Europe for Citizens programme

### Key Quotations
- "I arrived at ALDA in 2003, in an ALDA that was totally different from the ALDA of today."
- "ALDA at the time was solely and exclusively focused on the continuation of the program that had given rise to ALDA."
- "My hiring at the time was part of a program that had been defined by ALDA so that ALDA would exit the Balkans… expand its scope."

## Theme 2: The ALDA Ecosystem – Beyond a Single Organisation

### Summary
ALDA has evolved into a multi-entity ecosystem rather than a simple association. Over 25 years, it created spin-offs including ALDA Plus (a for-profit benefit corporation for service delivery), ALDA Italia (a national foundation for Italian fundraising and institutional dialogue), and legally registered branches in multiple countries. Boaria describes this as an intentional strategy for sustainability and operational flexibility.

### Detailed Explanation
Boaria explains that ALDA is not just a French association but "a community of different legal entities that belong to ALDA." The mother organisation remains the French association, but it has spawned:

**ALDA Plus** (2017): "Our in-house company, which is an SRL Benefit Corporation 100% owned exclusively by ALDA." Boaria is sole director. It generates resources "through the sale of services" to support the "mother gasket." This was a deliberate response to funding challenges.

**ALDA Italia**: A PS registered entity needed because "we needed an Italian non-profit entity that was able to dialogue with the authorities with the Italian institutions which could not be an association under French law much less a profit company like ALDA Plus." It opens fundraising channels like banking foundations.

**Registered branches**: "We have officially registered branches in Skopje, Chisinau, and Kyiv." ALDA is also working on registration in Senegal (Tunisia was put on hold due to political conditions).

**19 Local Democracy Agencies**: Autonomous NGOs in their countries, with ALDA as the umbrella organisation. Each has its own governance within the broader ecosystem.

The overall structure is not a "group" (too commercial a term) but a "community"—a term that reflects the values-driven nature of the organisational architecture.

### Specific Elements
- **Mother organisation**: French association (Loi 1901)
- **ALDA Plus**: Benefit corporation (SRL), 100% ALDA-owned, service delivery
- **ALDA Italia**: Italian non-profit foundation, institutional dialogue and fundraising
- **Branches**: Skopje, Chisinau, Kyiv (registered); Senegal in progress; Tunisia on hold
- **LDAs**: 19 autonomous local NGOs under ALDA umbrella
- **Staff distribution**: ~120 total (50% Vicenza HQ, ~15 Brussels, ~10 Skopje, ~5 Chisinau, ~4 Kyiv, ~2 Tunis, 1 Senegal)
- **Ecosystem term**: "Community" preferred over "group"

### Key Quotations
- "ALDA has created spin-offs… ALDA Plus was born, which is our in-house company."
- "We preferred to call it a community so as not to call it a group, which is a much more commercial term."
- "Over the years, ALDA has created spin-offs… to always respond to the different current challenges."

## Theme 3: Membership Management – Quality Over Quantity in a Mixed Network

### Summary
ALDA manages a diverse membership of approximately 350 entities, roughly split 50% local authorities and 50% civil society organisations (associations, cooperatives, universities, research centres). The challenge is maintaining engagement across this diversity while keeping membership fees low (2% of budget) so they do not become a barrier. Growth is managed through careful selection, a probationary year for new members, and creating multiple pathways for active participation.

### Detailed Explanation
ALDA's membership is remarkably diverse: "we have about 50% local authorities, and the remaining 50% we take from the rest of the world." This includes "associations, cooperatives, universities, research centres." Boaria calls it "perhaps one of the few mixed networks" and describes the mix as "an advantage but also a challenge."

Membership fees are deliberately low—"at least 2% of ALDA's budget"—meaning "we are not a network that lives off members." The budget is overwhelmingly project-based. This has advantages (no financial barrier to joining) and disadvantages (less structural financial stability).

The selection process includes a probationary period: applicants with less experience can be asked to wait until September "so they can learn and return in September with more experience." During this time, they stay connected through newsletters, project invitations, and events. Proactivity outside membership is valued: "The following year, I saw you proactive in the network, even if you're not a member, we've done projects together."

Active members can engage through the Governing Board (12 members elected every 4 years, balanced between local authorities and civil society, geographically), the Bureau (5 members: president, 3 VPs, treasurer), or as co-chairs of thematic hubs. Passive members—who pay fees but never participate—are accepted: "their mission is to concretely support what ALDA does."

### Specific Elements
- **Total members**: ~350
- **Composition**: 50% local authorities, 50% civil society/universities/cooperatives
- **Fee revenue**: ~2% of budget (deliberately low)
- **Probation**: Less experienced applicants wait, stay connected through other channels
- **Governing Board**: 12 members, 4-year terms, geographically and sectorally balanced
- **Bureau**: 5 members (President, 3 VPs, Treasurer)
- **Thematic hubs**: Member co-chairs alongside ALDA staff coordinators
- **Passive membership**: Accepted and valued as mission support

### Key Quotations
- "ALDA is perhaps one of the few mixed networks… we have about 50% local authorities."
- "Membership fees account for at least 2% of ALDA's budget, so we are not a network that lives off members."
- "There are some members who pay the fee but we never hear about them… they do it to support the mission of the organization."

## Theme 4: Geographic Expansion – From Balkans to Global

### Summary
Boaria traces ALDA's geographic expansion through distinct phases: Balkans consolidation (1999–2004), European enlargement (2004–2009), Eastern neighbourhood (2006 onward), Mediterranean (2009–2010), and most recently sub-Saharan Africa. Each phase brought new members, new fundraising sources, and new operational challenges. The expansion was driven partly by member demand and partly by strategic opportunism.

### Detailed Explanation
ALDA's geographic evolution parallels the periods Valmorbida described, but Boaria provides more operational detail. The shift from Balkans-only to European work began around 2004–2005, driven by "projects linked to participation in good governance." The first EU pilot project on citizen panels (EurAction, 2006) was a breakthrough.

The operating grant from the European Commission marked a turning point: "It was certainly beyond the funding that ALDA was recognized at the table… in the European Commission's structured dialogue on the issues of participation in good governance." This recognition "allowed us to open networks, significantly increase our partners and members."

The Mediterranean expansion was member-driven: "we had members asking to do something also in the Mediterranean area." The Ukraine war created a new focus requiring organisational restructuring.

Expansion is also about the Council of Europe's enlarged Europe vision: "our point of reference has always been an enlarged Europe, we went to the Balkans, the European Union came later." ALDA remains "strongly in favour of enlargement in the Balkans."

### Specific Elements
- **Phase 1**: Balkans consolidation (1999–2004)
- **Phase 2**: European expansion (2004–2009) – EU projects, citizen panels
- **Phase 3**: Eastern neighbourhood (2006–) – Georgia first agency
- **Phase 4**: Mediterranean (2009–2010) – member-driven
- **Phase 5**: Africa (recent) – Senegal registration in progress
- **Operating grant**: Key reputational accelerator, beyond its financial value
- **Enlargement stance**: Strongly pro-enlargement for Western Balkans
- **Current focus**: Ukraine, Moldova, maintaining Balkan engagement

### Key Quotations
- "ALDA's mandate greatly expanded… we began to develop European projects linked to participation in good governance."
- "Beyond the funding, ALDA was recognized at the table… in the European Commission's structured dialogue."
- "We have never exited [the Balkans], rather it is a continuous investment."

## Theme 5: Competition and Cooperation in Crisis Times

### Summary
Boaria identifies the current funding crisis as a threat to networking itself. When resources shrink, organisations turn inward and competition intensifies, paradoxically at a moment when cooperation is most needed. He sees this dynamic accelerating in the last year, with partners who were previously inactive suddenly pursuing the same limited funding.

### Detailed Explanation
Boaria was asked about threats to networking as a common good. His primary concern is the funding crisis: "a situation of crisis for the support of programs related to democratic resilience and democracy… the level of competition is leading to polarization and opposing positions."

He observes a dangerous dynamic: "perhaps there is a risk of turning around and everyone looking at their own fate because it is a moment of crisis. Instead of seeing the solution in alliances and networking, perhaps we see the threat because 'he takes the money or I take it.'"

This has been "very present in the last year." The scarcity of resources "polarises a bit and exaggerates the positions a bit when instead perhaps the solution is the opposite." He notes that "the European competition has become incredible in many aspects," with organisations that were previously passive now aggressively pursuing the same funds.

The challenge for networks is governing this internal competition well: "if this is not governed well within the network, in the end the network will lose much of its credibility and its meaning."

### Specific Elements
- **Primary threat**: Funding crisis intensifying competition
- **Observation**: Partners previously inactive are now active competitors
- **Risk**: Networks fragment under pressure instead of coalescing
- **Mitigation**: Good facilitation and governance of internal competition
- **Credibility stake**: Poorly managed competition destroys network value
- **Paradox**: Cooperation needed most when it becomes hardest

### Key Quotations
- "The level of competition is leading to polarization and opposing positions."
- "Instead of seeing the solution in alliances and networking, perhaps we see the threat because 'he takes the money or I take it.'"
- "If this is not governed well within the network, in the end the network will lose much of its credibility and its meaning."

## Theme 6: Safe Spaces and Work in Authoritarian Contexts

### Summary
Boaria describes ALDA's experience working in increasingly restrictive environments—Belarus, Georgia, Tunisia—where the word "democracy" itself can be dangerous. ALDA has developed practices for protecting local partners and citizens, including creative language use, low-profile programming, and the innovative concept of "Local Democracy Agencies in exile."

### Detailed Explanation
ALDA worked in Belarus for 5–6 years on EU-funded projects focused on citizen participation. This required "a very low profile and maximum protection." Projects included Citizens Libraries—physical spaces where citizens could meet and express themselves relatively freely. When Belarus took "a totally anti-democratic turn," ALDA had to withdraw.

Boaria describes ALDA's approach: "we evaluate situations very carefully… in regimes where we will put at risk the target, which in our case are mainly citizens and associations." In some countries, "we don't use the word democracy" even while implementing participatory democracy programmes. In Georgia, recent conditions have made work "just impossible because you can't bring even a lira."

The most innovative response is the concept of an **LDA in exile**: "we are establishing the first local democracy agency in exile" for Belarus, located in neighbouring territory, working with the Belarusian diaspora. Similarly, the LDA of Mariupol continues to operate from Kyiv—"a community without territories"—governed by the still-in-office mayor who sits on ALDA's board. This adaptation allows democratic work to continue even when the physical territory is inaccessible.

### Specific Elements
- **Belarus**: Citizens Libraries as safe spaces; withdrawal after anti-democratic turn
- **Georgia**: Currently impossible to operate
- **Tunisia**: Registration on hold due to political conditions
- **Language tactics**: "We don't use the word democracy" while implementing democratic programmes
- **LDA in exile**: First for Belarus, hosted in neighbouring territory
- **Mariupol LDA**: Operates from Kyiv; mayor continues in office governing a "community without territories"
- **Assessment criteria**: Priority country list based on impact potential and risk to local actors

### Key Quotations
- "We don't use the word democracy [in some countries]."
- "In Georgia… it's just impossible to work because you can't bring even a lira."
- "A community without territories… the mayor of Mariupol sits on our board of directors, still a mayor in office."

## Theme 7: ALDA's Role in EPD – The Local Democracy Voice

### Summary
Within EPD, ALDA plays a unique role as the only member organisation that brings the local democracy dimension. Boaria describes EPD as a generalist democracy platform where each member contributes a specific angle—parliamentary, media, elections, local—and ALDA's angle is local democracy and citizen participation. He values EPD as complementary to ALDA's other network memberships.

### Detailed Explanation
Boaria positions ALDA within EPD as "the only organization that brings the dimension of local democracy." Other members bring different specialisations: "NIMD deals with parliamentary democracy, CFI and Article 19 with media, others with electoral processes." This makes ALDA unique within the partnership.

EPD is "perhaps the most generalist" of the many networks ALDA belongs to. Others include Civil Society Europe, Concord Europe, Energy Cities, Eurocities, and Digital Europe. EPD's value lies in the advocacy and policy dimension: "collaborations on the front of advocacy, statements or other actions that EPD moves to support democracy."

Project-level collaboration within EPD is "a little less" frequent, partly because of non-competition sensitivities. Antonella Valmorbida served as EPD president for two terms until a few years ago, reflecting ALDA's active role.

Boaria describes ALDA's self-understanding as "connector," doing "this work of facilitating 360-degree network connections at a geographical and thematic level every day." This connector role is recognised externally: "it is a value that is recognized to ALDA."

### Specific Elements
- **EPD role**: Local democracy voice (unique among members)
- **Other networks**: Civil Society Europe, Concord Europe, Energy Cities, Eurocities
- **EPD value**: Advocacy platform, policy influence, joint statements
- **Project collaboration**: Limited due to competition sensitivities
- **ALDA governance**: Antonella Valmorbida was EPD president (2 terms)
- **Connector identity**: "360-degree network facilitation"

### Key Quotations
- "We are the only organization that brings the dimension of local democracy."
- "EPD is the European platform on democracy issues where everyone brings their contribution."
- "We do this work of facilitating 360-degree network connections at a geographical and thematic level every day."

## Theme 8: Networking as Common Good – Threats and False Myths

### Summary
Boaria reflects on networking as a common good that must be protected. He identifies the main threat as the funding crisis that turns organisations inward. On false myths, he argues that networks are often assumed to be inherently democratic and effective at creating critical mass, but in practice their impact depends on how well they manage internal competition and governance. The "false myth" is that simply being a network guarantees results.

### Detailed Explanation
Boaria strongly believes in networking as value: "it is the founding value of our work." ALDA's network is vast—"350 members but our network is made up of thousands of contacts… in the 80 projects we currently have underway, there will be 500 partners, maybe 30% are members, the other 70% are non-members."

On threats, he returns to competition: the funding crisis creates "polarization and opposing positions." The network must be "good at facilitation, otherwise this is a bit of a double-edged sword… it even risks exploding."

On the false myth of networks: "there is something that is attributed to networks as a possibility, a potential that in reality you don't see developed." The assumption that networks are automatically more democratic or effective is not always true. The key variable is whether the network can "facilitate and organize in order to be more effective." If governance is weak, the network "loses much of its credibility and its meaning."

He also notes the sustainability challenge: "if in the end you are not able to sustain your work, you close a few months later… it is always a challenge." Networks that cannot manage competition and secure resources ultimately fail, regardless of their democratic credentials.

### Specific Elements
- **Network scale**: 350 members, thousands of contacts, 500+ project partners
- **Threat**: Funding crisis → internal competition → fragmentation
- **False myth**: Networks are automatically democratic and effective
- **Reality**: Quality depends on governance, facilitation, and conflict management
- **Sustainability**: Existential challenge for all civil society networks
- **Boaria's conviction**: Still believes in critical mass and collective voice

### Key Quotations
- "I still strongly believe that [networking] is the founding value of our work."
- "The false myth of the network is precisely that… there isn't a great strength in facilitating and organizing in order to be more effective."
- "If this is not governed well within the network, in the end the network will lose much of its credibility."
