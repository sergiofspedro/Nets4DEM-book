## Theme 1: The Tool as "Storyteller of Participation" — From Rio Grande to a National Vision

### Summary
The interview traces a remarkable trajectory: from Roque's first use of Decidim (affectionately called "Decidinho") in the small municipality of Rio Grande (RS) for the city's master plan, through the pandemic-era digital acceleration, to Marcos Vinicius's broader vision of using Decidim at IBGE to create a national platform for public managers. The tool evolves from a local experiment to a potential national infrastructure for participatory public policy.

### Detailed Explanation
Roque begins his story as Planning Secretary in Rio Grande, a municipality in Rio Grande do Sul, around 2018. Decidim had no Portuguese translation at the time — they produced the first one. The initial use was for the city's Participatory Master Plan, a process that coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, turning Decidim from an experimental tool into an essential infrastructure. Roque's key insight: "the tool is a great storyteller of participation, because it records everything" — all hearings, meetings, proposals, minutes, and decisions become a permanent, transparent record.

Marcos Vinicius then reveals the deeper federal government roots. He previously worked on **Dialoga Brasil**, a participatory platform created during Dilma Rousseff's government (2014–2016), built on the NOSFER platform by the Colivre cooperative. When Temer took over, the tool was shut down. This traumatic discontinuity — the erasure of a national participatory infrastructure by a change of government — is the backdrop for their subsequent embrace of Decidim. They found Decidim and Consul at an OIDP meeting in Buenos Aires, eventually choosing Decidim for its stronger knowledge transfer structure.

The trajectory culminates in Marcos Vinicius's current project at IBGE: using Decidim to create an environment where Brazil's 5,570 municipalities can access IBGE data, exchange policy experiences, and receive guidance from federal managers — a national-scale participatory infrastructure for public policy formulation.

### Specific Elements
- **2018**: First Portuguese translation of Decidim by the Rio Grande team
- **COVID-19**: Pandemic forced virtual participation — Decidim became essential
- **Master Plan of Rio Grande**: Comprehensive use — maps, laws, public hearings, regional meetings
- **Dialoga Brasil (2014–2016)**: Predecessor platform, shut down by Temer government
- **Discovery**: Found Decidim and Consul at OIDP Buenos Aires meeting
- **IBGE project (current)**: National platform for public managers using Decidim
- **Key metaphor**: Tool is "storyteller of participation" — permanent record of democratic processes

### Key Quotations
- "The tool is a great storyteller of participation, because it records everything, it creates new forms of relationship, possibilities for contribution." (Roque)
- "When I left Rio Grande and went to work at the Getúlio Vargas Foundation, I developed a project called Open Government and Participation." (Roque)

## Theme 2: Identity, Anonymity, and the Brazilian Context of Polarisation

### Summary
A central tension in the interview mirrors the one in the Jericó interview: the conflict between Decidim's European philosophy of open, unrestricted participation and Brazil's reality of extreme political polarisation, trolling, and aggression. The Brazilian team chose to integrate Decidim with GovBR (the federal identity system) to ensure accountability — a decision that creates friction with the Barcelona community's principles.

### Detailed Explanation
Marcos Vinicius explains the dilemma clearly. Decidim's philosophy, championed by Barcelona, is "the lack of entry restrictions" — anyone can participate without identification. But Brazil lives with "a very large separation, a very large ideological division… where aggression is very natural." The fear is that unrestricted access makes the platform vulnerable to organised trolling, hate speech, and manipulation.

Their solution: integrate Decidim with **GovBR**, Brazil's federal digital identity system (linked to the CPF taxpayer ID). This means every participant is identified — not necessarily publicly, but accountable. As Marcos Vinicius explains: "there I validate my password as a taxpayer with the Federal Revenue Service… therefore, it's a good strategy… so that when someone enters Decidim, they identify themselves as someone from GOVBR."

Giovanni raises the counter-example of police officers afraid to propose changes to security laws for fear of retaliation. Marcos Vinicius acknowledges the trade-off but argues that Brazil's experience with Dialoga Brasil showed that anonymous participation without strong moderation is fragile and difficult to scale. They chose "individual accountability" over anonymity, while attempting to maintain mediation mechanisms.

### Specific Elements
- **Decidim's European philosophy**: No entry restrictions, anyone can participate
- **Brazilian reality**: Extreme polarisation, trolling, aggression
- **Solution**: Integration with GovBR (federal digital identity)
- **Trade-off**: Security vs. protection of vulnerable voices
- **Previous experience**: Dialoga Brasil had automatic message exclusion for hate speech + human mediation team
- **Unresolved tension**: Different cultural contexts require different solutions — Barcelona does not fully understand Brazil's needs

### Key Quotations
- "We live with a very large separation, a very large ideological division… where aggression is very natural." (Marcos Vinicius)
- "Decidim has a characteristic, and this is defended by the Barcelona community, which is the lack of entry restrictions." (Marcos Vinicius)
- "The people from Barcelona tell us, 'But that's not the characteristic here in Spain, so you are living a different reality from ours.'" (Marcos Vinicius)
- "We opted to prioritize individual accountability, making them feel responsible, rather than anonymizing them." (Marcos Vinicius)

## Theme 3: The Brazilian Free Software Paradox — Appropriation Without Community

### Summary
Marcos Vinicius offers a sharp critique of how Brazil adopted Decidim: instead of building local developer communities (as the free software model demands), the federal government contracted development to two entities (a university and a private company), effectively privatising the tool's evolution. This contradicts the very logic of free software and limits Brazil's voice in the global Decidim community.

### Detailed Explanation
Marcos Vinicius argues that Brazil made a strategic mistake. When the federal government decided to use Decidim for the Participatory PPA (Multi-Year Plan), instead of "encouraging the creation of local communities," it "ended up privatizing the development of these tools at the Federal University and its consultants." The result: "we have a small number of people, as if it were a private product and not a free software product."

He draws a powerful contrast with China, which reached technological parity with the US by "entering international communities and largely utilizing European communities." Brazil, he argues, should do the same: create developer communities, distribute support work among the "over two hundred Brazilian companies" that could provide Decidim services, and follow the logic of meritocracy — "the more I develop and give back, the more I am heard."

This critique goes to the heart of the free software dilemma: a tool can be legally open while its ecosystem remains closed. Without a community of developers contributing code, Brazil remains a "mere user, mere exploiter of what already exists" — dependent, voiceless, and unable to shape the tool's evolution to its needs.

### Specific Elements
- **Brazilian adoption model**: Contracted to University of Brasília + private company from Belém
- **Result**: Small developer base, no broad community
- **Contrast with China**: Entered international communities, built local capacity
- **Free software meritocracy**: Contribution = voice in the community
- **Goal**: Build Latin American developer communities to gain influence in MetaDecidim
- **Paradox**: Brazil is a huge consumer of US proprietary tech (AWS, Google, Microsoft) but fails to leverage free software properly

### Key Quotations
- "Brazil took a free software tool and, instead of encouraging the creation of local communities, ended up privatizing the development." (Marcos Vinicius)
- "Today, when we tell Barcelona that we have this problem of people who only come to attack, the people from Barcelona tell us, 'But that's not the characteristic here in Spain.'" (Marcos Vinicius)
- "The world of free software is a world of meritocracy: the more I develop and give back, the more I am heard." (Marcos Vinicius)
- "We have over two hundred Brazilian companies here that can provide this support. Hire these companies… you will pay for the work and effort of the people." (Marcos Vinicius)

## Theme 4: Building a Latin American Decidim Network — Shared Problems, Shared Voice

### Summary
Roque and Marcos Vinicius are actively working to build a stronger Latin American Decidim network, connecting Brazil with Mexico, Colombia, and other countries. The motivation is double: shared cultural-political characteristics (polarisation, inequality, security concerns) create common ground that Europe does not share, and a stronger regional bloc gives Latin America more influence in the global Decidim community.

### Detailed Explanation
Roque describes ongoing efforts to create "a more Latin American network" of Decidim users, involving cities in Mexico, Colombia, and Brazil. A project is being developed with Spanish agency funding to create new Decidim experiences in South America (including Brazil and Ukraine). The city of Rio de Janeiro is being brought in through its Environment and Climate Secretariat.

The deeper logic is explained by Marcos Vinicius: Latin Americans face different problems from Europeans — "social conflict, the need for secrecy" — and these commonalities make regional cooperation natural. A coordinated Latin American bloc would have "a voice in the international community" rather than being isolated individual voices. The tool (Decidim) provides the common language, but the network's purpose goes beyond the tool: it is about creating a political space where shared realities can be articulated and addressed together.

Giovanni challenges them: is using the same tool enough to build a network? Roque responds that Brazil's massive participatory PPA (1.5 million participants) gives it numerical weight, and this creates an opportunity to leverage the tool as an "excuse to dialogue with other administrations."

### Specific Elements
- **Spanish agency project**: Funding new Decidim experiences in South America + Ukraine
- **Cities involved**: Rio de Janeiro, Contagem, Mexico, Colombia
- **Brazil's participatory PPA**: 1.5 million participants — numerical weight
- **Shared problems**: Polarisation, security, inequality, digital sovereignty concerns
- **Regional strategy**: Combine forces for voice in MetaDecidim
- **Tool as entry point**: Common software enables political dialogue

### Key Quotations
- "We are participating in this project, including the city of Rio de Janeiro… to try and create a more Latin American network." (Roque)
- "If we had more Latin American communities with our own problems… we would have a voice in the international community." (Marcos Vinicius)
- "We have to encourage communities that have cultural characteristics similar to ours, from Latin America." (Marcos Vinicius)

## Theme 5: The Multi-Tenant Question — Scale vs. Autonomy

### Summary
Giovanni raises the multi-tenant model (already implemented in Catalonia) as a potential solution for Brazil's challenge of scale: offering Decidim as a shared service to hundreds of municipalities. Roque and Marcos Vinicius had considered this through FGV's "Meu Município" project, but it never progressed as expected. The discussion reveals the infrastructure gap between Brazil's need for scale and its current capacity.

### Detailed Explanation
Giovanni describes how the Catalan regional government uses a multi-tenant Decidim to offer participatory infrastructure to small municipalities — a central platform with autonomous sub-sites. He asks whether FGV had considered this model for Brazil. Marcos Vinicius confirms they had thought about it through a project called "Meu Município" (My Municipality), which aimed to provide both data and a Decidim-based participation tool to municipalities. But "we didn't have the kind of progress we expected."

Roque adds that the tool's potential remains underutilised in Contagem (where they implemented it) because municipalities lack "the systemic view of how the tool communicates" and remain attached to "the logic of face-to-face systems." The multi-tenant model could solve this by making the tool available as a ready-to-use service, but the deeper challenge is cultural: municipalities need to understand participation as a continuous, multi-channel process rather than isolated face-to-face meetings.

The discussion implicitly raises the question of who should provide this infrastructure — the federal government, universities, research foundations like FGV, or a community-driven effort.

### Specific Elements
- **Multi-tenant model**: Central platform, autonomous sub-sites (as in Catalonia)
- **FGV's "Meu Município"**: Intended to offer Decidim to municipalities — did not scale
- **Contagem implementation**: Tool underutilised due to attachment to face-to-face methods
- **Infrastructure gap**: Brazil lacks a central provider like Catalonia's AOC
- **IBGE project**: Marcos Vinicius's current initiative addresses this gap at national level

### Key Quotations
- "We also considered this logic… we already had a version of Decidim that allowed the creation of multiple pages… but we didn't have the kind of progress we expected." (Marcos Vinicius)
- "There's a bit of a lack of the vision… the systemic view of how the tool communicates and how to avoid becoming too entrenched in the logic of face-to-face systems." (Roque)

## Theme 6: Tool + Data + Policy — The IBGE Vision of Data as Common Good

### Summary
Marcos Vinicius's current project at IBGE represents the most ambitious vision in the interview: using Decidim to create a platform where IBGE's statistical data becomes the basis for participatory public policy formulation. This combines three elements — data, participation, and policy — in a way that positions Decidim not just as a participation tool but as an infrastructure for evidence-based democratic governance.

### Detailed Explanation
The IBGE project aims to create an environment where Brazil's 5,570 municipalities can:
1. Access IBGE data easily (overcoming the current fragmentation and poor data quality)
2. Debate with each other and with federal managers to build policies
3. Share successful policies and cross-reference data
4. Invite civil society and universities at specific moments for broader input

Roque adds that this space should allow "more time to mature a more medium- and long-term perspective" — unlike the "Senate portal" which has rigid deadlines and produces superficial discussions. The vision is of a permanent, structured dialogue space that combines "the richness of evidence-based policies" with genuine participation.

Giovanni connects this to the broader movement for data as a common good, citing examples from the US (alliances formed when Trump censored climate data) and France (inability to implement minority policies due to lack of ethnic data). Marcos Vinicius's project positions Decidim at the intersection of three movements: open data, participatory democracy, and evidence-based policy.

### Specific Elements
- **IBGE as convener**: Using its constitutional role to host participatory policy spaces
- **Three components**: Data (IBGE statistics) + Tool (Decidim) + Participation (managers + civil society)
- **Target users**: 4,000+ municipalities lacking technical capacity for policy formulation
- **Beyond government**: Opening to civil society and universities at strategic moments
- **Long-term horizon**: Replacing superficial consultation with mature deliberation
- **Data sovereignty**: Reducing dependence on US tech platforms

### Key Quotations
- "The idea is to create environments within the IBGE where we can bring together these public managers, where this data will be easily accessible, but where they can debate among themselves to build policies." (Marcos Vinicius)
- "The vast majority of municipalities in Brazil… more than 4,000 do not have the technical capacity for the formulation of public policies." (Marcos Vinicius)
- "A free space to discuss public policies, including inviting civil society and universities." (Roque)
- "Participation in civil society beyond public managers… participation in civil society." (Marcos Vinicius)
