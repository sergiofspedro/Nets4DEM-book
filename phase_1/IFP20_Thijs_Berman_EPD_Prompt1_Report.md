## Theme 1: The Origins of EPD – From Brussels Antenna to Multi-Pillar Organisation

### Summary
The European Partnership for Democracy was founded in 2008 when a small group of democracy support organisations identified a need for a collective antenna in Brussels. Initially focused on policy analysis and advocacy on EU democracy support, the organisation evolved through financial struggles and expanded into research and programming. Berman frames EPD's origin as pragmatic—joining forces for intelligence on EU policymaking—rather than ideological.

### Detailed Explanation
Berman explains the founding impetus: "a few democracy support organisations gathered and thought we need an antenna in Brussels." The first motivation was "not to lobby and do advocacy and do research on European policies and their development on their own, but join forces and establish a secretariat of this small network." The goal was to analyse EU policymaking dynamics and "do advocacy on behalf of democracy support organisations to have proper EU policy on the issue."

The initial years were marked by financial precarity: "there was hardly any support. We got some support from the Netherlands, but it was all very small." The organisation accumulated "a big deficit the first 10 years of its existence." Despite these difficulties, it evolved as more members recognised the importance of having "a strong antenna in Brussels" influencing policies and gathering intelligence—particularly because "the European Union is a major player in funding and defining action on democracy support globally."

Berman himself was not a founder. He became director of NIMD (a member organisation) in 2019, served on EPD's board, and later became board chair. At the time of the interview, he was interim director replacing Ken Godfrey (on sabbatical).

### Specific Elements
- **Founded**: 2008
- **Initial motivation**: Collective Brussels antenna for policy monitoring and advocacy
- **Early funding**: Dutch support, but insufficient—deficit for first 10–12 years
- **Members**: Democracy support organisations (NGOs, not local authorities)
- **Berman's role**: NIMD director (2019–2024), EPD board chair, interim director (2025)
- **Key person**: Ken Godfrey (EPD director, on sabbatical)
- **Distinction**: ALDA is described as "a different kind of organisation" because it includes local authorities

### Key Quotations
- "The first impetus, the first motivation to get together… join forces and establish a secretariat."
- "The European Union is a major player in funding and defining action on democracy support globally."
- "It had a big deficit the first 10 years of its existence."

## Theme 2: The Three Pillars – Policy, Research, and Programming

### Summary
EPD's work is structured around three interconnected pillars: policy and advocacy (analysis and influencing of EU policymaking), research (publications like the Annual Review on European Democracy Support, plus Horizon/Nets4Dem projects), and programming (grant-funded projects that also subsidise the policy work). This triangular structure evolved from an initial focus on pure policy analysis.

### Detailed Explanation
Berman describes EPD as "a triangle." The first corner is **policy and advocacy**: analysing EU political debates and policymaking (e.g., the multi-annual financial framework) and lobbying the Commission, Council, and Parliament. The second corner is **research**: the flagship Annual Review on European Democracy Support, plus wider research projects. The third corner is **programming**: implementing projects to generate income that sustains the secretariat.

He explains the interdependence: "in order to be able simply to do policy and advocacy analysis and influencing, you have to find other sources of funding. You can't pay for this simply." Programming income (from EU grants) covers overhead that membership fees alone cannot sustain—"usually under the grants that we acquire, there is an acceptance that, and there's also a requirement that we do analysis of policies."

The evolution was from narrower to broader: "in the beginning, I think it was only policy analysis and research on policy… and it later evolved in more research also on related topics, not directly on policies, but democracy support and how does it function, what works well, what doesn't." This expanded to include underrepresented groups (women, youth, indigenous groups).

### Specific Elements
- **Pillar 1**: Policy and advocacy – analysis + influencing EU institutions
- **Pillar 2**: Research – Annual Review, Horizon projects, Nets4Dem
- **Pillar 3**: Programming – grant implementation funding the secretariat
- **Evolution**: Started as policy-only, expanded to research and programming
- **Income model**: Membership fees (~€180–190k/year) cover ~3 jobs; programming covers the rest
- **Key product**: Annual Review on European Democracy Support (published March each year)

### Key Quotations
- "EPD consists of three pillars, or it is a triangle."
- "In the beginning, I think it was only policy analysis and research on policy."
- "You can't pay for this simply, if you have no other source of funding."

## Theme 3: Secretariat, Not Headquarters – Governance and Coordination

### Summary
EPD's governance is designed around the principle that the Brussels office is a "Secretariat" serving member organisations, not a "headquarters" commanding them. The board consists of directors of member organisations who control strategic decisions, budget, and membership. The Secretariat's autonomy is deliberately limited, and it plays the role of convener and coordinator—particularly in forming consortia for EU funding. The tension between being a service provider and an implementing body is a constant challenge.

### Detailed Explanation
Berman emphasises the linguistic choice: "what we do here in Brussels, we are called the Secretariat. And it's not headquarters. And it is a thing that is well chosen." The board, composed of member directors, "decide about the main strategic questions… safeguard the stability of the organisation… decide about the budget." The Secretariat's autonomy is "relatively limited."

EPD's coordinating role is especially important for EU funding: "it is simply more efficient for members to gather in a consortium with EPD as the coordinator." The Secretariat can "ensure the impartiality of this coordinating role" in a way that a member organisation might struggle with.

However, this creates an inherent ambiguity. Berman acknowledges: "sometimes, EPD is an implementing organisation because it implements programmes itself, and sometimes, EPD is the secretariat working for all of them at the same time, and this ambiguity cannot be lifted." This dual identity becomes particularly strained during financial hardship, when members question why the Secretariat takes an implementing role.

### Specific Elements
- **Secretariat model**: Deliberately not called headquarters
- **Board**: Directors of member organisations (21 members)
- **General Assembly**: Twice yearly; approves budget, membership, strategic decisions
- **Category A/B members**: A members eligible for board, B members not; self-selected
- **Coordinating role**: EPD leads consortia for EU calls, ensuring impartiality
- **Inherent tension**: Secretariat as service provider vs. implementing body
- **Current financial pressure**: 12 of 42 staff contracts terminated due to funding cuts

### Key Quotations
- "It's not headquarters. And it is a thing that is well chosen."
- "The autonomy of the Secretariat is relatively limited."
- "This ambiguity cannot be lifted, cannot be deleted. You can't get rid of it because of the financial difficulty."

## Theme 4: The Fragile Balance – Competition and Cooperation Among Members

### Summary
Berman candidly describes the internal tensions within EPD's network, exacerbated by severe funding cuts across European democracy support. Competition for limited EU grants strains relationships between members and between members and the Secretariat. The ideal of cooperation clashes with organisational self-interest, and Berman identifies ego and institutional pride as significant factors alongside financial pressure.

### Detailed Explanation
The financial context is dire: "this year alone I have to terminate the contracts of 12 people out of 42… it's because of lack of funding." Global ODA dropped 23% in 2025 alone, with cuts from Sweden, the UK, the Netherlands, France, and Germany. This scarcity intensifies internal dynamics.

When new EU calls for proposals arrive, "many, many member organisations will apply. But you can't all benefit from one grant." The Secretariat must mediate: "we would speak with organisations and then try to put together the organisations that are best complementary to each other." This "demands a lot of talking, and it's not always easy."

The tension is structural: "there is much more tension now between members of the network and between members and the Secretariat." Members question the Secretariat's role: "why does EPD need to be in there anyway?" Berman notes this discussion is ongoing and difficult—"just now, before I joined you, I had this discussion with some team members."

Beyond money, Berman identifies organisational ego: "these organisations are used to be proud of themselves and decide for themselves… directors have an ego… everybody likes coordination. But everybody wants to coordinate and nobody wants to be coordinated." He calls this a daily reality: "the egos of organisations and their leaders is something that this secretariat has to deal with on a daily basis."

### Specific Elements
- **Staff cuts**: 12 of 42 positions eliminated (2025)
- **ODA decline**: 23% reduction in 2025; Sweden, UK, NL, France, Germany cut
- **Consortium mediation**: Secretariat determines complementary member groupings for bids
- **Member frustration**: Some ask "Why does EPD need to be in there?"
- **Ego factor**: Organisational pride and competition for leadership
- **Current state**: "It's not easy at all"

### Key Quotations
- "There is much more tension now between members of the network and between members and the Secretariat."
- "Everybody likes coordination. But everybody wants to coordinate and nobody wants to be coordinated."
- "The egos of organisations and their leaders is something that this secretariat has to deal with on a daily basis."

## Theme 5: Credibility and Impartiality – The Grand Bargain of Democracy Support

### Summary
Berman distinguishes between two credibility challenges. The easier one is maintaining analytical credibility with academic and political audiences through impartial, evidence-based work. The harder one is maintaining moral credibility with the world's most vulnerable people—especially when European democracies are incoherent on Gaza, trade policy, or poverty. This external credibility gap, he argues, is a deeper threat than any internal tension.

### Detailed Explanation
On academic credibility, Berman is confident: EPD researchers "have PhDs… they know how to do proper academic research." On political credibility, the key is impartiality: "we are credible as long as we remain impartial, focused on not the political choices in terms of partisan, political party, but focused on the values that we need to strengthen." EPD's stance is to speak with anyone "who does not hold a Kalashnikov"—including far-right parties—"as long as you are not a racist."

The deeper problem is external credibility. Berman asks: "how do you remain credible in the eyes of the most vulnerable people of the world? You have the situation in Gaza, with the genocide, and what has Europe done? If Europe does not raise its voice properly, then you get the question… why are you supporting democracy here and you keep silent anywhere else?" He also notes the cynicism of "the poorest people of the world who say, yeah, it's not very fashionable to speak about underrepresented groups, but only mention women and youth… they are completely right."

He extends this to EU policy incoherence: "if governments, on the one hand, accept corrupt governments somewhere and, on the other hand, are happy to fund civil society for its voice… what will you achieve in terms of democracy support? Nothing. The contrary."

### Specific Elements
- **Academic credibility**: PhD-level researchers, proper methodology
- **Political credibility**: Impartiality on values, not parties
- **Engagement boundary**: Anyone except racists/criminals; "even if they are not democratic"
- **Gaza challenge**: European silence undermines democracy support credibility globally
- **Poverty critique**: Focus on women/youth excludes poorest; this is a real problem
- **Policy incoherence**: EU trade policy vs. democracy support contradicts itself
- **Berman's diagnosis**: "I'm afraid of democrats who do not take their responsibility"

### Key Quotations
- "As long as you are not a racist, I will speak with anyone."
- "Where is your credibility? That is a much more difficult problem."
- "Democracy is very cheap. It takes political will."
- "I'm not afraid of dictators. I'm not afraid of lack of money. I'm afraid of democrats who do not take their responsibility."

## Theme 6: The Gaza Red Line – When a Network Cannot Speak

### Summary
EPD faced an internal crisis when trying to issue a statement on Israel-Palestine. Divisions among member organisations were too deep to reach consensus, and EPD's policy requires unanimity for external positions. The inability to speak on one of the most consequential issues for democracy support globally reveals the limits of a network-of-networks model and the political fault lines within European democracy support.

### Detailed Explanation
Berman recounts: "at some point we wanted to issue a statement on the situation in Israel-Palestine. And it was not possible." The divides were significant: "some of the organisations… stood behind the Israeli government, and then in the end it was decided not to issue a statement, because it was just impossible to get all members behind it."

EPD's internal rule compounds this: "if we issue a statement, or if we subscribe to a statement that others make… then if only one member objects and says this is a red line for us politically, we can't be part of this, then we don't sign." A single dissenting member can block the entire network from taking a position.

This reveals a structural limitation of network-of-networks governance: on deeply polarising issues, the lowest-common-denominator position may be silence—which itself carries political weight, especially for an organisation whose credibility depends on speaking for democratic values.

### Specific Elements
- **Issue**: Israel-Palestine / Gaza statement
- **Outcome**: No statement issued; internal divisions too deep
- **Rule**: Unanimity required for external positions—single member can block
- **Fault lines**: Pro-Israeli government members vs. those critical of Israel
- **Implication**: Network structure prevents timely response to crises
- **Contrast with ALDA**: Valmorbida described similar internal debate but different resolution

### Key Quotations
- "It was not possible, because some of the organisations were… the divides between organisations were quite big on the issue."
- "If only one member objects and says this is a red line for us politically… then we don't sign."

## Theme 7: Diversification of Funding – The Existential Question

### Summary
Berman sees the collapse of public funding for democracy support as a structural shift, not a cyclical downturn. His vision for survival is a radical diversification toward private philanthropy—accessing "millionaires and billionaires" to sustain democracy support work. He does not see a viable future dependent on public funding alone.

### Detailed Explanation
The scale of the crisis is clear: global ODA fell 23% in 2025, and not only because of USAID cuts—European countries also slashed budgets. Berman believes this will continue: "I'm afraid that this will continue. There is a very urgent life or death question on diversification of funding."

He describes the current model's unsustainability: EU grants allow only 7% overhead, which "is not enough, and you can't pay your rent, your salaries… it should be double that figure." Membership fees total only €180,000–190,000 per year—enough for three jobs. The arithmetic simply does not work without programming income.

His proposed solution is private funding: "we will need to create a new infrastructure for funding of this type of work to support democracy. It will be private money." EPD is working on "getting access to newer and older millionaires and billionaires and say, listen, it is nice that you fund this philanthropic thing… but perhaps we should also save democracy."

### Specific Elements
- **ODA decline**: 23% in 2025 across US and European donors
- **Overhead cap**: 7% on EU grants—insufficient for rent and salaries
- **Membership fees**: ~€180–190k/year (range: €500 to €25,000 per member)
- **Proposed solution**: Diversify to private philanthropy
- **Target**: "Newer and older millionaires and billionaires"
- **Berman's stance**: Devastated by cuts but realistic about probability of further decline

### Key Quotations
- "I have to terminate the contracts of 12 people out of 42 this year alone."
- "There is a very urgent life or death question on diversification of funding."
- "I don't see a scenario where these organisations can thrive… if it is only with public funding."

## Theme 8: Commoning and Ambiguity – The Network as Both Collective Good and Competitor

### Summary
When asked about "commoning" in networking, Berman interprets the concept as the tension between the network as a shared good and the network as a competitor for resources. Members appreciate EPD in principle but struggle with its dual role in practice. The ambiguity between secretariat and implementing body is structural, not accidental, and cannot be resolved under current funding constraints.

### Detailed Explanation
Berman initially struggles with the concept ("I'm sorry, I don't understand what it means") and later reframes it: members see EPD's value "as a common good, their good, as a form of community," but "in reality, it becomes very, very delicate to sustain this, to keep their support in reality." The perception shifts when the network also competes for the same funding pool.

He identifies an "unavoidable ambiguity": EPD is simultaneously a secretariat serving members and an implementing organisation competing for grants. This dual identity is not a design flaw but a consequence of financial necessity: "you can't get rid of it because of the financial difficulty that you don't have other sources."

The result is a network that is valued in principle but contested in practice—especially as funding shrinks and competition intensifies. Berman notes this perception has changed over time, "and it's not a change for the better."

### Specific Elements
- **Commoning reframed**: Tension between collective good and competitive reality
- **Dual identity**: Secretariat vs. implementing body
- **Structural cause**: Financial dependency on programming
- **Temporal change**: Perception has deteriorated ("not a change for the better")
- **Ambiguity**: Described as unavoidable, not resolvable

### Key Quotations
- "They see the value of an organisation, and in that sense, as a common good… In reality, it becomes very, very delicate to sustain this."
- "This ambiguity cannot be lifted… because of the financial difficulty."

## Theme 9: External Partnerships – The Brussels Ecosystem and Global Networks

### Summary
EPD maintains strategic partnerships at multiple levels: with other Brussels-based democracy support organisations for joint advocacy (especially on the EU budget), with American democracy support institutions (NDI, Carter Foundation, NED), and with implementing partners in the global South. The most important institutional relationships are with the European Commission (DG INTPA, EEAS, DG NEAR) and the European Parliament.

### Detailed Explanation
On EU budget advocacy, EPD cooperates closely with "international IDEA, with ENOC, the European network of political organisations like Abel, Stiftung, and some more," meeting weekly or biweekly to "share intelligence and develop a common position." Globally, EPD works with "NDI, the Carter Foundation, and other Americans, IRI… NED also."

The most important EU interlocutors are "DG INTPA, the development corporation DG, and the External Action Service, clearly. But also DG NEAR, of enlargement." The European Parliament is crucial because "they vote on the directives that define how funding shall be spent." The Council of Ministers remains "more of a black box"—Berman, a former MEP (2004–2014), notes with sadness "that not much has changed since then."

In the global South, partnerships are primarily operational—programme implementation with local civil society organisations. Berman cites work in Moldova (first citizens' assembly, with the Parliament), South Africa, and across Eastern Europe. Politically, EPD also engages in joint statements, such as the RightsCon conference in Zambia that was cancelled under Chinese pressure.

### Specific Elements
- **Brussels partners**: International IDEA, ENOC, Abel, Stiftung (budget advocacy)
- **Global partners**: NDI, Carter Foundation, IRI, NED
- **EU institutions**: Commission (DG INTPA, EEAS, DG NEAR), European Parliament, Council (opaque)
- **Global South**: Programme implementation in Moldova, South Africa, Eastern Europe
- **RightsCon case**: Zambia conference cancelled under Chinese influence—EPD joined statement
- **Berman's background**: MEP 2004–2014—gives insider perspective on EU institutions

### Key Quotations
- "More of a black box, Council, has always been. I was a member of the European Parliament between 2004 and 2014. I have to sadly say that not much has changed since then."
- "We have a weekly or two-weekly meetings on that issue to share intelligence and to develop a common position."
