## Theme 1: The Three-Layer Model as Decidim's Foundational Architecture

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
Decidim is analytically structured around three interdependent layers: the political layer (articulating democratic processes), the technical layer (open source, transparent, auditable code rooted in hacker ethics), and the technopolitical layer (democratic governance of the community itself). This was not an afterthought — it was a foundational decision that the code's license, architecture, and governance had to be democratic from the first line of code. "A platform for democracy, its governance obviously had to be democratic — if not, it was a contradiction or an oxymoron." The three-layer model operates simultaneously as an analytical framework, a design principle, and a permanent arena of productive tension.

**Detailed explanation:**
Arnau opens with the three-layer analogy as the project's self-understanding. The political layer concerns the object of the platform: articulating democratic processes. The technical layer — deliberately not dwelt upon but foundational — governs the technological conditions of the project: free and open source code, transparent and auditable, copyable and modifiable. This hacker ethics is embedded as a "foundational principle of the project itself," a political decision that from the first line of code everything would be open. The architecture of the code — whether it is more or less contributable — is itself a political choice that determines the project's capacity to extend, network, and generate cooperation processes. The third layer, the technopolitical, concerns the democratic governance of the community: "There could not be Decidim without a community that governed it."

The layers are not merely descriptive but operational. Governments choose Decidim because it is "a safe, open solution with public financing, with public guarantees." The technical and political layers reinforce each other: open code enables trust, trust enables adoption, adoption extends the community, the community improves the code. The technopolitical layer ensures the project cannot be captured by any single actor. Arnau describes how the three layers come into conflict — for instance, when the political layer is "very excited" about federation protocols while "the technical layer is afraid" of the technological challenge — but frames this conflict as productive rather than pathological.

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Decidim, Barcelona City Council, Metadecidim association, free software movement, indignados movement, Occupy.
- Practices: Three-layer model as analytical and operational framework; open-source licensing from first line of code; democratic governance as architectural principle; modular code architecture enabling contributability.
- Problems: Tension between political ambition (federation) and technical feasibility; the difficulty of maintaining democratic governance as the project scales.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**
*"A code, a platform for democracy — its governance obviously had to be democratic. If not, it was a contradiction or an oxymoron."*
*"From the first line of code, they are open. You can see how it is written."*

## Theme 2: Genealogy and the "Oil Slick" Diffusion — Viral Spread Without Strategy

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
Decidim's birth in 2016 Barcelona drew on three genealogies: the 2011 indignados/Occupy/square movements, the free software movement in Spain (particularly Barcelona), and a "collective need of democracies." The project spread through an "oil slick effect" — without any international extension department, without neighbor offices, cities and governments worldwide approached Decidim. Helsinki adopted the platform "almost automatically without us knowing." The Barcelona Provincial Council, French cities, Italian municipalities all knocked on the door. This semi-invisible virality was enabled by the open code itself — anyone could copy and replicate — and by the political moment of post-2011 democratic innovation.

**Detailed explanation:**
Arnau traces three genealogical streams converging in Decidim: (1) the mobilizations since 2011 — indignados, the squares, Occupy; (2) the free software movement in Spain and specifically Barcelona; (3) a "collective need of democracies." These genealogies are "inserted almost in the DNA of the community." The initial scope was Barcelona-based, but "very soon it overflowed." Cities, governments, activists, and researchers worldwide approached Decidim not because of marketing or outreach but because they wanted to understand "what were the democratic innovations we were making in the city of Barcelona."

The "oil slick effect" — a vivid metaphor for decentralized, capillary diffusion — was enabled by the open code: "in the end, the code is there and anyone can copy and replicate it." Helsinki's automatic adoption without Decidim's knowledge exemplifies this dynamic. Code adoption becomes "the excuse to make the link for where to start weaving a community." From this emerged the Metadecidim days (annual conferences), the community portal, and ultimately the 2019 constitution of the Metadecidim association. The international Decidim Fest, now in its sixth edition (2026), consolidates this growth with participants from Brazil, across Europe, Argentina, and Mendoza.

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Decidim, Barcelona City Council, indignados/Occupy movements, free software movement (Spain/Barcelona), Helsinki, Barcelona Provincial Council, French cities, Italian municipalities.
- Contexts: Barcelona (2016 origin), Spain, Finland (Helsinki), France, Italy, Brazil, Argentina (Mendoza).
- Practices: "Oil slick effect" — organic, demand-driven diffusion; Metadecidim annual conferences; community portal; Decidim Fest (6th edition, 2026).
- Problems: Growth without strategy creates coordination challenges; semi-invisible virality means limited control over who adopts and how.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**

## Theme 3: Metadecidim Association (2019) — Governance Independence as Institutional Safeguard

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
In 2019, the community constituted the Metadecidim association as a legal instrument independent from all governments, designed to guarantee democratic governance and safeguard the digital commons regardless of political changes in Barcelona. The governance model operates through concentric circles: a paid technical team (product, communication, strategy), an elected coordination committee (voluntary, by community vote), partners (fee-paying, higher involvement), the general community (any contributor), official service providers (companies with collaboration agreements), and governments (with institutional agreements, e.g., New York City). Crucially, the association does not compete with service providers — it handles code curation, research, and consulting, tasks justified by its unique community nature.

**Detailed explanation:**
The decision to constitute the Metadecidim association was driven by a structural insight: "the project could not have the exclusive anchorage of Barcelona." A change of government could threaten everything, and the project needed an actor with international capacity. The association was modeled on large open source communities like Wikipedia, Mozilla, and Drupal — "we did a lot of research and talked a lot with these communities to learn how they had done it."

The governance architecture is layered. At the center: a technical secretariat with a product team, communication team, and strategic management team. Alongside it: a coordination committee of eight people, elected by community vote, working hand-in-hand with the technical office. Around them: the community, split into two tiers — partners (paying a symbolic fee, with higher involvement) and the broader community (anyone who contributes). Then: service providers — companies doing development, facilitation, or communication on Decidim — who have official partnership agreements that partially finance the association in exchange for visibility. Finally: governments, with collaboration agreements (New York City for participatory budgets, plus a "committee of cities and institutions").

Arnau emphasizes a critical design choice: "the association itself is not a service provider, it does not compete with the companies that provide services." This prevents the association from becoming a competitor to its own ecosystem. Instead, it does "code curatorship, research, consulting — things that only the community can do."

Community governance operates transparently on Metadecidim — "everything happens in Decidim, everything is transparent, the whole community can participate through open channels." There are monthly community meetings, committee meetings, working groups on usability/design, development, and research (currently focused on AI). The governance is described as "a living community that has a series of protocols and regulations, but is also redefining itself as it progresses and grows."

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Metadecidim association, Barcelona City Council (Arnau, Carol, Andrés), coordination committee (Xavier, Antonio, others), Nilo (technical office), Elsa (communication), Jupa (design), New York City, service providers/companies.
- Practices: Concentric governance circles; community-elected coordination committee; symbolic partner fees; official partnership agreements with companies; government collaboration agreements; committee of cities and institutions; monthly community meetings; working groups (usability, development, research/AI); transparent governance on Metadecidim platform.
- Problems: The transition from voluntarist activism to professionalization; managing the tension between pre-association community forms and formal legal structures; ensuring the association doesn't become a service provider competing with its ecosystem.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**
*"We saw that in order to guarantee democratic governance, the project could not have the exclusive anchorage of Barcelona."*
*"The association is not a service provider, it does not compete with the companies that provide services."*
*"Everything happens in Metadecidim — everything is transparent, the whole community can participate through open channels."*

## Theme 4: The Social Contract — Political Positioning Beyond the License

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
Decidim's "social contract" functions as an internal constitution — an instrument validated, discussed, and approved by the community that governs acceptable use beyond what the open source license covers. When Piñera's right-wing Chilean government sought to use Decidim for a constitutional process, the community mobilized, debated intensely, and issued a statement rejecting the use: "Decidim is not good for anything if there is no real participation." Similarly, the community took a political position on Palestine, refusing to let individuals use the community space for partisan positioning while asserting collective political agency. Arnau frames this capacity — "the agency of the community to take a position in certain political situations" — as a distinctive feature. The social contract is now under pressure to evolve, particularly regarding AI.

**Detailed explanation:**
Arnau describes two cases of community political agency. The Chile case: Piñera's government (pre-Boric, "almost far-right") contacted Decidim to use the platform for the constitutional debate process. "A part of the community said no to our name." An intense debate followed, resulting in a community statement: "SIDIN [Decidim] is not good for anything if there is no real participation — we reject the use of Decidim." Arnau emphasizes that this was not about censorship but about defending the social contract: "an instrument validated by the community... that serves us to tell any government or institution that uses Decidim that not only the license has to be complied with, but the social contract has to be fulfilled."

A parallel capacity was exercised on Palestine. Giovanni notes that Decidim is "one of the few networks where the network has taken a stand" — it didn't let individuals use the community space for partisan positions while asserting collective political voice. This dual capacity — to refuse instrumental use by hostile actors and to take collective positions on issues of principle — distinguishes Decidim from neutral-platform networks.

The social contract is now under pressure to evolve. Arnau observes that "it is beginning to be seen that it has limitations" and that the community is debating how to update it, particularly concerning AI: "a statement has to be prepared on how AI is going to be used... not just anything is enough, not just any AI is worth, and the principles of the social contract also have to be applied to AI."

Arnau also introduces the concept of "participatory washing" — the attempt to use technology as a "magic solution to a problem that is not real." This is the first warning Decidim gives any organization: "if there is no political commitment, it doesn't work."

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Decidim community, Piñera government (Chile), coordination committee, Palestine solidarity movement.
- Contexts: Chile (pre-Boric constitutional process), Palestine/Israel conflict.
- Practices: Social contract as internal constitution; community debate and collective statements; refusal of instrumental use; concept of "participatory washing."
- Problems: Limitations of the current social contract; need for AI-specific governance; tension between being a "free" platform (open source) and restricting use by hostile actors.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**

## Theme 5: The Tension Between Meritocracy and Shared Needs — "My Need vs. the Commons' Need"

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
The open source world operates on a meritocratic ethic: those who contribute more code earn more karma and voice. But Decidim introduces a countervailing dynamic — "the voice of the community that thinks about how to improve democratic processes has had more weight." Arnau articulates a permanent, productive tension between individual/company needs and the "need of the commons." Companies, driven by customer demands, advocate for specific functionalities; the community applies the "rule of 3" (a need must be shared by at least three platforms) to determine what enters the core. Even Barcelona, the founding city, has features kept outside the main repository when they were not shared needs. This tension between "my need" and "the commons' need" is "productive, because it has to be negotiated permanently."

**Detailed explanation:**
Arnau acknowledges the meritocratic ethic of open source: "whoever contributes and does it with greater quality has greater karma and has a greater capacity to have recognition." But Decidim bends this dynamic through its political commitment. Companies become "defenders of the interests of the customer in front of the community" — their business needs drive them to push specific functionalities. The community, by contrast, thinks about "how the code reinforces democratic processes" and asks: is this a shared need or a particular one?

The "rule of 3" operationalizes this: if a need is shared by at least three platforms, it may warrant core inclusion. Otherwise, Decidim's modular architecture allows anyone to build modules that work with Decidim without entering the core. Barcelona itself has had to accept this: "Barcelona understood perfectly well that this was not a shared need, so Barcelona has things that are not in the main repository."

Arnau notes that the core's defenders have been "somewhat conservative" — sometimes "strictly too technical, issues of architecture and sustainability, and not so much political." This creates a counter-tension: technical conservatism can impede political innovation. But the overall framing is that this permanent negotiation between particular and shared needs is the community's health — it forces clarity about what the commons actually is.

The debate extends to technological frontiers: federation protocols (technologically complex, politically exciting), AI (hype vs. substance), and the balance between possibilities and priorities. Giovanni crystallizes the insight: "The difference between possibilities and priorities is a central element of the community discussion — there is no money for everything and no energy to do everything."

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Companies/service providers, Barcelona City Council, government of Brazil, community members.
- Practices: "Rule of 3" for core inclusion; modular architecture enabling non-core modules; permanent negotiation between particular and shared needs; distinction between possibilities and priorities.
- Problems: Technical conservatism vs. political innovation; companies' customer-driven advocacy vs. community's democratic-process focus; the challenge of identifying genuinely shared needs across diverse use contexts.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**
*"There is this tension between my need — as a company, as an individual, as a person — and the need of the commons."*
*"It is a productive tension, because it has to be negotiated permanently."*
*"The voice of the community that thinks about how to improve democratic processes has had more weight."*

## Theme 6: Technology as Guarantee of Democracy — Decidim vs. Proprietary Platforms

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
Decidim's competitive advantage is not technological superiority — it is that "Decidim is not a company that wants to make money selling decision software. Decidim is a project that wants more democracy in the world." The community provides a guarantee that no proprietary vendor can match: governments who adopt Decidim know they will not be trapped in a vendor cage, forced into escalating service contracts, or abandoned when the vendor pivots. The UN Digital Public Goods Alliance recognition formalizes this status. The contrast with GoVocal (proprietary platform acquired by Osprey in France, which "has a business model and wants to make money") crystallizes the distinction: "the starting point is abysmally different — above all it is political, not economic."

**Detailed explanation:**
Arnau argues that the Decidim model fundamentally changes the rules of the market for participation software. The difference from proprietary competitors is not about code quality — "no matter how well it works at a technological level" — but about the political nature of the project. A proprietary vendor has a business model; its decisions are ultimately governed by profit. Decidim's decisions are governed by the democratic governance of the community.

Giovanni adds the user perspective from his interviews: governments told him "we chose Decidim because they have the guarantee of working around a common good." The community provides assurance against "the aggressiveness that companies classically have" — the risk that technology evolves and governments, unable to evaluate which technology is more ethical, find themselves "inside a prison, a cage where we are then forced to buy more services and more services."

This guarantee is "connatured to the dimension of seeing technology as a common good and not as a product to make money." Arnau extends the argument: Decidim is not just setting different rules of the game in a market — it is "drawing another way of doing a public service with the community... drawing another world on how things can be built." The recognition as a UN Digital Public Good, the opening of an open source office in Barcelona, the city pact for digital rights — all are "triggered by the trajectory of Decidim itself."

Barcelona's internal experience with participatory budgeting illustrates the model's depth: two very powerful editions, budget doubled for the next term, communities already organizing around new projects, and the challenge of "universal participation" and face-to-face digital mediation.

## Theme 8: Consul, Local Chapters, Big Tech — External Relations as Ongoing Negotiation

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
Decidim's external relations are characterized by pragmatic openness with political guardrails. Consul (the other major open source participation platform) is viewed as a "first cousin" — they "tried to negotiate, tried to go hand in hand, but didn't get it for more technical than political reasons" — but Arnau sees potential for collaboration. Local chapters are welcomed as territorial extension models, but with a non-negotiable condition: "the form of governance has to be untouchable — if we end up reproducing local chapters that subvert the democratic logic of the project, this is the risk." On Big Tech, the approach is modulated pragmatism: WhatsApp integration is acceptable if it extends participation, using open protocols to avoid dependency — "when we have the objective of extending participation to the maximum, we have to modulate what instruments we use."

**Detailed explanation:**
On Consul, Arnau is notably generous: "I think it is a first cousin because it follows many of our principles, it is open source, so we cannot reproach it for anything." The initial attempt at collaboration failed for "more technical than political reasons," but he sees shared DNA and "the interesting thing would be to explore some forms of collaboration."

On local chapters: Arnau sees "an opportunity and it is good news that these cooperation mechanisms are built." The goals are to "energize local ecosystems, strengthen the companies that provide services, and extend the models of participation at the local level." But the red line is governance: local chapters must not subvert the democratic logic. The vision is distributed, not centralized management — "precisely what we want is that there is not a centralization."

On Big Tech, the framing is pragmatic modulation rather than ideological purity. Integrating with WhatsApp is "problematic because of the relationship with big tech," but if it extends participation levels and uses open protocols, "a priori, I don't see it as bad." Arnau articulates a broader balancing principle: "when you have to give security guarantees [for a vote], you also have to measure accessibility — if you make a very robust system but no one can vote, then you have failed." Giovanni connects this to the classic commons dilemma: "radical purists say you don't talk to the market, and others say you have to maintain an open dialogue."
