## Theme 1: The Self-Funded Professional Body — Autonomy as a Core Value

### Summary
IAF (International Association of Facilitators) is a self-funded professional body with approximately 2,000 members worldwide. It deliberately avoids external funding from donors or institutions to maintain its independence and protect the network from "business dynamics." Its funding model — membership fees, certification/accreditation fees, and conference revenue — allows it to keep its budget entirely focused on networking rather than territorial operations.

### Detailed Explanation
Gerardo stresses this point from the start: "our organisation is self-funded, so we don't get funding from other bodies, donors or other kind of people. We live the money we raise from members, so membership and mostly accreditations, plus just maybe sometimes a little bit from conferences." This model means "we use our budget entirely for networking, and we haven't really the need to do something on the territory."

Tamara adds that individual chapters may apply for funding (e.g., Erasmus+ in Europe), but these projects are run through separate legal structures, not under the IAF umbrella. The organisation is deliberate about keeping "the business out of the network" — business dynamics, if introduced, would change the nature of member relationships. As Gerardo explains: "if you do a business, which can also be, I mean, just working on a project funded by an external donor, which means that someone is getting paid to do something… you enter into a completely different thing."

Giovanni notes the paradox that in the current funding environment, "there is more funding for networking than for operating in the territories." For IAF, this paradox does not apply because its networking IS its core mission.

### Specific Elements
- **Self-funded model**: Membership fees + certification + conference revenue
- **No external donors**: Deliberate choice to maintain independence
- **Budget entirely for networking**: No territorial operations to fund
- **Local chapters may seek funding**: Through separate legal structures
- **Rationale**: Avoid business dynamics that would change member relationships
- **Governance implication**: No accountability to external funders

### Key Quotations
- "Our organisation is self-funded, so we don't get funding from other bodies, donors or other kind of people." (Gerardo)
- "We use our budget entirely for networking, and we haven't really the need to do something on the territory." (Gerardo)
- "We keep, I would say quite intentionally, the business out of the network." (Gerardo)
- "If you do a business… you enter into a completely different thing." (Gerardo)

## Theme 2: Community of Practice as Primary Value — The Solopreneur's Need for Connection

### Summary
IAF members join primarily for community, not for training or certification. The organisation's core value proposition is providing a professional community for facilitators who are often solopreneurs or independent consultants — people who work alone and need peer connection, exchange, and mutual learning. The real benefit emerges when members contribute, not just consume.

### Detailed Explanation
Both Gerardo and Tamara confirm that the most-cited reason for joining is "community of practice." At a recent conference in Mexico, members affirmed that "community and networking is the first reason why they join." This is because "very often in our practice, we are kind of solopreneurs" — independent consultants working alone who "seek for a community, they seek for somebody to work with, to find their people to exchange."

Crucially, the IAF does not provide training directly. Learning happens peer-to-peer: "our organisation is not providing training per se, but we are mostly relying on the learning between members." This "specific level of networking" is where members organise around topics, share methods, and learn from each other.

Gerardo articulates a key principle: "the real benefit is once you start doing something for the association. When you offer something to the others, you get something back." The organisation changed its strategic pillar from "offering value to members" to "helping our members to shine in the profession" — recognising that the value is co-created, not delivered.

### Specific Elements
- **Primary reason for joining**: Community and networking (confirmed at Mexico conference)
- **Member profile**: Mostly solopreneurs, independent consultants
- **No training provision**: Learning happens peer-to-peer
- **Strategic pillar**: "Helping our members to shine in the profession"
- **Value co-creation**: Get benefit by contributing, not just consuming
- **Membership size**: ~2,000 globally

### Key Quotations
- "Community and networking is the first reason why they join. Because very often in our practice, we are kind of solopreneurs." (Tamara)
- "They seek for a community, they seek for somebody to work with to find their people to exchange." (Tamara)
- "You get a benefit once you start doing something for the association. When you offer something to the others, you get something back." (Gerardo)
- "We changed the strategic pillar from offering value to members to helping our members to shine in the profession." (Gerardo)

## Theme 3: Safe Space and the Management of Commercial Dynamics

### Summary
IAF deliberately maintains a "safe space" for members by separating commercial dynamics from community exchange. This is operationalised through multiple dedicated spaces: self-organised Special Interest Groups for deep topical exchange, Share and Learn sessions for peer feedback, and a formal Marketplace for business opportunities. Ethical rules govern behaviour, and the organisation has complaint procedures that are used but rarely escalate to expulsion.

### Detailed Explanation
Giovanni probes the tension between cooperation and competition, noting that solopreneurs need both community support and business opportunities. Tamara describes how IAF structures this: "self-organised communities that people gather around a topic" (Special Interest Groups), "share and learn sessions" where members offer methods or trials for feedback, and a "marketplace" where external work requests are posted for members to apply.

Gerardo clarifies that selling services is not unethical — it is simply not the association's role: "we don't find selling your trainings unethical. It's not something we do as an association. We don't promote your training in our network." The separation is spatial and structural, not moral.

The safe space also requires enforcement. IAF has an ethical code, complaint procedures, and appeal processes. Tamara describes a case where a member misrepresented their expiring status for business promotion. The board intervened, and the outcome was a systemic improvement: digital badges that automatically expire, preventing future misuse. Gerardo notes that in 30 years, no member has been expelled through a complaint — "we talk with the people and try to find a solution."

### Specific Elements
- **Special Interest Groups**: Self-organised around topics
- **Share and Learn sessions**: Offer methods for peer feedback
- **Marketplace**: External work requests for members
- **Ethical rules**: Enforced but rarely escalated to expulsion
- **Complaint process**: Written → board → regional director → resolution
- **System improvement**: Learned from individual cases (e.g., digital badges)
- **Safe space philosophy**: Not about censorship — about transparent rules

### Key Quotations
- "Safety is being protected by aggression, by an excess of marketing. But at the same time, safe is also a place where you know exactly what will happen." (Giovanni)
- "We try to be a safe space for our members. So for us, safety is important." (Gerardo)
- "We never expelled a member because of this, but we had several complaint processes. We went through this complaint process." (Gerardo)
- "We didn't intervene only with the solution of this specific case, but we learned from this case and then we did some improvements." (Tamara)

## Theme 4: Leveraging Cultural Difference — North-South Dynamics in Facilitation

### Summary
IAF's global structure creates opportunities for cross-cultural exchange that are considered the organisation's "real added value." Facilitation styles differ markedly between regions: North America focuses on business facilitation, Latin America on indigenous community participation, Africa incorporates music and WhatsApp-based methods. The organisation intentionally creates spaces for these differences to meet, through cross-regional mentoring, diverse committee composition, and global conferences.

### Detailed Explanation
Giovanni asks about the "ecology of knowledge" between North and South, using the example of African practitioners innovating with social networks (not internet) because of cost. Tamara observes that at the Mexico conference, a clear discussion emerged: "in North America, facilitation mostly focused on business… while in South, that was more connected to the communities, indigenous communities, including them into the system."

Gerardo adds: "if you work with African people, they use a lot of music. They start with a song." In Africa, online workshops often happen without cameras due to bandwidth constraints — an adaptation European facilitators would not consider. These differences are not problems to be solved but resources to be learned from: "it's very useful for us also to see what kind of facilitation, how a facilitation is applied in different contexts."

The organisation operationalises this through several mechanisms: the mentoring programme matches mentors and mentees across different regions; committees are deliberately composed to include representatives from each region; global conferences bring diverse styles into direct contact. The main constraint is no longer geography but time zones — connecting Japan with Vancouver or Oceania remains the biggest practical challenge.

### Specific Elements
- **North America**: Business facilitation focus
- **Latin America**: Indigenous community participation
- **Africa**: Music, WhatsApp, low-bandwidth methods
- **Europe**: Split between Western (business) and Eastern (public participation)
- **Cross-regional mentoring**: Mentors/mentees from different regions
- **Diverse committees**: Requirement for regional representation
- **Time zone challenge**: New constraint replacing geography
- **Philosophy**: Everyone has something to contribute — learn from difference, not despite it

### Key Quotations
- "You don't join the IAF to work with other people in Milan. The real added value comes when you are connected with people from other cultures." (Gerardo)
- "If you work with African people, they use a lot of music. They start with a song." (Gerardo)
- "We create spaces and we invite exchange. Everyone has something to contribute and you can learn from everyone." (Tamara)
- "North-South differences are becoming much more close. You need to work with African people in Europe." (Gerardo)

## Theme 5: From Volunteer Club to Professional Association — A Traumatic Transition

### Summary
IAF is in a painful but necessary transition from a volunteer-run organisation (informal, friendship-based, with high board turnover) to a professional association with staff, systems, and institutional memory. The transition was triggered by the recognition that volunteers could no longer sustain the organisation's scale, but it has been traumatic — including the loss of their association management provider and the challenge of maintaining organisational memory.

### Detailed Explanation
Gerardo describes the IAF's history: founded 50 years ago by a group of friends (from ICA — the Institute of Cultural Affairs), it operated for decades as a volunteer-run "old friends club." The first non-North American board members arrived gradually — Gerardo was the first European chair, Tamara the first chair from Serbia. Board turnover was high because volunteers needed time to learn the organisation's mechanics before they could be effective.

The key shift: "at some point we realised that it was really a must, we couldn't rely anymore on our volunteer work as a board to run the organisation." They contracted an Association Management Organisation (AMO), but when that contract ended, "we found ourselves without an office in Canada with two bank accounts in America." They have since hired a new AMO and an executive director.

The challenge of institutional memory is acute: "one of our big problems is the corporate memory and the high turnover that we have in the government." The board's ability to maintain long-term relationships with partner organisations is limited because each new board starts from scratch. The founding generation (ICA) still exerts influence — Gerardo mentions receiving emails from founders complaining about new practices: "I was one of the founders. We have never done something like this before."

### Specific Elements
- **50-year history**: Founded by ICA friends group
- **Volunteer era**: Board of volunteers, limited structure
- **Transition trigger**: Could not scale without professional staff
- **Traumatic moment**: Loss of AMO, no office, messy finances
- **Current state**: New AMO + executive director hired
- **Corporate memory problem**: High board turnover, no consistent relationship management
- **Founder influence**: ICA still exerts informal power through complaints
- **Need for more funding**: Professional office costs more than volunteer model

### Key Quotations
- "At some point we realised that it was really a must, we couldn't rely anymore on our volunteer work as a board to run the organisation." (Gerardo)
- "We found ourselves without an office in Canada with two bank accounts in America, a bit messy and complicated." (Gerardo)
- "One of our big problems is the corporate memory and the high turnover that we have in the government." (Gerardo)
- "When I started to volunteer for the IAF was around 2011. Europe was an independent island in the organisation." (Gerardo)

## Theme 6: Networking as People-to-People — The Limits of Organisational Partnership

### Summary
IAF's networking philosophy is fundamentally interpersonal: "networks are made by people, not by organisations." The organisation's external partnerships depend on individual board members' personal connections, making them vulnerable to turnover. Institutional partnerships with other global bodies (ICF, ICA) exist but are limited by the organisation's voluntary governance and lack of corporate memory.

### Detailed Explanation
When Giovanni asks about partnerships with other networks (e.g., AI ethics networks, democracy networks), Gerardo and Tamara acknowledge that the IAF is connected to the International Coaching Federation (ICF), the Institute of Cultural Affairs (ICA), and various national facilitation networks. However, these connections are largely individual-driven: a board member who also belongs to another organisation creates a bridge. When that board member leaves, the connection weakens.

Gerardo illustrates: "I had the pleasure to meet the executive director of the International Coaching Federation in a meeting… it happened just because one of our board colleagues was in contact with this lady." The hired executive director is expected to solve this by providing continuity and the capacity to "establish more long-term relationship with these networks."

The ICA relationship is particularly significant: the founders of IAF came from ICA, and ICA's "Technology of Participation" was the original endorsed training programme. This creates a complex dynamic where ICA is both a partner and a founding influence, sometimes resistant to change.

### Specific Elements
- **Partnership model**: Individual-driven, not institutional
- **Key partners**: ICF (coaching), ICA (Technology of Participation), national facilitation networks
- **Vulnerability**: Connections lost when board members leave
- **Solution**: Executive director for relationship continuity
- **ICA relationship**: Foundational but sometimes resistant to evolution
- **Geographic challenge**: Global organisation, so regional partnerships (e.g., EU) are less relevant

### Key Quotations
- "Networking is made by people, not by organisations." (Gerardo)
- "We have been working with ICF until a point, but then we have been working with another organisation… because a board member was also a board member of that other organisation." (Gerardo)
- "One of our big problems is the corporate memory and the ability to establish more long-term relationship with these networks." (Gerardo)
- "Our regional organisation… Europe and the European Commission is very important in Europe, but it's not so important if you look at it from a global perspective." (Gerardo)
