## Theme 1: From PB Hub to People Powered — An Origin Story of Experimentation and Expansion

### Summary
People Powered did not emerge from a single founding vision but from an experimental process: a global workshop identified the need for a "hub" to support participatory budgeting practitioners. From this seed, the organisation grew into a global member-based network that expanded beyond PB to embrace multiple participatory methods, design principles, and a more flexible understanding of what participation means in different cultural contexts.

### Detailed Explanation
Clara traces the origin to a global workshop convened by Reboot and funded by the Hewlett Foundation and others. Practitioners, NGOs, and government staff identified the most pressing needs for making participatory budgeting scalable and effective. One outcome was creating a "hub" for knowledge exchange — the PB Hub, which Clara co-founded with Josh Learner.

The hub operated with two boards: a research board and a practitioner board, which set priorities for grants, resources, and support. Nikhil joined a few months after People Powered began as the fourth staff member. The organisation initially remained centred on PB due to its institutional history, but over time diversified into citizens' assemblies, legislative theatre, and participatory policymaking.

The critical shift was from methodological rigidity to design principles: "the important thing was the design and design principles behind it… what are the key design principles that you are using to make this process inclusive, meaningful, binding?" This reflects a maturation from a single-issue network to a flexible, context-responsive organisation.

### Specific Elements
- **Origin**: Global workshop → PB Hub → People Powered
- **Initial governance**: Research board + Practitioner board
- **Initial focus**: Participatory budgeting only
- **Expansion**: Citizens' assemblies, legislative theatre, participatory policymaking
- **Conceptual shift**: From methodology-focused to design-principle-focused
- **Key insight**: "Citizens assembly" does not resonate in African contexts as it does in Europe
- **Vocabulary adaptation**: From "democracy" to "participatory governance" to simply "participation"

### Key Quotations
- "The important thing was the design and design principles behind it. It's not as much about the methodology." (Clara)
- "We've been able to diversify our work to go more in-depth on the other participatory mechanisms." (Nikhil)
- "Some things that people call… citizens assemblies… this didn't resonate in the African context at least two years ago." (Clara)
- "We've been using the term 'participation' without saying 'democracy' more." (Nikhil)

## Theme 2: Member-Based Legitimacy — The Network as Source of Credibility

### Summary
People Powered's legitimacy derives from its members. The organisation explicitly positions itself as a member-based network where the staff's role is to support, coordinate, and amplify — but the expertise and credibility come from the global community of practitioners. This is operationalised through participatory internal governance (People Powered Decides) and member review of all major outputs.

### Detailed Explanation
Nikhil emphasises that "we really do derive our legitimacy from our members." The staff brings expertise, but "the work that we produce is reviewed and it's contributed to by our members, and in that way it is relevant in different contexts." Members are described as "the experts and the leaders of this global movement for participatory democracy."

The annual **People Powered Decides** process — a participatory budget and planning exercise where members vote on organisational priorities — is a key governance mechanism that practices democracy internally. This creates alignment between the organisation's mission and its internal operations.

However, this model creates tensions. When the organisation engages members in committees to implement projects, questions arise about whether this is genuinely collaborative or extractive — "using free labour from our members to move our work forward." This tension between meaningful member engagement and efficient programme delivery is ongoing and unresolved.

### Specific Elements
- **Member base**: Grew from ~50 to ~300 in five years
- **Legitimacy model**: Members as experts and leaders
- **Internal democracy**: Annual People Powered Decides (participatory budget/planning)
- **Staff role**: Coordination, support, amplification
- **Tension**: Extractive vs. genuinely collaborative member engagement
- **"Connective tissue"**: Organisation as hub connecting members to each other

### Key Quotations
- "One of the first things that I say about it is that we're a member-based organization, because I think we really do derive our legitimacy from our members." (Nikhil)
- "Our members are like the experts and the leaders of this global movement for participatory democracy." (Nikhil)
- "We do the annual People Powered Decides, which is like the participatory budget slash planning process with our members. So in that way we're practicing participatory democracy internally." (Nikhil)
- "We're small. We do a lot. We're very prolific with a very small team." (Clara)

## Theme 3: Protecting Reputation in a Hostile Political Environment

### Summary
People Powered faces three levels of reputational threat: (1) supporting processes that are merely cosmetic participation ("participation washing"), (2) the current US political environment where terms like "democracy," "climate," and "inclusion" are becoming "forbidden terms," and (3) the risk of being associated with members or governments that do not genuinely practice democratic values. The organisation has responded by incorporating in Europe to diversify risk and by becoming more selective about members and programme participants.

### Detailed Explanation
Clara identifies the primary threat: "we deliver participation without real power, we are threatening our reputation." Any process that People Powered supports that turns out to be "fake cosmetic participation" damages the organisation's credibility. The second, more existential threat is the current political climate in the US: "the biggest threat People Power and other organizations are facing at the moment is persecution. Because we talk about democracy, climate inclusion. These are forbidden terms in the US."

Nikhil adds that a funder questioned why People Powered works with programme participants from non-democratic countries, asking if they are "complicit in what the government is doing." This has forced the organisation to become "more selective and more thoughtful about who we are working with."

The response has been strategic: incorporating in Europe to diversify risk ("not putting all of our eggs in the basket of the US") and developing clearer criteria for member selection. The organisation is aware that its reputation depends not only on what it does, but on who it associates with.

### Specific Elements
- **Reputational threat #1**: Supporting cosmetic participation
- **Reputational threat #2**: US political persecution (forbidden terms)
- **Reputational threat #3**: Association with non-democratic actors
- **Response #1**: Incorporating in Europe for risk diversification
- **Response #2**: Stricter member/participant selection
- **Response #3**: Democratic Narratives Alliance to reframe democracy messaging

### Key Quotations
- "Whenever we deliver participation without real power, we are threatening our reputation." (Clara)
- "The biggest threat people power and other organizations are facing at the moment is persecution. Democracy, climate inclusion — these are forbidden terms in the US." (Clara)
- "We recently incorporated in Europe. A big motivation for that was diversifying our risk." (Nikhil)
- "Over time, as we get more interest to join as members… we've had to become more selective and more thoughtful about who we are working with." (Nikhil)

## Theme 4: The Challenge of Community Building — Regional Hubs and Member Initiative

### Summary
People Powered has struggled with the classic network challenge: how to move from a staff-driven model to one where members spontaneously initiate and organise activities. Efforts to create regional hubs (Latin America, Africa, Europe, Asia) have had limited success despite dedicated funding. The organisation has adapted by maintaining informal regional connections (WhatsApp groups) while focusing its formal energy on global-scale activities.

### Detailed Explanation
Clara describes the ideal: "the great virtue of a network is when you have members meeting and working from themselves and this network creates things that it's not connected to the main node anymore." But the reality is that members have their own organisations to run, and "being part of the network, it's also work." Without immediate value, members do not engage proactively.

Nikhil illustrates this with the experience of regional hubs: "one year we even had some funding to give a lead organization of the hub. But then, even with that funding, there wasn't much to show from it." The hub leaders either could not organise effectively, or participants were not engaged. The organisation has concluded that the skill needed to activate network members is itself a scarce resource.

The response has been to maintain informal regional contact through WhatsApp groups while focusing staff capacity on global programmes. This approach "dilutes asymmetries" — members are not humiliated if their region cannot organise as actively as others.

### Specific Elements
- **Regional hubs attempted**: Latin America, Africa, Europe, Asia
- **Challenge**: Even funded hub leaders could not sustain engagement
- **Current approach**: Global focus + informal regional WhatsApp groups
- **Underlying issue**: Network participation is work — members have competing priorities
- **Giovanni's insight**: Personal issues (e.g., hub leader's family health) can affect entire regions
- **Tension**: Between staff-driven agenda and member-driven initiative

### Key Quotations
- "The great virtue of a network… is when you have members meeting and working from themselves and this network creates things that it's not connected to the main node anymore." (Clara)
- "Being part of the network, it's also work. So if you don't see value or something that you can use immediately, you might not interact as much." (Clara)
- "There wasn't much to show from it. Because either the hub leader just wasn't able to organize much, or participants just weren't very engaged." (Nikhil)
- "The global scope dilutes a little bit these asymmetries… nobody remains humiliated for what is not there." (Giovanni)

## Theme 5: Equity, Differential Pay, and the North-South Funding Divide

### Summary
A significant internal governance debate centres on how to pay members and contractors fairly across vastly different economies. People Powered moved from a flat rate to a differentiated model based on cost of living, but the decision was extremely split among members — reflecting a deep and unresolved tension between equity (paying according to need) and market reality (paying according to local professional rates).

### Detailed Explanation
The original model paid a flat rate to all contractors. But staff salaries were already differentiated based on cost of living: "Nikhil earns more than me. I'm in Brazil. And I'm a director, he's a manager. And that's not a complaint. He lives in one of the most expensive cities in the world."

When this logic was extended to member fees (for mentors, contractors), the organisation faced a dilemma. Mentors from expensive countries (UK, US) would quit because the flat rate was too low for them. Meanwhile, members from lower-cost countries received rates that felt generous locally but still below the organisation's own standards of equity.

The policy was debated and voted on through People Powered Decides. The vote produced three options with virtually equal support — a deeply split decision that left the organisation with imperfect buy-in. Nikhil notes they reframed the policy by making the lowest cost-of-living band the base rate, with adjustments added on top, but "I don't know how much of a difference that makes."

### Specific Elements
- **Original model**: Flat rate for all contractors
- **Problem**: Mentors from expensive countries quit; inequity perceived
- **New model**: Cost-of-living adjusted rates with a cap
- **Decision process**: Voted through People Powered Decides
- **Result**: Extremely split vote (3 options, nearly equal support)
- **Ongoing tension**: Psychological framing vs. material difference
- **Budget constraint**: Equity policies must be balanced with overall resources

### Key Quotations
- "If I got the same salary that [Nikhil] gets, I would be a queen here. And he is not a king where he is with his salary." (Clara)
- "The mentors that were in the program that were from countries that were more expensive, they quit." (Clara)
- "The voting result was extremely split. There were three options, and they almost got the exact same amount of votes for each option." (Nikhil)
- "We changed it to be that the lowest cost of living is the base rate. And then you're just adding like a cost of living adjustment on top." (Nikhil)

## Theme 6: Playing "Connective Tissue" — Coalition Building Beyond the Network

### Summary
People Powered has increasingly taken on a role beyond its own network: acting as "connective tissue" between organisations working on different aspects of democracy. The Democracy Narratives Alliance (DNA) exemplifies this — a coalition to change how people think about democracy, moving beyond the "tip of the iceberg" focus on elections toward a richer understanding of democratic participation. This role creates tension between programme delivery and strategic coalition-building.

### Detailed Explanation
Clara describes how People Powered has stepped beyond its participatory democracy expertise to convene organisations working on broader democracy challenges. The DNA brings together organisations focused on youth engagement, behaviour change, systems change, and government reform — all united by a recognition that "democracy is more than elections" and that the pro-democracy field needs to create emotionally engaging narratives, not just critique authoritarian populism.

This coalition work is difficult to fund: "we don't get as much fund to think about what is happening in the world." It exists alongside the organisation's core programmes and competes for limited staff capacity. But Clara sees it as essential: "it's beyond even the mission that People Powered has. But we think it's very important because what we want with it is just change how people think about democracy."

Giovanni observes that having too many levels (organisation, network, alliances) can cause participants to disengage — "the loser wants to lose somehow." Clara acknowledges this challenge: "how network members engage with that beyond commitments within the consortium… it's very challenging."

### Specific Elements
- **DNA**: Democracy Narratives Alliance — changing mental models about democracy
- **Previous coalition**: SDG priority-setting for participation (with Brookings, Rockefeller)
- **Role**: Convening organisations across the democracy ecosystem
- **Challenge**: Coalition work is hard to fund
- **Tension**: Programme delivery vs. strategic thinking and coalition-building
- **Narrative shift**: From "democracy is threatened" to "democracy is super cool"
- **Long-term goal**: Change how people imagine democracy — beyond elections

### Key Quotations
- "The coalition space is a good opportunity for that, because that's where you have the chance to listen from other folks from other parts of the democracy universe." (Clara)
- "Democracy is not just about that [elections]. It's about other practices… How can we make people expand imagination? How can we expand their mental models?" (Clara)
- "We want to make sure that we're not just focusing on programs that are not really making a big impact and figuring out how can we connect with funders and partners that are really in the big leagues." (Nikhil)
- "We don't want to stop on the think. DNA, it's pretty much at that stage, but we thought a lot, we had many discussions, now we need to do it." (Clara)
