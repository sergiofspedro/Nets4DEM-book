## Interview: Stefano Stortone — BIPART / OGP / World Atlas of Participatory Democracy

---

### Theme 1: The Sustainability Paradox of Networking

**Summary**  
Stortone describes a fundamental paradox: participation in networks is essential for visibility, project access, and professional relevance, but it requires time and resources that small organisations simply do not have. For BIPART — a small social enterprise — networking became an unaffordable luxury, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of exclusion.

**Detailed explanation**  
Stortone explains that when he was at university or working as a consultant, he could attend international meetings, travel, and maintain relationships because he had a stable income and flexibility. Since founding BIPART, which struggles with sustainability, he has "essentially cut all ties" because he no longer has the time or resources for networking. This creates a vicious circle: networking is essential for being part of projects, for keeping people aware that you exist and are active, but if you cannot afford to network, you disappear from the field. He observes this directly: after two years away from networks, he finds himself "only in the world of Erasmus Plus where there is a continuity of projects that call for other projects," while in the broader participatory democracy world he is "a bit out — and it is not by choice."

He clarifies that most of his time is dedicated to making BIPART a sustainable reality. BIPART was deliberately founded as a social enterprise to "escape the logic of the third sector that survives on public funds and private financing" — but this independence comes at the cost of network participation.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Actors/Context:** BIPART as a small Italian social enterprise; the Erasmus Plus project cycle as a residual network lifeline.
- **Practices:** Selection of networking opportunities based on scarce time; the need to "carve out" time for relationships.
- **Problems:** Networking as an unpaid overhead cost that small actors cannot afford; the danger of becoming invisible ("people forget what you do").
- **Contradictions/Nuances:** Stortone frames this as a structural problem of the field, not a personal choice. He would like to participate but cannot.

**Key quotations**  
> "Since Bipart was born, which still struggles with the issue of sustainability, I have essentially cut all ties because I no longer have the time or resources to go networking, and this is a dog chasing its tail because networking in our world is essential."

> "If you don't network, people forget what you do, and above all, it means that you are no longer doing anything."

### Theme 2: Governance Tensions — Participatory Ideals vs. Organisational Structure

**Summary**  
Stortone's central critique of networks like People Power is that their formal organisational structure (board, steering committee, president, legal responsibilities) is inherently centralised and representative, which clashes with the informal, participatory, and horizontal dynamics that members expect. This structural contradiction produced his disillusionment and withdrawal.

**Detailed explanation**  
Stortone argues that any organisation with a legal structure — "a base, a steering committee and a president, a director with legal responsibility" — is inevitably a centralised organisation. The rules of governance create decision-making moments that clash with informal participatory expectations. He experienced this directly in People Power: he co-designed proposals, saw them voted on democratically, but then found them implemented in ways different from how they were conceived, without adequate accountability back to the original proposers. This created what he calls "a conflict of interest as big as a house" — he was simultaneously a proposal-maker, a voter, and a potential implementer of the same proposal, with resources at stake.

The deeper structural problem is that networks claim horizontality but operate through vertical accountability structures. He draws a parallel with participatory processes in general: "Most participatory processes are democratic in their decision-making... but then how it develops is the traditional bureaucracy." Voting on keywords is not the same as controlling implementation.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Actors/Context:** People Power's governance structure; the Resource Center proposal; the platform mapping proposal.
- **Practices:** Co-design processes that were undermined during implementation; voting that lacked accountability to originators.
- **Problems:** The gap between democratic input and bureaucratic execution; conflict of interest when proposers also bid for implementation.
- **Contradictions/Nuances:** Stortone acknowledges that the tension is not about bad faith but about structural design: "It's not that someone like People Power sees bad faith in it, it's simply a vision."

**Key quotations**  
> "Inevitably that organization is a centralized organization and therefore the rules somehow lead to decision-making moments that... clashed with the informal moments of participation of the base."

> "A conflict of interest as big as a house was created, because the resources... I had designed it and I had also proposed creating the platform... and then I left it both for a conflict of interest issue and above all for the annoyance."

### Theme 3: The Extractivist Logic of Collective Resources

**Summary**  
A recurring concern in the interview is the risk that networks, despite their participatory rhetoric, enable extractivist dynamics: collectively produced ideas and projects are captured by well-resourced actors who benefit disproportionately, while the community that generated them receives little or nothing in return.

**Detailed explanation**  
Stortone and Giovanni jointly recall a letter they co-wrote with Diana Dyer at the founding of People Power, which posed the question: what "antibodies" exist to prevent a situation where a project built collectively by a community becomes "a comparative advantage for earning money" for one individual or organisation? The concern was that well-funded actors (especially American organisations, given the professionalised nature of the US third sector) could absorb community-generated projects, implement them with their own resources, and capture the benefits — jobs, contracts, visibility — without meaningful reciprocity.

Stortone frames this as a matter of "giving back" that must become structural, not voluntary. He notes that in OGP he raised the budget issue explicitly: "this is a project whose budget goes almost entirely to development of the platform, so you are aware and for my part I also take on a bit of the burden of giving back, but the very concept of giving back must become structural."

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Actors/Context:** The founding letter of People Power (Stortone, Giovanni, Diana Dyer); Paolo Spada's experience with project appropriation; American third-sector professionalisation.
- **Practices:** Co-designed projects being implemented unilaterally; the "I do, I exploit, I steal" pattern.
- **Problems:** The absence of structural mechanisms for reciprocity when collective work generates individual gain.
- **Contradictions/Nuances:** The extractivist dynamic is not necessarily malicious — it emerges from different conceptions of the third sector (professionalised vs. communitarian).

**Key quotations**  
> "If a community project suddenly becomes a comparative advantage for earning money... what antibodies... have those who win a project built collectively but financed by an individual, and what do they give back to the community?"

> "The very concept of giving back must become structural."

### Theme 4: The Crisis of Representativeness in Democratic Innovation

**Summary**  
Stortone argues that participatory and deliberative theories systematically sideline the issue of representativeness — the very foundation on which modern democracy was built. This creates a legitimacy deficit because participatory processes implicitly depend on representative institutions while simultaneously critiquing them, without resolving the tension.

**Detailed explanation**  
In Stortone's analysis, participatory and deliberative processes do not question the concept of representativeness; they simply "accept that representativeness lies elsewhere" — in elected bodies, in formal institutions. But this creates a distortion: participatory processes claim legitimacy without having the democratic mandate that representation provides. He sees this as "the foundation that has fostered democracy's growth worldwide" being neglected.

He extends this critique to the entire architecture of democratic innovation: civil society "lacks strong democratic legitimacy and therefore must somehow submit to those who do have it" — political parties and elected bodies. Participatory budgeting, which he admires, works precisely because it combines "the representativeness of the idea with the social dimension that is somehow expressed by representativeness." But in general, the field has not resolved the relationship between participation and representation.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Actors/Context:** OGP Italy's participation documents; the relationship between civil society and political parties.
- **Practices:** Participatory budgeting as a rare successful synthesis of representation and participation.
- **Problems:** Participatory processes that lack decision-making power become mere consultation; the legitimacy gap between participatory claims and representative mandates.
- **Contradictions/Nuances:** He calls himself "radical" on this point — "out of sadness, out of desperation."

**Key quotations**  
> "In all participatory and deliberative theories, the issue of representativeness is practically sidelined, when in reality it is the foundation that has fostered democracy's growth worldwide."

> "Civil society lacks strong democratic legitimacy and therefore must somehow submit to those who do have it."

### Theme 5: The Need for a New Paradigm — Beyond Network Informality and Formality

**Summary**  
Stortone identifies a fundamental gap: there is no available paradigmatic model for democratic governance that escapes the dichotomy between informal networks (with their problems of power and exclusion) and formal organisations (which replicate representative democracy's flaws). He calls for a reconceptualisation that spans political science, economics, and social theory.

**Detailed explanation**  
He argues that both extremes are problematic. Informal networks have all the problems of social movements — hidden power relations, lack of accountability, elite capture through informality. Formal networks, when structured, reproduce the same governance patterns as representative democracy: a summit, a board, a self-selecting elite. He finds "no middle ground between these two extremes."

He illustrates this with his personal trajectory: the World Social Forum, the Lilliput network, Indymedia, the Five Star Movement, the New Municipality network, the OEDP board, OGP's self-selection mechanisms. None offered a genuinely different model. In every case, "the networks inevitably clash over a reproduction of the same flaws."

He contrasts this with historical moments of genuine paradigm change: participatory budgeting in Brazil arose from liberation theology and Paolo Freire's paradigm; modern democracy arose from the French Revolution's radical questioning of legitimacy. Today, he argues, "what's missing is someone, something circulating within the networks and somehow leading the networks themselves to question all the paradigms that today render the network static."

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Actors/Context:** World Social Forum; Lilliput network; Indymedia; Five Star Movement/Beppe Grillo; New Municipality network; OEDP board; OGP forum; Civil Economy meetings in Florence; Zamagni; Edgar Morin.
- **Practices:** Theoretical inability to move beyond formality/informality dichotomy; absence of "holistic or systemic thinking."
- **Problems:** No organisation or network is asking the fundamental question: "Isn't it that we need to change the way democracy works in order for democracy to work?"
- **Contradictions/Nuances:** Stortone is working on his own model ("Idea in Fuga") but acknowledges it is difficult to model, communicate, and implement.

**Key quotations**  
> "I can't find a middle ground between these two extremes, so if the networks are informal, they have all the problems of informality... if the networks are structured in some way, there is always a summit."

> "What's missing today is someone, something circulating within the networks and somehow leading the networks themselves to question all the paradigms that today render the network static."

> "If you find someone who's asking this question [whether we need to change how democracy works for democracy to work], please share it with me, because I haven't found one yet."

### Theme 6: BIPART's Paradox — A Social Enterprise with Concentrated Power

**Summary**  
Stortone explicitly acknowledges a personal paradox: he runs BIPART as a social enterprise in which he holds the majority of capital and therefore absolute decision-making power. This concentrated authority contradicts his advocacy for democratic participation, but he sees it as a necessary condition for survival and experimentation outside traditional funding dependency.

**Detailed explanation**  
BIPART was founded as a social enterprise deliberately to break free from the third-sector dependency on public grants and private financing. However, this model concentrates power in Stortone's hands. He recognises the paradox: "I have a social capital enterprise in which I hold the majority of the capital, therefore I have absolute decision-making power over this company, which is a paradox." But he also argues that this concentration is what allows him to experiment with democratic governance models — precisely because he is not accountable to a board, funders, or a participatory base in his daily operations.

The paradox reflects the larger structural problem: you cannot experiment with radically democratic governance if you are constantly in survival mode. The autonomy that concentrated ownership provides is the precondition for the very experimentation that networks, trapped in their own governance constraints, cannot undertake.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Actors/Context:** BIPART's legal structure as a social enterprise; Stortone's majority ownership.
- **Practices:** Using concentrated power to fund research and platform development; the Swiss foundation case as a contrasting example.
- **Problems:** The tension between democratic values and hierarchical organisational necessity.
- **Contradictions/Nuances:** Stortone does not resolve this paradox — he names it openly as a limitation and a strategic choice.

**Key quotations**  
> "I have a social capital enterprise in which I hold the majority of the capital, therefore I have absolute decision-making power over this company, which is a paradox."

> "If I am in a democratic network context, experimenting with these visions that I have... I will not be able to do them neither here nor in three centuries, because networks have different dynamics."

### Theme 7: Critique of Decidim's Foundational Paradigm

**Summary**  
Stortone offers a pointed critique of Decidim as a platform built on a paradigm that does not question institutional democracy. Rather than creating new forms of democratic participation, Decidim supports and reinforces existing democratic structures, which Stortone believes gives it "no future."

**Detailed explanation**  
He argues that Decidim's core architecture embeds a specific democratic philosophy: "in the software code there is the code of democracy." The problem, in his view, is that Decidim "is born on a paradigm that doesn't question institutional democracy." It creates a platform to support current democracy rather than to create new forms of democracy. This is a foundational limitation that constrains what the platform can achieve, regardless of how well it is implemented or how many people use it.

This critique aligns with his broader argument about the absence of paradigmatic questioning in the field. Decidim, for all its openness and participatory design, reproduces the same assumptions about what democracy is and how it should work.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Actors/Context:** Decidim as a platform; the broader Decidim ecosystem.
- **Practices:** Software as an embodiment of democratic theory; the limitations of platforms that do not question underlying paradigms.
- **Problems:** Platforms that support existing institutions rather than enabling institutional transformation.
- **Contradictions/Nuances:** Stortone calls this "my vision, a bit of a madman walking around naked in the streets" — acknowledging it is a radical minority position.

**Key quotations**  
> "Decidim is born on a paradigm that doesn't question institutional democracy... it doesn't create a platform to create new forms of democracy but creates a platform to support current democracy."

> "That in my opinion makes Decidim a platform with no future."

### Theme 8: Networks as Closed Circuits — Inclusion, Exclusion, and Power

**Summary**  
Stortone argues that despite the rhetoric of openness, all networks — whether formal or informal — operate as closed circuits. They have rules, customs, habits, and power relations that determine who is included, who is marginalised, and how resources are distributed. Openness is a function of size and visibility, not of democratic design.

**Detailed explanation**  
He explicitly states that "the openness of a network depends on how large the network is," referencing Granovetter's network analysis. Larger networks have more visibility and more opportunities, but they also make it harder to include everyone meaningfully. He argues that power redistribution within a network "cannot be entrusted to informality" — it must be established by rules designed upstream. But since those rules are themselves created by centralised processes, "the networks themselves are inevitably centralized and tend to exclude a little or include with somewhat flawed logic."

He further argues that the decision to join a large network is never purely altruistic: "It's a small reality, saying I'm joining a large network because I have more opportunities and you're no longer doing it just for the common good." The line between serving the common good and self-preservation becomes blurred, even internally for the participant.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Actors/Context:** Granovetter's network theory; the general phenomenon of network hubs and distributed networks.
- **Practices:** Marketing and visibility as mechanisms of inclusion/exclusion; the blurring of common good and self-interest.
- **Problems:** Informal rules create unaccountable power structures; formal rules reproduce centralisation; the difficulty of distinguishing altruistic from instrumental participation.
- **Contradictions/Nuances:** Stortone acknowledges this applies "to the internal governance of a network, and for networks of networks, and for relationships between networks."

**Key quotations**  
> "The redistribution of power within a network... cannot be entrusted to informality. It must be entrusted to rules established upstream. And since the rules established upstream are centralized, the networks themselves are inevitably centralized."

> "The bigger we are, the more visibility we have, but the bigger we are, the more difficult it is to include everyone."

### Theme 9: The Ambivalence of Networks — Utilitarian Instrument vs. Value Space

**Summary**  
The interview surfaces a deep ambivalence about what networks are for. Giovanni articulates networks as spaces with both utilitarian and value-sharing dimensions; Stortone takes a more radical position, refusing to instrumentalise relationships even when this limits his opportunities. This tension between instrumental and expressive uses of networks remains unresolved.

**Detailed explanation**  
Giovanni describes networks as having two components: "networking as a productive aspect, but also as an ideal space where you, as a person, perhaps feel at ease, make friends." He later elaborates a distinction between "relational capital" (maintaining relationships, potentially for utilitarian purposes) and "social capital" (personal growth, shared values). Stortone responds with a personal rule: he "never use[s] friends" for work purposes. He refuses to do "any kind of advertising" and explicitly does not participate in tenders where friendships might give him an advantage. "I go there to have relationships because that relationship satisfies me in itself, not because it brings other things."

However, he acknowledges that these relationships "bring them [benefits] without wanting to" — someone calls him, an opportunity arises. This creates what Giovanni calls an "objective ambiguity" that is "the absolute foundation of this world of relationships." Stortone accepts this ambiguity intellectually but maintains his personal refusal to instrumentalise relationships, even at the cost of opportunities.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Actors/Context:** Stortone's personal ethics; Giovanni's conceptual distinction between relational and social capital.
- **Practices:** Refusal to use friendships for work advantage; opening one's home to rebalance hospitality; maintaining a "pure idea" of relationships.
- **Problems:** The impossibility of fully separating instrumental and expressive dimensions in practice; the cost of maintaining ethical purity.
- **Contradictions/Nuances:** Stortone acknowledges the ambiguity and accepts it as structural, not resolvable at the individual level.

**Key quotations**  
> "I always maintain a rule which is never to use friends... In the world of work, I've never used my friendships to do anything, at most to share a vision."

> "This refusal to do any kind of advertising... keeps me with a basically pure idea that I go there to have relationships because that relationship satisfies me in itself."

> "This ambiguity is probably the absolute foundation of this world of relationships."

### Theme 10: OGP as a Different Opportunity — Aligning Vision with Structure

**Summary**  
Stortone found in OGP (Open Government Partnership) a more productive context than People Power because it offered an opportunity to contribute at the level of models and procedures — his core competence — without the same governance contradictions that frustrated him in other networks.

**Detailed explanation**  
He explains that before the OGP opportunity arose, he participated "with half an ear and half an eye" because he lacked the time. But when a concrete project aligned with his vision emerged — one where he could contribute "something procedurally, model-wise, or vision-wise" — he fully engaged. The key difference was that the OGP context allowed him to work on a platform development project whose budget went "almost entirely to development," and where he could explicitly negotiate the giving-back mechanism upfront.

He contrasts this with People Power: "If I have to participate with the model and visions I'm not working to promote, for me it's a waste of time." The OGP opportunity emerged because of his prior research activity, funded by a Swiss foundation, which gave him the conceptual groundwork ready when OGP "opened its arms to that project hypothesis."

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Actors/Context:** OGP (Open Government Partnership); the Swiss foundation (Botnar); the platform development project.
- **Practices:** Timing and readiness — having a well-developed concept when opportunity appears; negotiating structural reciprocity upfront.
- **Problems:** The difficulty of finding spaces where one's vision and the network's needs genuinely overlap.
- **Contradictions/Nuances:** Even in OGP, the giving-back question remains unresolved: "the very concept of giving back must become structural."

**Key quotations**  
> "If there's an opportunity where I can contribute something procedurally, model-wise, or vision-wise, I'll do it. Otherwise, if I have to participate with the model and visions I'm not working to promote, for me it's a waste of time."

> "I took all the time in the world, because I saw an overlap of interests in good faith."

### Theme 11 [Implicit]: Networks, Survival, and the Third Sector's Structural Dependency

**Summary**  
Throughout the interview, an implicit theme is that the entire logic of networks, the third sector, and civil society operates within a structure of dependency on external resources (public funds, foundations, institutional recognition). This dependency constrains what networks can democratically achieve and forces organisations into competition disguised as cooperation.

**Detailed explanation**  
Stortone frames this as a "paradigmatic" issue: "the third sector is third always depends on someone else in charge." Civil society lacks autonomous resources and democratic legitimacy; it must seek both from institutions, foundations, or markets. This dependency shapes every dimension of network life: who can participate (those with resources), what gets funded (projects aligned with funder priorities), and how governance works (organisations must maintain legal structures that are inherently representative, not participatory).

The "exit from the survival state" is identified by Vittorio as the key that would unlock genuine democratic experimentation. Stortone agrees: the constant pressure of survival creates "all this chaos, all this ambivalence." Until organisations have the material security to experiment with governance, they will reproduce the same patterns.

He also notes a material paradox: "Soon, we'll find ourselves having more money for travel and meetings and training than for participatory processes" — networking activities are increasingly better funded than the democratic practices they are supposed to support.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Actors/Context:** The third sector overall; foundations like Botnar; the shift in funding toward networking activities.
- **Practices:** Survival-driven governance choices; competition for grants disguised as network cooperation.
- **Problems:** Dependency as a structural constraint on democratic experimentation; funding that prioritises networking over practice.
- **Contradictions/Nuances:** Stortone does not claim to have resolved this — BIPART's social enterprise model is his attempt, but it creates its own paradoxes.

**Key quotations**  
> "The fact that the third sector is third always depends on someone else in charge, until they're not there, and that's why I like participatory budgeting."

> "Soon, we'll find ourselves having more money for travel and meetings and training than for participatory processes."

## Synthesis

Stefano Stortone's interview offers a structurally critical perspective on networks for democratic participation. His analysis is distinctive for its refusal to accept the participatory field's self-understanding at face value. He identifies deep contradictions:

1. **Material constraints** undermine participatory ideals — those who cannot afford to network are excluded from the very spaces meant to be inclusive.
2. **Governance structures** reproduce the representative democracy that participatory processes claim to transcend, creating conflicts between formal accountability and informal participation.
3. **Resource flows** create extractivist dynamics where collectively generated projects benefit well-resourced actors.
4. **The representativeness question** is systematically evaded by the field, creating a legitimacy gap.
5. **Paradigmatic stagnation** means that neither informal networks nor formal organisations offer a genuinely new model of democratic governance.

His position is not merely critical: through BIPART, his work with OGP, and his ongoing theoretical development ("Idea in Fuga"), he is attempting to build alternatives. But he is candid about the paradoxes this entails — including the concentration of power in his own enterprise and the unresolved tension between network participation and organisational survival.

The interview contributes to the book's analytical framework by showing that **democratic intensification** cannot be understood solely as a matter of scaling practices or building connections. It requires confronting the structural conditions — material, legal, paradigmatic — that constrain what networks can be and do. Stortone's voice is that of a practitioner-theorist who has experienced networks from the inside as activist, researcher, and entrepreneur, and whose disillusionment is not cynicism but a demand for more fundamental transformation.
