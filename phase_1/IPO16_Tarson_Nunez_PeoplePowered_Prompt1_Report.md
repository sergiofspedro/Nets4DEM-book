# Prompt 1 Report — IPO16: Tarson Nuñez (People Powered)

**Interviewee:** Tarson Nuñez — Brazilian political scientist, researcher, former public administrator in Rio Grande do Sul, former member of the People Powered board.  
**Case study:** People Powered (with contributions also on OIDP).  
**Date of interview:** Not specified.  
**Length:** ~58 minutes.

---

## Theme 1 — Origins and Entry into People Powered: Serendipity and Prior Connections

**Summary**  
Tarson discovered People Powered by chance through a Facebook post by Josh Lerner calling for participants in a new hub for participatory democracy. However, this chance encounter was sustained by a pre-existing network of relationships: he already knew the founding group from participatory budgeting conferences in the United States, where he had participated as a state government official from Rio Grande do Sul. His entry thus combines digital serendipity with long-established personal and professional ties.

**Detailed explanation**  
The interviewee describes his first contact with People Powered as "totally by chance" — he saw a Facebook post by Josh Lerner inviting people to join a new initiative. He wrote to them immediately, offering his experience with participatory budgeting in Porto Alegre. But the chance nature of this discovery is qualified by the fact that he already knew the key people behind the initiative. Between 2012 and 2013, while working at the state government of Rio Grande do Sul, Tarson had attended two or three conferences organised by the Participatory Budgeting Project (PBP) in the United States (New York, Chicago, Boston). Those conferences made a strong impression on him because of their commitment to including grassroots members alongside academics and government officials — a feature he greatly admired. Josh Lerner and Mike Menser had first encountered participatory budgeting at the World Social Forum in Porto Alegre in 2005, creating a direct biographical link between Porto Alegre and the birth of People Powered. Tarson frames his entry as driven by a long-standing interest in international connections that go "bottom up," in contrast to the government-oriented international networks he had previously experienced (such as UCLG/CGLU).

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Platform:** Facebook (the post by Josh Lerner).
- **Events:** Participatory Budgeting Project conferences in New York, Chicago, and Boston (2012–2013).
- **Key actors:** Josh Lerner, Mike Menser.
- **Places:** Porto Alegre, Rio Grande do Sul (Brazil); United States.
- **Prior experience:** Organisational committees of the World Social Forum since 2001; international relations work at the state government.
- **Organisational link:** The World Social Forum in Porto Alegre (2005) as the place where Lerner and Menser first encountered participatory budgeting.

**Key quotations**
> "My first contact was totally by chance and in some ways it shows something about the importance of the use of the information technology as a tool to spread up initiatives."

> "I already knew the guys, admired their work, especially their efforts to include in their discussions with protagonists the grassroots members."

> "To be in a group and a political initiative that works with democracy from the bottom up and from the grassroots was very appealing to me."

## Theme 2 — Organisational Culture: Practising Democracy Internally

**Summary**  
For Tarson, People Powered's most valuable feature is its systematic effort to align internal organisational practices with its democratic values. He highlights concrete measures: inclusive meeting methodologies, childcare provision at events, the integration of rational and subjective dimensions, and a deliberate levelling of hierarchies between powerful institutions and grassroots members. This is not merely rhetorical but embedded in daily operations.

**Detailed explanation**  
The interviewee emphasises that People Powered genuinely tries to "practice what they say." He describes a methodology where everyone is encouraged to speak, where personal and subjective dimensions are given space alongside intellectual and institutional ones. He recalls with particular appreciation that at the first meeting he attended in Edinburgh, there was a dedicated space for children, enabling mothers who otherwise could not attend to participate. This seemingly small detail exemplifies a broader philosophy: creating conditions for genuine inclusion. The organisation attempts to "level the field" among participants — whether from the World Bank or a grassroots movement — acknowledging that power differences exist but deliberately working to mitigate them. Tarson interprets this as a break from the tradition of Northern organisations that address democracy only at the "theoretical and conceptual level" without attending to the "day-to-day aspects of democracy, like equality, like transparency." He links this to a "more integral approach on politics" that integrates rational, subjective, and existential dimensions of the individual.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Practice:** Childcare provision at the Edinburgh meeting.
- **Methodology:** Icebreaking, check-in rounds ("what are the good things that you've done this week"), stimulating everyone to speak.
- **Value:** Equality between members regardless of institutional power; gender balance; protagonism of Global South members.
- **Tension:** Tarson acknowledges that power differences are "always present" — the levelling is a deliberate effort, not a natural state.

**Key quotations**
> "The biggest value of People Powered is that it really tries to transform this theoretical decolonial approach into practice, day-to-day practices."

> "Their approach breaks with the tradition of most of the organizations that emerged in the north, that deals with participatory democracy without, only at the theoretical level, only at the conceptual level, but didn't give much attention to the day-to-day aspects of democracy."

> "We develop a relationship that is not only based on functional aspects or rational aspects of intellectual aspects, but also in an existential dimension."

## Theme 3 — The Post-Colonial and Decolonial Commitment: Between Sincerity and Paradox

**Summary**  
Tarson acknowledges People Powered's genuine effort to adopt a post-colonial or decolonial approach, both intellectually and organisationally — by including members from Africa, Asia, and Latin America and giving them protagonism. However, he also reflects on the irony of decoloniality arriving in the Global South from the North, and points to the structural limits of this commitment given the concentration of funding and institutional power in the North.

**Detailed explanation**  
The interviewee offers a nuanced perspective. On one hand, he values the decolonial turn as a "mind changing" development among first-world intellectuals who are trying to see the world beyond the Enlightenment illusion that "civilization came from Europe to the rest of the world." He recognizes People Powered's "solid and effective commitment" to this approach in organisational terms: the concern for geographic balance, the effort to give voice to Global South members. On the other hand, he observes a structural irony: in Brazil, decolonial theory often arrives from the North rather than emerging from indigenous or local thought. He notes that Brazil already had its own "decolonial avant la lettre" thinkers — Paulo Freire, Darcy Ribeiro, Milton Santos — who reframed Northern knowledge from a Southern perspective. He is not idealistic: the organisational core and especially the funding of People Powered come mainly from the North, meaning power is still concentrated (even if not deliberately) with "our North American colleagues." He sees this as an acknowledged problem that the organisation tries to address, but structural asymmetries remain difficult to overcome.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Thinkers:** Paulo Freire, Darcy Ribeiro, Milton Santos (Brazilian decolonial avant la lettre).
- **Practice:** Geographic balance in membership; protagonism of Global South members.
- **Structural constraint:** Funding from the North; concentration of power with North American colleagues.
- **Nuance:** Decolonial as an "ironic" concept when imported from the colonisers.

**Key quotations**
> "It's like rather ironic that the colonizers now are trying to be decolonial."

> "I think that in the case of People Powered, there's a very solid and effective commitment with this post-colonial approach, not only in theoretical terms, but also in organizational terms."

> "The organizational core, and especially the funding of PP, comes mainly from the north. And in many senses, the power is still concentrated, even though not deliberately."

## Theme 4 — North-South Asymmetries in Knowledge Production

**Summary**  
Tarson reflects on an often-overlooked dimension of the North-South divide: the unequal capacity to produce systematic theoretical knowledge. The Global South's "greatest asset is its experience and its struggles," but activists and scholars in the South lack the time, funding, and institutional support to produce the kind of codified theory that circulates internationally. This gap is not easily bridged.

**Detailed explanation**  
The interviewee explains that formulations from the South are "much more fragmented and less abundant" than those from the North. There are more researchers, more funding, more time for writing and theorising in the North. In the Global South, especially in Latin America, indigenous and popular movements are "more concerned with their own survival and their own struggles." The South's contribution is experiential and practical rather than theoretical and systematic. Tarson does not see this gap being filled in the near future, because the conditions are not favourable — the struggle to defend democracy and resist the far right consumes most energy. He frames this as a structural limitation to the decolonial project: even when Northern organisations sincerely want to include Southern voices, the Southern contribution tends to be less codified and therefore less easily integrated into international discourse.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Contrast:** Abundant Northern theory vs. fragmented Southern formulations.
- **Root cause:** Lack of time, funding, and institutional support in the Global South.
- **Case:** Brazil's current struggle against the far right as a drain on intellectual production.
- **Exception:** China and East Asia are noted as possible exceptions to this pattern.
- **Implicit theme:** The epistemological challenge for networks like People Powered — how to genuinely value experiential knowledge alongside academic theorising.

**Key quotations**
> "Our greatest asset is our experience and our struggles. And this, I think, is the reason that we don't have lots of literature and formulation coming from the south."

> "We're now in a much more vital struggle to defend democracy and avoid the extreme right Trumpist candidate to be president here."

## Theme 5 — From Democratic Deepening to Democratic Defence: A Paradigm Shift

**Summary**  
Tarson identifies a profound change in the political environment since People Powered was founded: the shift from an era when democracy was mainstream and the organisation could focus on deepening it, to a defensive moment where even liberal democracy is under threat. This shift has reshaped the organisation's internal discussions and its strategic priorities.

**Detailed explanation**  
The interviewee contrasts two moments. In the early years of the hub, democracy was "still mainstream" — there was no serious questioning of it, and the threats we see today were not as visible. The mood was optimistic: "we already have liberal democracy and we are going beyond that." People Powered was building new initiatives within a stable framework. Over time, however, the global political climate changed dramatically. Traditional liberal democracy proved less stable than assumed. The organisation's discussions turned from "how to deepen democracy to how to defend democracy." Tarson uses a striking metaphor: the staircase that the organisation was climbing has become a "sliding thing" — now they are grabbing on just to stay in place. This defensive turn is reflected in new topics of discussion: how to defend democracy from right-wing populism, how to build pro-democracy narratives. The very subject of the organisation's conversations tells the story of the times.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Chronology:** Early years (optimistic, deepening) → later years (defensive, protecting liberal democracy).
- **Shift in discourse:** From "going beyond liberal democracy" to "defending democracy."
- **New topics:** Right-wing populism, pro-democracy narratives.
- **Metaphor:** The "sliding staircase."

**Key quotations**
> "Our discussions turned from how to deepen democracy to how to defend democracy. And this is a huge change because when we started, the idea was that we were on like a consolidated place and then we can go up the stairs little by little. And now we see that the stairs is like a sliding thing that we are going down and we are grabbing to like stay on the same place."

## Theme 6 — The Brazilian Context: Democratic Erosion and Its Consequences for Participation

**Summary**  
Tarson's personal involvement in People Powered has been shaped by the deterioration of Brazil's political environment. After the impeachment of Dilma Rousseff, the Temer and Bolsonaro governments, and the ongoing struggle against the far right, his capacity to engage with participatory democracy work has been severely reduced. The Brazilian case illustrates how democratic backsliding at the national level directly weakens participation in transnational networks.

**Detailed explanation**  
The interviewee offers a first-hand account of how democratic erosion affects activists. In Brazil, the post-2016 period — the "coup that overthrew president Dilma," the Temer government, and the Bolsonaro years — fundamentally changed the political environment. Most of his energy is now consumed by the struggle to "make liberal democracy survive in the country" and to "prevent the right-wing fascists to come back to power." His participation in People Powered has become "very, very limited" — he tries to stay in tune, attend some meetings, help where possible, but cannot contribute new experiences or ideas because Brazil is in a "stalemate." He contrasts his earlier involvement in the mentorship programme, when he was much more active, with his current passive role. Nevertheless, he notes that some participatory processes still exist at the federal level, such as the National Conferences on Culture and on Cities.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Key events:** Impeachment of Dilma Rousseff (2016); Temer government; Bolsonaro government.
- **Current threat:** "Right-wing fascists" attempting to return to power.
- **Participatory processes still active:** National Conference of Culture, National Conference of Cities.
- **Personal consequence:** Shift from active mentorship role to passive observer.
- **Implicit theme:** The vulnerability of transnational participation networks to national-level political crises — when members are fighting for basic democratic survival at home, their contribution to international organisations necessarily shrinks.

**Key quotations**
> "After the coup that overthrew the president Dilma and the years of the Temer government and the years of Bolsonaro government, the political environment in Brazil changed dramatically."

> "My participation in people power today is very, very limited because I only managed to be in tune with what is happening, participate in some of the meetings and trying to learn, trying to help the organization."

> "It's very difficult today to like regain initiative in terms of building participatory democracy in Brazil."

## Theme 7 — Tension between Technical and Political Approaches

**Summary**  
Tarson identifies a significant internal tension within People Powered — and, by extension, within the broader field of participatory and deliberative democracy: the risk of operating as a "technical advisor" disconnected from the political environments in which initiatives are embedded. He calls for a more openly political discussion within the organisation.

**Detailed explanation**  
The interviewee expresses a concern that much of the work on participatory democracy — including within People Powered — treats democratic methods as a "recipe" or a "methodology" that can be applied regardless of the political context. He observes that deliberative processes such as mini-publics often run "in parallel with the current political processes" without any reflection on whether they can actually affect real political outcomes. This creates a paradox: an organisation dedicated to democracy sometimes works in a way that is "much more technical than political." He argues that the organisation cannot ignore the fact that there are now many governments that "really don't like democracy at all" — not even liberal democracy, let alone participatory democracy. The political shift of the last 20 years must be confronted. He advocates for politicising internal debates more, even at the risk of disagreement, because the alternative — treating democratic methodologies as context-neutral — is a fundamental weakness.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Example:** Mini-publics and deliberative conferences that run parallel to real political processes.
- **Critique:** "It's almost as if one thing has nothing to do with the other. So we have a recipe and we have a methodology and we try to implement it."
- **Proposal:** More political discussion inside People Powered; acknowledging that the political environment matters.
- **Risk:** "We always run the risk of finding differences and divergences, but I don't think it's a threat."

**Key quotations**
> "Sometimes I think our work is much more technical than political. And I think this is a fragility because you can't really impact at the societal level at the political process if you are not looking at the political process as a whole."

> "Our internal planning normally is designed around our projects and we don't put them in the context of the world that we are living."

> "I think that definitely we should politicize a little more our debates inside People Power."

## Theme 8 — Geopolitical Blind Spots: Palestine and Ukraine

**Summary**  
Tarson addresses People Powered's handling of two major geopolitical crises — the war in Ukraine and the genocide in Palestine — and finds the organisation lacking, especially on Palestine. He identifies a divergence between Northern and Southern perspectives within the organisation and a general tendency toward silence on difficult geopolitical issues.

**Detailed explanation**  
The interviewee is critical of People Powered's silence on Palestine, which he describes as "the biggest civilizational question on the world." He perceives a division between those who are "solidary with the Palestinian people" and those who are "complicit with genocide," and feels the organisation has been "shy" on the issue. On Ukraine, he notes that there were some discussions, but significant disagreements emerged. He personally sees the war as more complicated than the simple "Russian aggression" narrative — pointing to NATO expansion as a provocation — and recognises that most of the Global South does not share the European sympathy with Ukraine. The organisation did not go further on Ukraine because of these differences. On Palestine, however, he believes there should have been at least some discussion, even if difficult. He speculates that the subject may have been avoided because it seems distant from democracy and participation — "it's like pure strength and pure force and pure violence" — but he insists this is not a sufficient reason for silence.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Crisis 1:** Palestine — described as "genocide"; lack of organisational response; "much more silence than any other thing."
- **Crisis 2:** Ukraine — discussed but not resolved due to internal disagreement; North-South divide in perspectives.
- **Tension:** The organisation's difficulty in addressing geopolitical issues that divide its membership.
- **Position:** Tarson is explicit about his own stance (pro-Palestinian solidarity; critical of the NATO-expansion narrative on Ukraine).

**Key quotations**
> "In the case of Ukraine, I think that we didn't went further because there were differences, lots of differences between the members. Most of the global south doesn't have the same sympathy with Ukraine."

> "In the case of Palestine, definitely people power has been shy."

> "I think that Palestine, the war in Palestine and the genocide in Palestine is like the biggest civilizational question on the world."

> "It's like much more silence than any other thing."

## Theme 9 — Technology and Digital Tools for Democratic Innovation

**Summary**  
Tarson notes that People Powered is making significant contributions in the area of information technology for democratic processes. He sees this as a growing aspect of the organisation's value: serving as an instrument for exchanging technological knowledge and tools that can enhance participatory and deliberative practices.

**Detailed explanation**  
While this theme is not extensively developed, the interviewee mentions it as an area where People Powered is "really aggregating" and "adding knowledge and resources." He frames this as a learning opportunity for himself and for practitioners in the Global South. The use of new technological tools for enhancing democratic processes is something he considers a genuinely valuable contribution from the organisation. He anticipates that this role will grow in importance over the next decade, with People Powered becoming an "instrument of interchange of technologies and knowledge about how to use this kind of resources in democratic practices."

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Area:** Information technology as a tool for democratic processes.
- **Role of PP:** Intermediary/exchange platform for technological knowledge.
- **Personal relevance:** Tarson frames it as something he has been "learning a lot with People Powered."

**Key quotations**
> "This initiative of incorporating new technological tools is something that People Powered is really aggregating. It's really adding knowledge and resources to our practices."

> "It's going to be a growing aspect of what People Power can do. Like being an instrument of interchange of technologies and knowledge about how to use this kind of resources in democratic practices."

## Theme 10 — Intergenerational and International Dialogue

**Summary**  
The interviewee values People Powered as a space for exchange across generations and continents. As an older practitioner, he can share his experience with younger activists, while learning from them about new technologies and methodologies. This mutual learning is one of the organisation's core functions.

**Detailed explanation**  
Tarson highlights the intergenerational dimension of People Powered's work. He describes himself as an "old practitioner and activist" whose experience can be "socialized and help others to be inspired." At the same time, he learns from "younger folks, especially on this technological and all these new instruments and new methodologies." The organisation has managed to create an environment where different countries, experiences, and generations can interact productively. He sees this integrative capacity as one of People Powered's main roles — "to be a space of integration."

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Generational exchange:** Older practitioners share experience; younger members bring technological and methodological innovation.
- **Geographic exchange:** Integration across different countries.
- **Organisational role:** People Powered as a "space of integration."

**Key quotations**
> "I really like the international, intergenerational dialogue. I am learning a lot and I think that People Powered managed to build an environment where I think my experience as an old practitioner and activist for participatory democracy can be socialized and help others to be inspired."

> "I think this is one of the main roles of PP, to be a space of integration."

## Theme 11 — The European Chapter: Beyond Geographical North-South

**Summary**  
Tarson supports the idea of a European chapter of People Powered in Ireland, seeing it not as a contradiction of the organisation's Global South orientation but as a pragmatic and ideologically coherent expansion. He argues that North-South is more an intellectual and political orientation than a geographical one.

**Detailed explanation**  
When asked about the establishment of a European chapter in Ireland, Tarson expresses agreement. Although in theoretical terms he would be cautious about creating European structures that could weaken the Global South focus, he considers Ireland a special case: it is "like a global south country in Europe" — anti-colonialist, with a strong national sentiment and a history of rebellion. He further argues that political orientation is not determined by geography: "you can be colonial from Germany and you can be totally colonialist in Argentina." The division between North and South is primarily ideological and intellectual. Pragmatically, having a presence in Europe also provides access to funding. His personal affection for Irish people — "almost Latin American Europeans" — adds a warm personal note.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Country:** Ireland.
- **Characterisation:** "Global south country in Europe," anti-colonialist, rebel, festive.
- **Principle:** North-South is ideological, not geographical.
- **Pragmatic argument:** Access to European funds.
- **Personal note:** Affection for Irish culture ("they are festive... almost Latin American Europeans").

**Key quotations**
> "If you look at the specific country that was chosen, I think it's an interesting evolution because in many aspects, Ireland is like a global south country in Europe."

> "The political orientation is not determined by geography."

> "I think that this north-south divide is much more an ideological and an intellectual issue than a geographical issue."

## Theme 12 — Looking Ahead: Uncertainty and the Need to Politicise

**Summary**  
Tarson concludes with an open and uncertain outlook for People Powered's future. The erosion of the democratic consensus that once supported the organisation's work calls into question the very premise of "going beyond liberal democracy." He reiterates the need to confront political realities directly.

**Detailed explanation**  
Looking ten years ahead, the interviewee expresses genuine uncertainty. He recalls that when the organisation started, democracy was a mainstream value supported by international institutions (the UN, the European Union, the World Bank). People Powered was part of a vanguard pushing the limits of that consensus. Now, democracy is "being abandoned by many social actors," and he does not know what will become of organisations that aimed to go beyond liberal democracy in a world where even liberal democracy is threatened. This reveals what he describes as "one of the main fragilities of our approach": the tendency to work as a "technical advisor without discussing much of the politics of the environments that we are inside." The future of People Powered, in his view, depends on its ability to integrate political analysis into its work and to confront the uncomfortable reality that the democratic consensus of the early 2000s has collapsed.

**Specific elements mentioned**
- **Institutions:** UN, European Union, World Bank (former pro-democracy consensus).
- **Fragility:** The depoliticised, technical approach to democratic innovation.
- **Uncertainty:** "I really don't know what is going to be made of organizations that wanted to go beyond liberal democracy on an environment when even liberal democracy is being threatened."

**Key quotations**
> "We are now living in an environment where democracy is being abandoned by many social actors."

> "This reveals to me one of the main fragilities of our approach on the issue. That is like we are more or less like a technical advisor without discussing much of the politics of the environments that we are inside."

> "We should definitely incorporate [politics] because our internal planning normally is designed around our projects and we don't put them in the context of the world that we are living."

## Summary of Recurring Concepts Across Themes

- **Practice vs. theory:** The tension (and desired alignment) between democratic values and organisational practices runs through nearly every theme.
- **North-South dynamics:** A central axis of the interview — as both a geographical and an ideological divide, with structural asymmetries in funding, power, and knowledge production.
- **Defensive shift:** The transition from optimism and deepening to defence and survival, at both global and Brazilian levels.
- **Politicisation:** The perceived need for the organisation to engage more openly with political context rather than treating democratic methodologies as technically neutral.
- **Inclusion and horizontality:** The value of creating spaces where diverse voices (grassroots/academic, North/South, old/young) can interact as equals.
- **Geopolitical limits:** The organisation's difficulty in addressing divisive international crises, revealing the boundaries of its consensus-based model.
