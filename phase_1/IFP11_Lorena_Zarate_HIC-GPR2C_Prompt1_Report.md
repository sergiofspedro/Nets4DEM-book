## Theme 1: Multi-Scalarity as Survival Strategy — HIC's 50-Year Resilience

**Theme summary:** HIC's defining adaptive capacity is its ability to operate at multiple scales simultaneously and switch between them strategically. *"When we see setbacks at any of those scales, then we use the other scales to maintain pressure and maintain working together."* This multi-scalarity — local, national, regional, international — is deliberate: when national governments resist, HIC shifts to local action or global advocacy. The "trans-local scale" — connecting local actions across geographies — is particularly important. Lorena describes this as using "the diversity and complexity also as part of the strategy."

**Detailed explanation:** Lorena frames HIC's multi-scalar strategy in explicitly tactical terms. The network works at the UN level (Habitat I, II, III, special rapporteurs), at the national level through member organisations, and at the local level through grassroots and community-based organisations. The critical innovation is not just working at all scales but knowing when to shift between them: "when we see windows of opportunity to advance advocacy at that level, we do that. But then when we see setbacks... we use the other scales." The local scale has become increasingly important — not just local action, but "the articulation of actions at the local scale... a trans-local component." The interviewer describes this as a "bread or sandwich" strategy: working the two extremes (local communities and global institutions) while the middle (national governments) remains the hardest to reach.

- Practices: Scale-switching advocacy; trans-local action articulation; strategic use of global policy windows.

## Theme 2: Actor Diversity as Strength and Challenge

**Theme summary:** HIC's internal diversity — NGOs, grassroots/community-based organisations (some with thousands of members, like housing cooperative federations), social movements, and academics — is its distinctive feature. Lorena describes this as both the network's richness and its operational challenge: *"Complexity is at the same time the richness of these kind of institutions, but it's also a big challenge. We're talking about complexity and diversity of actors."* The inclusion of social movements alongside professionalised NGOs creates internal tensions but also unique legitimacy — HIC can speak both to UN forums and to street-level organising.

**Detailed explanation:** Each actor type brings different organisational cultures, decision-making speeds, resource bases, and accountability structures. Aligning them is described as a constant process of negotiation. The UCLG partnership adds another layer — bringing local and regional governments into the coalition. The Global Platform for the Right to the City was created partly to manage this complexity, providing a meta-governance space where multiple networks (including HIC) can collaborate without merging. "You can imagine the complexity of working across those different lines, aligning priorities, visions, narratives, strategies."

**Specific elements:**
- Actors: HIC members (NGOs, grassroots/community-based organisations, social movements, academics), UCLG, Global Platform for the Right to the City.
- Practices: Multi-actor coalition governance; meta-governance through Platform.
- Problems: Aligning diverse actor types; tension between professionalised NGOs and movement-based organisations.

## Theme 3: Bottom-Up Knowledge Production

**Theme summary:** HIC's knowledge model reverses the typical flow: innovations emerge from local practice and travel upward to influence global agendas. *"We didn't see that coming from the global agendas first. That was something that local governments and civil society organizations working together were piloting at the local level and then working with the multilateral system to try to also influence national governments."* The example given is local care systems in Latin America, which emerged from municipal experimentation and later influenced global policy discourse. This practitioner-led knowledge production gives HIC distinctive credibility.

**Detailed explanation:** Unlike networks that produce knowledge through commissioned research or expert-led processes, HIC's knowledge emerges from the grounded experience of its member organisations. The network functions as a conduit — collecting local innovations, articulating them at global forums, and feeding global frameworks back to local actors. This bottom-up model is politically significant: it positions grassroots actors and local governments as knowledge producers, not just knowledge recipients. The example of care policies in Latin America illustrates the mechanism: "local governments and civil society organizations working together were piloting... and then working with the multilateral system."

**Specific elements:**
- Actors: Local governments, civil society organisations, multilateral system.
- Practices: Bottom-up knowledge production; practitioner-led innovation; local-to-global policy transfer.
- Territories: Latin America (care systems).

**Key quotations:** *"We didn't see that coming from the global agendas first. That was something that local governments and civil society organizations working together were piloting at the local level."* (IFP11, Lorena Zarate)

## Theme 4: The Politics of Naming — Coalition, Platform, Network

**Theme summary:** Lorena reflects on the significance of organisational names. HIC is a "coalition" — a term with political connotations of alliance and shared struggle. The Right to the City initiative chose "Platform" — a more open, technologically-inflected term that, according to Nelson Saule (another interviewee), *"comes from really a technological thing, that was a place where you deposit material... first a tool and then a name for a group."* These naming choices reflect different theories of how collective action should be organised: coalition implies political commitment and shared identity; platform implies openness, multi-actor collaboration, and infrastructure for collective work.

**Detailed explanation:** The naming question is not merely semantic — it shapes how organisations are perceived, who joins them, and what kinds of action they can legitimately undertake. "Coalition" signals a political alliance with shared objectives; "platform" signals an open space for multiple actors to collaborate without necessarily sharing all objectives. The evolution from HIC (coalition) to the Global Platform for the Right to the City (platform) reflects a strategic shift toward broader, more inclusive collaboration — but also a potential dilution of political clarity. The interviewer notes that "platform" originated as a technological metaphor, which creates an interesting resonance with digital platforms like Decidim.

**Specific elements:**
- Actors: HIC (coalition), Global Platform for the Right to the City (platform), Nelson Saule.
- Practices: Strategic naming; evolution from coalition to platform.
- Contradiction: Platform signals openness but may dilute political identity.

**Key quotations:** *"Platform comes from really a technological thing... first a tool and then a name for a group."* (IFP11, Lorena Zarate, quoting Nelson Saule)
