## Theme 1: Barcelona's Procurement Rules as a Barrier to Network Participation

**Theme summary:** Marc Serra describes how evolving public procurement regulations in Spain have created legal obstacles for Barcelona City Council's financial contributions to international city networks like OIDP. The concept of contributing to a network — rather than paying for a specific service — is "questioned by the legal services, the auditing department, and the secretariat." This has practical consequences: the Council's legal services increasingly scrutinise network contributions as potential irregularities. The solution Marc's administration pursued was linking OIDP more closely to UCLG, which provided "more legal credibility to the OIDP agreement file... because it gave it the legitimacy of the UCLG members, who are many more than OIDP, and with a structure."

**Detailed explanation:** Marc provides a practical, legal-administrative perspective that complements the more political accounts from other OIDP interviewees. The procurement problem is structural: public administrations are increasingly constrained in how they can spend money internationally. Contributing to a network — as opposed to contracting a service — falls into a legal grey area that auditors and legal departments view with suspicion. The UCLG integration was, from this perspective, not primarily a political strategy but a legal necessity: routing Barcelona's contribution through UCLG gave it institutional legitimacy and procurement compliance. This legal dimension of network financing is often overlooked in discussions of network governance but is, Marc suggests, a critical practical constraint.

## Theme 2: The Fragility of Politically-Dependent Networks

**Theme summary:** Marc reflects on the vulnerability of networks that depend on the political will of a single host city. He observes that the current Barcelona City Council "hasn't prioritized citizen participation or the OIDP," which has made it "difficult to convene this steering committee with any regularity." He contrasts OIDP with other city networks that are "more closely linked to a specific electoral cycle and personal leadership — often, to the person who establishes them." The networks that endure are those that "have become institutionalized as associations." The UCLG umbrella, he argues, is what saved OIDP from Barcelona's political fragmentation: *"It's a good thing that UCLG existed to support the OEDP, because with that fragmentation you can't govern an international entity."*

**Detailed explanation:** Marc draws on his experience both as Secretary General of OIDP and as a Barcelona provincial politician to analyse the structural vulnerability of city-led networks. He distinguishes three types of networks: (a) those tied to a specific electoral cycle and individual leader (fragile); (b) those that have institutionalised as associations (more durable); and (c) those nested within larger institutional structures like UCLG (most durable). OIDP has moved from type (a) toward type (c) — a trajectory that Marc sees as essential for its survival. The current Barcelona administration's disengagement is presented as evidence that the UCLG integration strategy was prescient: without it, OIDP would have been paralysed by the host city's shifting priorities.

**Specific elements:**
- Actors: Barcelona City Council (current administration), OIDP steering committee, UCLG.
- Practices: Institutionalisation through UCLG nesting; association as durable legal form; electoral-cycle dependency as fragility.
- Problems: Host-city political disengagement; solved through UCLG integration.

**Key quotations:** *"It's a good thing that UCLG existed to support the OEDP, because with that fragmentation you can't govern an international entity."* (IFP11b, Marc Serra)

## Theme 4: The Fragility of Network Memory and Individual Leadership

**Theme summary:** Marc reflects on the personal dimension of network leadership. When the political leadership changes and no new politician takes up the OIDP portfolio, the network lacks a champion within the host institution. "If there isn't a politician who takes up the issue and says, no, no, I'm the general secretary... I don't know who the Secretary General is." This creates a vacuum that technical staff (like the coordinator) cannot fill alone, because they lack the political mandate. The network's survival depends on individuals stepping into leadership roles — and when they don't, institutional mechanisms are insufficient to compensate.

**Detailed explanation:** Marc's observation connects to the broader individual-institution paradox identified across OIDP interviews. The Secretary General role is political — it requires a politician with the authority to convene, set agendas, and mobilise resources. When Barcelona's political leadership deprioritises participation, the role effectively becomes vacant, even if formally filled. The coordinator (Adrià) can manage day-to-day operations but cannot exercise political leadership without a mandate. This creates a situation where the network continues to function technically but is politically adrift — a form of institutional hibernation that may be sustainable in the short term but erodes the network's capacity to adapt and grow.

**Specific elements:**
- Actors: Marc Serra (former SG), Adrià Duarte (coordinator), unnamed current SG.
- Practices: Political vs. technical leadership distinction; network hibernation during political disengagement.
- Problems: Vacuum when political leadership withdraws; technical staff cannot fully compensate.
