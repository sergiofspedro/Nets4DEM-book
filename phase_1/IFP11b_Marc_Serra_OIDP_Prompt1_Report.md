# IFP11b – Marc Serra (OIDP / Barcelona Provincial Council) – Prompt 1 Report

## Theme 1: The Permanent General Secretariat – A Double-Edged Sword

### Summary
Marc Serra describes the central dilemma of the OIDP (International Observatory on Participatory Democracy): Barcelona's willingness to act as Permanent General Secretariat, funding 70–90% of the network's costs, was the single factor that guaranteed its survival for 25 years. However, this same generosity created a structural imbalance that contradicted the network's own values of participation and horizontality.

### Detailed Explanation
When Serra took on the role of Secretary General, he inherited a structure where Barcelona City Council financed "70, 80, or 90%" of the network's operating costs. This arrangement was, in his assessment, the decisive factor in OIDP's longevity: "if this Permanent General Secretariat hadn't existed, we certainly wouldn't be able to talk about the OIDP's twenty-five or twenty-six years of existence today."

The alternative—networks built on personal political leadership or even rotating secretariats—tends to produce "comet networks" that flare brightly during a single electoral cycle and disappear. Serra had seen this repeatedly: "countless city networks that begin and end their work after just one election cycle."

Yet this stability came at a price. The knowledge asymmetry between Barcelona's Technical Secretariat and the rest of the membership created what Serra describes as a fundamentally unidirectional flow: "all the knowledge about its functioning is accumulated by the Technical Secretariat, and ultimately, it is the Technical Secretariat with whom they report." The annual Assembly, intended to be the supreme decision-making body, became a rubber-stamp exercise: "the OIDP's annual work plan, when presented at the General Assembly, ended up being ratified almost without discussion."

### Specific Elements
- **Funding share**: Barcelona covered 70–90% of OIDP costs
- **Structural asymmetry**: Technical Secretariat held all institutional knowledge
- **Assembly weakness**: Annual meetings lacked informed debate
- **Comparison**: Other networks without permanent secretariats died within one electoral cycle
- **Serra's job**: To balance Barcelona's indispensable role with democratic checks and balances

### Key Quotations
- "If this Permanent General Secretariat hadn't existed, we certainly wouldn't be able to talk about the OIDP's twenty-five or twenty-six years of existence today."
- "I've seen countless city networks that begin and end their work after just one election cycle."
- "The OIDP's annual work plan, when presented at the General Assembly, ended up being ratified almost without discussion."

## Theme 2: Creating the Steering Committee – An Attempt at Horizontality

### Summary
Recognising the governance deficit, Serra created an intermediate body—the OIDP Steering Committee—to sit between the annual Assembly and the General Secretariat. It required members to contribute something (dues, venues, expertise, translation work) and was intended to generate strategic direction collectively rather than unilaterally.

### Detailed Explanation
The Steering Committee was Serra's most concrete institutional innovation. Membership was self-nominating but conditional on material contribution to the network: dues, hosting events, specialised knowledge, translation capacity, or co-organising activities. The logic was that contribution-based membership would create genuine stakeholding.

Serra's assessment of the intervention is measured: the Committee "did manage to create a space, as I said, for intermediate governance between the Assembly and the General Secretariat, which gave it a bit more horizontality." It was intended to generate "strategic lines to shape the vision and work plans of the OIDP" rather than having these defined unilaterally by Barcelona.

However, the Committee's effectiveness depended on active convening by Barcelona, and Serra acknowledges that after his departure and with high turnover in Barcelona's participation portfolio, "there haven't been any steering committee meetings because there's no one to call them." The fragility of the fix reveals the underlying structural problem: even well-designed governance innovations cannot survive the collapse of the sponsoring city's political commitment.

### Specific Elements
- **Innovation**: Steering Committee between Assembly and General Secretariat
- **Membership condition**: Contribution to the network (dues, venues, expertise, translation)
- **Function**: Generate strategic lines and work plans collectively
- **Success**: Created horizontality for 2–3 years during Serra's term
- **Vulnerability**: Dependent on Barcelona's capacity to convene
- **Post-Serra collapse**: No meetings due to staff turnover in Barcelona

### Key Quotations
- "We did manage to create a space for intermediate governance between the Assembly and the General Secretariat."
- "There haven't been any steering committee meetings because there's no one to call them."

## Theme 3: The Paradox of a Centralised Participation Network

### Summary
Serra openly acknowledges the existential contradiction at the heart of the OIDP: a network that promotes participatory democracy is itself governed in a highly centralised, top-down manner. He is the first to name this paradox, but argues it was the necessary condition for the network's survival, given that participation is a "low priority" for most governments.

### Detailed Explanation
The contradiction is impossible to ignore: "a network that promotes participatory governance is so centralized, in a way." Serra does not defend this arrangement as ideal but frames it as a realistic adaptation to the political economy of participation.

He contrasts OIDP with city networks focused on tourism, industry, or urban planning, where "members have a vested interest in hosting these networks and in housing their General Secretariats." Participation, by contrast, is "a low priority for governments." The people working on it do so "because we really believe in it," but the political interest is "limited."

This structural weakness of the policy field itself forced the centralisation: "If I may say so, this has been its lifeline, ensuring its survival over the years, given that participation is, if I may say so, such a low priority for governments." Without Barcelona's disproportionate commitment, the network would have collapsed long ago.

### Specific Elements
- **Self-awareness**: Serra is the first to name the paradox
- **Justification**: Policy field weakness necessitated centralisation
- **Contrast**: Other policy areas (tourism, planning) attract more balanced contributions
- **Realism**: "Participation is such a low priority for governments"
- **Survival logic**: Centralisation was the lifeline, not the ideal

### Key Quotations
- "It's indeed contradictory: a network that promotes participatory governance is so centralized."
- "This has been its lifeline, ensuring its survival over the years, given that participation is such a low priority for governments."
- "Many times those of us who are working on these issues are doing so because we really believe in them."

## Theme 4: The Vulnerability of City Networks to Electoral Cycles

### Summary
Serra draws on his broader political experience to argue that institutionalisation—specifically, establishing networks as legal associations with their own legal personality—is the best protection against electoral-cycle disruption. He contrasts the OIDP with other networks he helped create that survived government changes precisely because they had a formal associational structure.

### Detailed Explanation
During his tenure with Barcelona en Comú, Serra helped create "hundreds of networks"—on refugees, tourism management, public energy, public water, and more. Most of the politically-driven ones vanished after the 2023 government change: "These networks, for example, no longer exist. And they were informal networks."

The exceptions were those that had been formalised as associations. The Association of Municipalities for Public Energy and the Association of Municipalities for Public Water both survived the government transition because "there's an institutional structure linked to this, it's maintained." Even though the new Barcelona administration "hardly ever attends the assemblies," the networks continue because other cities have taken the political lead.

The lesson for OIDP is clear: the network lacked its own legal personality—"it didn't even have its own legal entity"—and was ultimately just the Barcelona City Council. This structural weakness made it entirely dependent on whoever held the participation portfolio in Barcelona.

### Specific Elements
- **Survival factor**: Legal personality as association outlasts electoral cycles
- **Public energy network example**: Survived government change because other cities took leadership
- **Public water network example**: Continued functioning despite Barcelona's disengagement
- **OIDP vulnerability**: No legal entity—essentially a branch of Barcelona City Council
- **Political will dependency**: "The folder they have at the very bottom"

### Key Quotations
- "The fact that the city networks have a legal structure, with their own legal entity, with an association, if possible, helps both their long-term sustainability."
- "The OIDP—we must remember that it didn't even have its own legal entity."
- "Those are the only ones that have stood the test of time."

## Theme 5: Bureaucratic Barriers to Network Sustainability

### Summary
Serra identifies a surprising obstacle to OIDP's sustainability: the increasing formalisation of public procurement and transparency policies has made it harder, not easier, for cities to contribute financially to the network. The administrative burden of membership fees has become a significant barrier.

### Detailed Explanation
The evolution of "doctrine affecting everything related to public procurement and subsidies" has had a counterintuitive effect. Serra explains that twenty years ago, a city could join the OIDP by passing a simple motion and paying an invoice. Now, "it has to be in the budget, it has to be itemized, it has to be public, it has to be voted on by a majority of the members of the plenary session."

The practical consequence is that cities that once contributed small membership fees (around €6,000–€10,000) now struggle to do so. Serra and his team would sometimes tell prospective members: "it's better if you don't contribute anything, or if you do contribute something, it should be more in the form of a service contract"—producing a video, doing translation, co-organising an event.

This creates a paradox where well-intentioned transparency and anti-corruption policies inadvertently undermine international networking by local governments. Serra notes that "the concept of contributing to a network of cities" is now questioned by "legal services, the auditing department, and the secretariat."

### Specific Elements
- **Historical ease**: Simple motion + invoice = membership
- **Current burden**: Budget line item, plenary vote, auditor scrutiny
- **Workaround**: In-kind contributions (service contracts, videos, translation)
- **Policy paradox**: Transparency policies hinder networking
- **Impact**: "Mayors dismantling their participation in city networks"

### Key Quotations
- "It's often a bureaucratic nightmare because it has to be in the budget, it has to be itemized, it has to be public."
- "This is not a minor issue, it is not a minor issue."
- "You increasingly find mayors who are dismantling their participation in city networks because they are put in administrative difficulties."

## Theme 6: North-South Asymmetries in Network Participation

### Summary
Serra describes pragmatic efforts to ensure balanced representation from the Global South, primarily through the host city's conference budget. The presidency model—where the city hosting the annual conference sets aside funds to invite participants from the Global South—was the primary mechanism during his tenure.

### Detailed Explanation
When asked about how OIDP handled the structural disadvantage of Global South cities facing visa difficulties, travel costs, and limited budgets, Serra points to the conference-hosting model: "whoever has hosted the conference, the city hosting it, has always been asked to set aside a budget to invite mayors, elected officials, and representatives from southern cities."

This produced tangible results. Iztapalapa (Mexico) was particularly effective: "the conference that was held in Iztapalapa… there were a lot of people from the south." The mechanism worked by inviting participants as speakers, "which is the administrative mechanism that allows you to pay for the trips."

However, this model remains ad hoc rather than structural. There is no dedicated fund for Global South participation—it depends on each host city's commitment and capacity. It mitigates asymmetry without resolving it.

### Specific Elements
- **Mechanism**: Host city funds Global South participation from conference budget
- **Success case**: Iztapalapa conference had strong Global South attendance
- **Administrative workaround**: Speaker invitations cover travel costs
- **Limitation**: Ad hoc rather than structural solution
- **Dependency**: Relies on host city's commitment and resources

### Key Quotations
- "Whoever has hosted the conference has always been asked to set aside a budget to invite mayors, elected officials, and representatives from southern cities."
- "Iztapalapa really got involved, and there were a lot of people from the south."

## Theme 7: The Role of Meso-Level Institutions in Supporting Participation

### Summary
Now serving as Deputy for Citizen Participation at the Barcelona Provincial Council (Diputació de Barcelona), Serra argues that intermediate-level governments—provincial councils, regional governments—have a crucial role to play in supporting small and medium municipalities that lack the technical capacity to run high-quality participatory processes. He sees OIDP as an ideal forum for this discussion.

### Detailed Explanation
Serra's move from Barcelona City Council to the Provincial Council changed his perspective. He now sees that "supra-local administrations… which support local governments, are very important for addressing the shortcomings of medium and small municipalities." These shortcomings are not only economic but technical: "the person in charge of citizen participation is likely also responsible for youth and the environment."

The Provincial Council's Decidim programme is his flagship example: offering municipalities a free, multi-tenant digital participation platform with full technical support. This model ensures quality: "we essentially offer municipalities a free service and support to answer all their questions regarding platform updates, module updates, and everything else."

He extends this logic to participation regulations and associative frameworks: "the Provincial Council can help us develop a municipal participation regulation." The idea is that meso-level institutions provide "frameworks and models—not only financial resources, which are also important, but above all, frameworks and models that ensure high-quality participation."

He explicitly argues that OIDP should create more space for intermediate governments: "a space for reflection, exchange of experiences, and academic and political debate like the OIDP is an ideal forum for the participation of regional and local governments."

### Specific Elements
- **Role**: Meso-level institutions support small municipalities lacking capacity
- **Technical gap**: Small municipalities have staff responsible for multiple portfolios
- **Decidim model**: Free platform + technical support raises participation quality
- **Regulatory support**: Provincial councils help draft participation regulations
- **OIDP fit**: Ideal forum for meso-level institutional participation

### Key Quotations
- "If we say that participation is also a model and a method, and that it must be done well and with quality, it's crucial to ask: who can help ensure this quality?"
- "A space for reflection like the OIDP is an ideal forum for the participation of regional and local governments."

## Theme 8: Bridging Traditional Participatory Democracy and Digital Innovation

### Summary
Serra identifies a cultural gap within the OIDP between the older tradition of grassroots, in-person participatory democracy and the emerging world of digital democratic innovation. He sees the Provincial Council's Decidim deployment as a bridge between these two worlds.

### Detailed Explanation
After his return to the Provincial Council, Serra perceived "a kind of OIDP tradition, which is more of a participatory, grassroots, community action tradition." This tradition values in-person deliberation and community organising. Meanwhile, "the whole world of democratic innovation and participation has emerged"—digital platforms, online deliberation, open-source tools.

Serra's diagnosis is that "these are two worlds that need each other." The OIDP's Rio conference was used to present Decidim and "to make Decidim available to local governments that need it." The Provincial Council's multi-tenant model lowers the barrier for small municipalities: "we've managed to bring digital participation not only to tech enthusiasts and hackers, but also to local councils."

The insight is that digital tools are not replacing traditional participation but enabling it at scale, especially for resource-constrained municipalities. The mediating role of meso-level institutions is critical: they provide the infrastructure that makes digital participation accessible to those who would otherwise be excluded.

### Specific Elements
- **OIDP tradition**: Grassroots, in-person, community action
- **Emerging field**: Democratic innovation, digital platforms
- **Bridge**: Decidim deployment by Provincial Council
- **Target**: Small municipalities without technical capacity
- **Rio conference**: Showcased Decidim to OIDP membership

### Key Quotations
- "My mission was to see if we could help bridge these two worlds, which are two worlds that need each other."
- "We've managed to bring digital participation not only to tech enthusiasts and hackers, but also to local councils."

## Theme 9: Political Turnover and the Fragility of Institutional Knowledge

### Summary
Serra offers a stark illustration of how political turnover in Barcelona has crippled the OIDP's governance. In the three years following his departure, four different people were responsible for citizen participation in the Barcelona City Council, creating a leadership vacuum that made it impossible to convene the Steering Committee or maintain institutional continuity.

### Detailed Explanation
When asked why the Steering Committee stopped meeting, Serra provides a detailed account of the turnover: "there have been four people responsible for citizen participation in the Barcelona City Council over the last three years." Starting with Luis Rabel, who "took about eight months to issue the decree to take office," followed by a commissioner, then a different councillor.

The consequence: "I don't know who the Secretary General of the OIDP is right now. I really don't." This is not a criticism of individuals but a structural observation: "the current government isn't interested in the issue of participation." The participation folder is "the very bottom of all the folders they have to open and work on."

Serra's response has been cautious: "I don't want to be a nuisance and say, no, I have ideas, I want to do things. Because, of course, if there isn't a politician who takes up the issue and says, no, no, I'm the general secretary." The network, lacking its own legal structure, cannot move without Barcelona's initiative.

### Specific Elements
- **Turnover rate**: 4 officials in 3 years
- **Start-up delay**: 8 months for the first successor to formalise appointment
- **Knowledge loss**: Institutional memory concentrated in departing official
- **Current state**: Serra does not know who the Secretary General is
- **Network paralysis**: Steering Committee cannot function without convener

### Key Quotations
- "There have been four people responsible for citizen participation in the Barcelona City Council over the last three years."
- "I don't know who the Secretary General of the OIDP is right now. I really don't."
- "It's the folder they have at the very bottom of all the folders they have to open and work on."

## Theme 10: The OIDP-UCLG Transition as Institutional Survival

### Summary
Serra discusses the decision, taken during his tenure, to anchor the OIDP within UCLG (United Cities and Local Governments). He frames this as a pragmatic move to give the network "legal credibility" and access to a wider membership base, acknowledging that the OIDP's lack of formal legal personality made it dependent on this larger structure for survival.

### Detailed Explanation
The interviewer confirms that the OIDP is "an informal arm of UCLG today." Serra explains that during his term, the link to UCLG was seen as "a way to give more legal credibility to the OIDP agreement file." UCLG provided "the legitimacy of the UCLG members, who are many more than OIDP, and with a structure."

This move was a response to the network's fundamental structural weakness: lacking its own legal personality, the OIDP needed an institutional host. UCLG offered that hosting with the added benefit of scale and legitimacy. However, Serra does not present this as an ideal solution but as the realistic option available.

The broader implication is that networks built on the policy area of participation—which is structurally marginal within most governments—face a choice between dependence on a single sponsoring city (Barcelona) or absorption into a larger institutional framework (UCLG). Neither option fully resolves the tension between stability and autonomy.

### Specific Elements
- **Rationale**: Legal credibility and wider membership base
- **Structure**: OIDP became informal arm of UCLG
- **Serra's role**: Participated in the decision during his tenure
- **Trade-off**: Legitimacy and scale versus autonomy
- **Structural constraint**: OIDP lacked legal personality for independent survival

### Key Quotations
- "We saw it as a way to give more legal credibility to the OIDP agreement file, linking it to UCLG."
- "It gave it the legitimacy of the UCLG members, who are many more than OIDP."
