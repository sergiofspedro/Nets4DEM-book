## Theme 1: The Name That Outlived Its Meaning

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
The International Observatory of Participatory Democracy (OIDP) began as an EU-funded project (Urbal A/B) designed to create local observatories of participation across European and Latin American cities. When EU funding ended, Barcelona decided to maintain the network — but the local observatories model was abandoned. The name "Observatory" persisted through institutional inertia. Adrià acknowledges he doesn't know why the name was kept. Today, the observatory function is performed by the **Best Practice Award** — a substitute mechanism that generates the field intelligence the original observatories were meant to produce.

**Detailed explanation:**  
OIDP was created in 2001 as a project within Urbal A/B EU programmes, involving nine planned local observatories across Europe and Latin America. The original idea was "to have this international observatory that were making these observance of the different local observatories" (ITR1). When EU funding ended (~2007), Barcelona City Council decided to continue the network, but the local observatories were largely abandoned — "the idea of the local observatories... finished." However, the name persisted: "I don't know why but they decided to keep" (ITR1). The Best Practice Award (20 editions) now functions as the de facto observation mechanism: "You are an observatory thanks to the Best Practice Award. Absolutely" (ITR1). The Award generates data that surveys and other methods failed to produce: "Sometimes we did a survey or any other way of receiving inputs and it's really not working at all. And the award is really working on that sense" (ITR1).

**Specific elements:**
- Actors: Barcelona City Council, EU Urbal programmes, City of Porto Alegre (early partner).
- Practices: Best Practice Award as substitute observatory; conference rotation.
- Problems: Name persistence despite functional change.

**Key quotations:** *"You are an observatory thanks to the Best Practice Award. Absolutely."*

## Theme 2: Lean Resilience — The Strategic Advantage of Being Small

Adrià frames this not as a weakness but as a strategic strength: *"Also the good thing is that it's so small amount of money that it's probably more easy to survive."* The network's low cost makes it politically invisible enough to survive changes in Barcelona's municipal administration. Membership fees were explored but rejected. Crowdfunding was considered but not pursued. The lean model is limiting — the secretariat cannot sign project letters, cannot bid directly for EU funds — but it is survivable.

**Detailed explanation:**

Adrià describes exploration of alternative funding models: membership fees ("we made some research... we decided it was really not the good way to go"), crowdfunding ("micro donations and things like that"), neither pursued. The small budget has a paradoxical resilience: it's too small to attract political scrutiny during administrative changes, allowing survival across electoral cycles. The integration with UCLG provides administrative back-office functions that a small network couldn't sustain independently.

**Specific elements:**

Adrià is explicit: *"This network has keep working because there's persons around... it's really the people who are behind."* Many institutional members are effectively single individuals using their institution's name. ## Theme 4: The Defensive Turn — From Optimistic Expansion to Protecting Democracy

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
OIDP's political framing has shifted over the last decade: from an optimistic "let's improve participation" discourse to a defensive "let's protect democracy" posture. Adrià attributes this to the global rise of authoritarianism and democratic backsliding. *"Probably since the last 10 years... the narrative against democracy rising of authoritarian... it's more defensive."* The network's agenda now focuses more on how participation can "regain and build trust again between communities" — a protective rather than expansive framing.

**Detailed explanation:**  
Adrià describes three broad phases in OIDP's trajectory: the EU project era (optimistic, small community), the internationalisation period (expansion to Africa, award maturing), and the current defensive period (responding to authoritarian threats). The change is contextual, not strategic: "the context changed so much in the world politically." The defensive turn is experienced as a shift in mission — from showcasing best practice to defending the space for democratic participation. However, OIDP's core activities (conference, award) have not fundamentally changed.

**Specific elements:**
- Actors: OIDP members, local governments facing authoritarian central governments (e.g., Argentina cited).
- Contexts: Global democratic backsliding, rising authoritarianism.
- Problems: Maintaining democratic discourse when the political environment is increasingly hostile.

**Key quotations:** *"Probably since the last 10 years... it's more defensive."*

## Theme 5: Managed Openness — Including Non-Democratic Countries

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
OIDP maintains an explicitly open posture toward all cities worldwide, including those in non-democratic countries. The philosophy: *"We understand that it's better to have one city of a non-democratic country being part of our neighborhood because... with us they're gonna be listening about our narrative, our values, and our principles."* This policy has been tested — for instance, when Tehran submitted an award nomination, some jurors declined to evaluate it on ethical grounds. The governance mechanism is distributed decision-making rather than institutional gatekeeping.

**Detailed explanation:**  
Adrià articulates a clear philosophical position: engagement is better than exclusion. The network believes that exposure to democratic values and practices can influence participants from authoritarian contexts, and that excluding them would foreclose that possibility. No formal "charter of values" is required for membership — alignment is implicitly understood through the network's declarations and mission. When ethical tensions arise (like the Tehran award nomination), they are managed through distributed decision-making: individual jurors exercise discretion rather than the network taking an institutional position that might foreclose future engagement.

**Specific elements:**
- Actors: Tehran (award nomination), individual jurors, OIDP members.
- Practices: No formal charter of values; distributed ethical decision-making through juror discretion.
- Contradiction: Openness to all vs. institutional values coherence.

**Key quotations:** *"We understand that it's better to have one city of a non-democratic country being part of our neighborhood because... with us they're gonna be listening about our narrative."*

OIDP's relationship with UCLG has evolved from purely bureaucratic shelter (2007) to deep structural integration (2024–2025), with Adrià now coordinating both OIDP and the UCLG Committee on Participatory Democracy. The convergence has increased award nominations from UCLG Committee-linked cities and has broadened OIDP's agenda to include human rights alongside participation. The main cost is the coordinator's reduced time for OIDP-specific work.

**Specific elements:**
- Actors: UCLG, UCLG Committee on Participatory Democracy, Barcelona City Council, Adrià Duarte Griño.
- Practices: Dual-coordinator model, administrative integration, political umbrella.
