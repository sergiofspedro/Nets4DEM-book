## Theme 1: The Founding Period — Urbal A/B and the Local Observatories Model

She describes how OIDP began as a spin-off of the Urbal A project, with Urbal B providing the funding to create nine local observatories (six in Latin America, three in Europe). The project was designed by a consultant before she arrived: "when I arrived, a consultant had done the entire project design, those laborious projects." The observatories model was the defining feature of this period.

**Detailed explanation:** Melissa started working at OIDP in 2004, at the very beginning of the Urbal B project. She describes the project scope: nine local observatories, with Barcelona City Hall as the lead partner, along with cities like Porto Alegre. The observatories were intended as local-level analysis structures — "to analyze the state of democracy and participation." The project ran from 2004 to approximately 2007, which is when the search began for "more definitive solutions for the institutionalization of the network." During this period, OIDP was housed within Barcelona's citizen participation department, with the department director (Francisco Ossan, later Alex Montes) as the primary responsible person, a manager from the City Hall, and then the technical staff (Melissa's role). The international initiative of the participation department was essentially the Observatory.

## Theme 2: The Move to UCLG — Purely Bureaucratic at First

**Theme summary:** In 2007, OIDP moved its secretariat to UCLG, but Melissa emphasises that the initial relationship was "purely bureaucratic" — staff were hired by UCLG and desks were moved, but "it was completely disconnected from the entire UCLG agenda, there was nothing in common." The move was a solution to institutionalize OIDP after project funding ended, leveraging Barcelona's existing relationship with UCLG. The Barcelona City Council committed (tacitly, not formally) to maintain funding for the minimum structure.

**Detailed explanation:** The move occurred around 2007, after the Urbal B project was closing. Melissa describes the negotiation: Barcelona's international relations secretary (Antònia Sabartes) negotiated with UCLG to host OIDP. The staff were moved to UCLG's administrative floor — upstairs, away from the main UCLG operations — and "we from IDP were there, Vanessa [Marques, from UCLG's Commission] also had her desk there, but it was completely disconnected." There was no substantive collaboration or agenda integration at this stage. Barcelona's commitment to maintain funding was implicit: "perhaps not with a formal commitment... a very clear announcement to the world... but there was a tacit commitment... Barcelona would maintain this funding."

**Specific elements:**
- Actors: Antònia Sabartes (Barcelona international relations), UCLG, Vanessa Marques (UCLG Commission).
- Practices: Pure administrative hosting; no programmatic integration initially.
- Problems: Finding a permanent institutional home after project funding ended.

**Key quotations:** *"In 2007, the solution to institutionalize it was... let's try to put IDP inside UCLG. And in the beginning... the initial relationship was purely bureaucratic; that was it."*

## Theme 3: Creating the Award and the Rotating Presidency

**Theme summary:** During Melissa's tenure, two key institutional innovations were introduced: the Best Practice Award and the rotating presidency system. The presidency was separated from Barcelona for the first time — the secretariat remained in Barcelona (funding, structure), but the presidency would be held by whichever city hosted the annual conference, which also paid for the conference. This was designed to expand and strengthen the network's "future sustainability."

**Detailed explanation:** Under the Urbal A project, both the presidency and technical secretariat were held by Barcelona. During Urbal B, a restructuring occurred: "the technical secretariat would remain in Barcelona, because there's funding, there's a structure, etc., but the presidency would be held by whoever hosted the annual conference." The host city took on responsibility for the political agenda, convening members, and paying for the conference itself. Melissa describes this as a deliberate strategy for network sustainability, distributing political leadership and financial burden beyond Barcelona. The award was also created during this period and became a central mechanism for knowledge production.

**Specific elements:**
- Practices: Rotating presidency tied to conference hosting; award creation; separation of technical secretariat (Barcelona) from political presidency (rotating).
- Actors: Barcelona City Council (secretariat), rotating host cities (presidency).
- Problems: Reducing dependency on Barcelona for network sustainability.

**Key quotations:** *"The presidency would be held by whoever hosted the annual conference... that presidency would be responsible for the more political aspects, the agenda, calling members... and also paying for the conference."*

## Theme 4: Quality Standards in Participatory Democracy

**Theme summary:** Melissa describes an important early intellectual debate within OIDP about what constitutes quality in participatory democracy. At a time when deliberative participatory budgeting coexisted with merely advisory councils, OIDP developed "common quality criteria for these different experiences... some minimum standards that can determine if the PB is of quality or if this advisory council is of quality." This was an effort to establish methodological rigor without imposing a single normative standard — recognizing that different forms of participation had different values, but all needed to meet minimum democratic quality thresholds.

**Detailed explanation:** The debate emerged from the diversity of participatory practices among member cities. Some cities had deliberative PB (where participants decide on resource allocation), others had advisory councils (where participants are consulted but cannot decide). The network developed criteria that acknowledged both as legitimate forms of participatory democracy while establishing "minimum standards... regardless of whether one is deliberative or advisory." This marked a move away from judging different models as better or worse, toward a quality framework that could accommodate diversity. Melissa does not know if this discussion continued after her departure.

**Specific elements:**
- Practices: Development of common quality criteria for participatory democracy; distinguishing deliberative from advisory mechanisms.
- Problems: Tension between normative standards and methodological pluralism.

**Key quotations:** *"We started to focus... not on judging what was better or worse, but on establishing some order, saying, all of this is local participatory democracy, but they are very different experiences."*

## Theme 5: The Nanterre Exclusion — When the Network Erases Its Own People

**Theme summary:** Melissa recounts a personally significant experience that illustrates the institutional rigidity of OIDP toward former staff. After leaving the secretariat in 2007, she attended the OIDP conference in Nanterre (France) months later — but was denied entry because she was not registered and no longer represented a city. The experience left her feeling that her prior contribution was unrecognized: *"The Nanterre city hall wouldn't let me in because I hadn't registered, because I didn't represent any city... I found it strange, this rigidity."* She contextualises this as probably not personal — the gatekeeper likely did not know who she was — but the incident vividly illustrates how formal institutional boundaries erase individual knowledge and relationships.

**Detailed explanation:** After leaving OIDP, Melissa went to IGOP (Institute of Government and Public Policies, Autonomous University of Barcelona) for her master's and doctorate. That same year, the OIDP conference was held in Nanterre. Ricardo and others from Brazil were attending. Melissa went to meet them — but was stopped at the door. The registration was paid that year, she had not paid, and as a non-city representative, she had no standing. The gatekeeper (Antonio Aniesa, Melissa recalls) enforced the rule rigidly. She reflects that it was not personal — "I don't think he even knew who I was" — but the experience was significant enough that she remembers it vividly nearly 20 years later. The interviewer frames it as "denying, to some extent, the value of the memory that people could represent within that space."

**Specific elements:**
- Actors: Melissa Pomeroy, Antonio Aniesa (Nanterre participation coordinator), Ricardo (from Fortaleza).
- Places: Nanterre (France), IGOP/Autonomous University of Barcelona.
- Events: OIDP Nanterre conference (2007/2008).

## Theme 6: Siloed Networks in Barcelona

**Theme summary:** Melissa describes the early OIDP's isolation from other Barcelona-based city networks. "There was Cidades Educadoras [Educating Cities], which was in the same building as Metrópolis... but there was never any substantial contact." She attributes this to Barcelona City Council's lack of an ecosystemic vision: "they didn't really care... they didn't have a perspective of, since we have all these international networks here, all in the same building, all on the same street, let's get them to talk to each other." She also notes that networks had very different structures and funding levels, creating a protective silo mentality — "this network here, the IDP, isn't very stable yet... it's better not to mess with it too much."

**Detailed explanation:** During Melissa's tenure, multiple city networks operated from the same Barcelona location: OIDP, Educating Cities, Metrópolis, and an Iberian network. Despite physical proximity, there was no cross-network collaboration. Melissa attributes this to: (a) Barcelona's departmental silos — each department "owned" its network (the Education Department owned Educating Cities, the Participation Department owned OIDP); (b) the fragility of OIDP's own institutional position, making it protective rather than collaborative; and (c) the absence of a coordinating vision. The interviewer notes that Barcelona has recently recovered a more ecosystemic vision, with dialogue between networks on topics like overtourism.

**Specific elements:**
- Networks: OIDP, Educating Cities, Metrópolis, Iberian city network.
- Places: Barcelona (shared building).
- Problems: Departmental silos, lack of cross-network coordination, protective isolation of fragile networks.

**Key quotations:** *"There was never any substantial contact... they didn't really care... they didn't have a perspective of, since we have all these international networks here, all in the same building, let's get them to talk to each other."*
