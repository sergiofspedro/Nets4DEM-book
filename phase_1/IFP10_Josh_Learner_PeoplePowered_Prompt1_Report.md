## Theme 1: The Co-Created Origins of People Powered — From Philanthropic Impetus to Collective Agency

### Summary
People Powered did not emerge from a single founder's vision, but from a multi-stakeholder co-creation process funded by the Hewlett Foundation and Omidyar Network. A global research and convening process in 2017–2018 identified common challenges across participatory budgeting programmes worldwide, leading to the proposal of a global hub that later evolved into People Powered. Josh Learner frames this origin as fundamentally collective — "a vehicle for collective action at a global level" — and distinguishes it from organisations that originate from top-down design.

### Detailed Explanation
Learner narrates the founding process in detail: two philanthropic foundations supporting participatory budgeting work "felt that this work was not living up to its potential because it was too fragmented around the world." They commissioned Reboot, an organisation led by Panthea Lee, to facilitate a global co-creation process using design thinking. This involved on-the-ground research in four different sites and a two-day workshop in Barcelona (2018) convening 40 global participatory budgeting experts and leaders. The collective identified that "even though every PB programme has local issues, a lot of the biggest challenges were common" — including "empty top-down mandates," communication difficulties with stakeholders, and funded projects not being implemented on time.

The 40 participants proposed creating a global PB hub. The foundations issued a call for proposals; Learner applied and was selected. He was already part of those 40 people, having founded the Participatory Budgeting Project (PBP) in 2009. The Global PB Hub launched in 2019 with a research board and a practitioner board. However, it soon became clear that the challenges were not limited to participatory budgeting but applied to "other kinds of participatory programmes." A second co-design process involving around 70 people defined the scope of a new entity with a broader mandate — what became People Powered. Learner emphasises that this origin story is not merely historical: "the real key issue for that origin was, for me at least, to bring together the people who were leading work on participatory budgeting and participatory democracy to identify what we needed, and then to create an organisation to address those needs."

### Specific Elements
- **Foundations**: Hewlett Foundation, Omidyar Network — provided initial funding
- **Facilitator**: Panthea Lee / Reboot — design thinking process
- **Key event**: 40 PB experts convened in Barcelona, 2018
- **Precursor**: Global PB Hub (2019–2020) — narrower mandate, focused on PB only
- **Second co-design**: ~70 people around the world defined People Powered's broader mandate
- **Learner's prior organisation**: Participatory Budgeting Project (PBP), founded 2009 — gave him direct experience of the fragmentation problem
- **Key insight**: Challenges were common across sites; fragmentation was the core problem

### Key Quotations
- "The original impetus for people-powered came from a couple philanthropic foundations, the Hewlett Foundation and the Omidyar Network which were supporting work on participatory budgeting and felt that this work was not living up to its potential because it was too fragmented around the world."
- "That really remains the core narrative for me of People Powered, is it's a vehicle for collective action at a global level to make participatory democracy work and work better."

## Theme 2: The Name as Identity — "People" and "Power" as Political Words

### Summary
The name "People Powered" emerged from a participatory process involving the community, with a word cloud revealing "people" and "power" as the most recurring concepts. For Learner, both words carry political weight: "people" is a moral commitment to centring human relationships over abstract processes; "power" signals that participation must shift decision-making authority toward historically marginalised communities.

### Detailed Explanation
The naming process was itself an exercise in the organisation's values: "we had a whole participatory process to decide the name for the organisation with a series of discussions and then polls to rank different name options," supported by a branding consultant. A word cloud of the ideas generated by the community showed that "the two most common words were people and power, or some variation of those." Learner describes coming up with the name while lying in bed, thinking about the word cloud and combining the two words "in a way that wasn't just around participatory budgeting and also didn't use big words even like participation or democracy, but got to the heart of that work in a way that could hopefully be more accessible and more meaningful for people."

For Learner, both words are deeply political. "People" is not merely descriptive: "it's a moral word that this work needs to be about people, both in communities, but also as us doing this work that we need to pay attention to the actual people and not to, let's say, abstract processes or concepts." "Power" has always been central to his understanding of participation: "the goal isn't just to have a good participatory process, but to actually shift power and shift power towards communities that have historically had less power. And if you're not doing that, to me, it's not successful."

### Specific Elements
- **Method**: Participatory process with discussions, polls, word cloud
- **Consultant**: Branding consultant helped narrow options
- **Rejected alternatives**: Not specified, but some used "big words" like "participation" or "democracy"
- **Implicit theme**: The name reflects a deliberate distancing from academic/technical jargon in favour of accessible, mobilising language

### Key Quotations

## Theme 3: Relationship Before Task — The Centrality of Trust and In-Person Connection

### Summary
Learner articulates a core operational principle: "relationship before task." People Powered invests significantly in in-person convenings because they build the trust necessary for effective long-distance collaboration. Virtual tools (Zoom) are useful but insufficient for deep relationship-building, co-creation, and the "recharging the human battery" that sustains distributed work across continents and cultures.

### Detailed Explanation
Giovanni observes that People Powered invests "a lot of the money" in meeting, never fully replacing in-person dialogues with technology even after COVID. Learner confirms this priority: "relationship before task. And so you need to have relationships before you can do work." In-person meetings are essential for "building the relationships of trust." He compares them to "recharging the battery, the human battery of collaboration. You need to recharge that in person so that you can go off and still invest energy in the work."

The practical value of in-person time is not merely social but functional: "co-creation work is harder to do online. You can do a webinar where you're presenting a report and getting feedback online. But mapping out people's needs and ways to address that in systems, that in-person time is really effective for that still." People Powered convenes the community every one to two years — less than pre-COVID but still a conscious investment. This principle has been present since the Participatory Budgeting Project days and continues through People Powered.

### Specific Elements
- **Frequency**: In-person convening every 1–2 years

- **Function**: Trust-building, co-creation, strategic alignment
- **Limitation of virtual**: "It's harder to build relationships by Zoom"; co-creation is harder online

## Theme 4: Anti-Extractivist Design and Structural Power-Sharing

### Summary
People Powered was explicitly designed to avoid extractivist dynamics, both in North-South relations and in relations with member organisations. Concrete structural measures include: majority board composition from the global South and women, majority funding allocation to global South communities, and a formal commitment not to compete with member organisations for local contracts. The result is an organisation that systematically redirects resources and decision-making power away from its own centre.

### Detailed Explanation
Learner describes a set of intentional decisions made during People Powered's founding to address power imbalances. The goal was "making sure that the organisation reflected the diversity of the community" through enforceable rules rather than aspirational statements. Two concrete measures stand out: "at least half of members of the board and membership need to be women from the global South" and "the majority of the funds that we allocate need to also go to those communities." Learner notes that these are "ways that are easier to follow through on and that we have followed through on."

The most distinctive measure is the commitment not to compete with member organisations: "we made a commitment at the beginning to not compete with member organisations, so that we would not do local participatory work, but rather to support the organisations and people who are already doing that." This means People Powered would not take on a contract to implement participatory budgeting in a city where a member operates. This was "a way to address the kind of anti-extractivist approaches, making it clear that we're not going to try to do your work and take your work away. Instead, to actually invest money into your work and into the space."

Beyond these structural rules, inclusion practices permeate day-to-day operations: interpretation at meetings, interactive activities, speakers representing community diversity, and ongoing learning. Learner acknowledges that "there's always more ways to do that and trying to adapt based on that."

### Specific Elements
- **Board composition**: Majority women, majority global South — enforced since founding
- **Funding allocation**: Majority to global South communities
- **Non-competition commitment**: Will not take local contracts that compete with members
- **Operational inclusion**: Interpretation, interactive activities, diverse representation
- **Anti-extractivism**: Explicit framing — "invest money into your work and into the space" rather than extracting
- **Tension**: Giovanni raises the challenge of affirmative action in culturally sensitive areas (e.g., LGBTQ in African contexts)

### Key Quotations
- "At least half of members of the board and membership need to be women from the global South. That's at least half of the majority of the funds that we allocate need to also go to those communities."
- "We made a commitment at the beginning to not compete with member organisations."
- "Making it clear that we're not going to try to do your work and take your work away. Instead, to actually invest money into your work and into the space."

## Theme 5: Navigating Inclusion Across Diverse Cultural Contexts

### Summary
People Powered's approach to inclusion — particularly on sensitive issues like LGBTQ rights — is based on creating spaces for communities to define their own priorities rather than imposing a universal framework. However, Learner acknowledges the ongoing challenge of translating collectively developed principles into concrete practice across widely different cultural and legal contexts.

### Detailed Explanation
Giovanni probes a difficult tension: how does People Powered balance its principles with the risk of being perceived as neocolonial when addressing taboos like LGBTQ issues in conservative cultural environments? Learner responds by describing the "Building Inclusive Democracies" programme, which emerged from a participatory priority-setting process where members themselves chose inclusion as a focus. The programme works with 13 member organisations across Latin America, Eastern Europe, and Africa.

The approach is facilitative rather than prescriptive: "we didn't dictate that, we created the space for that." In the programme, organisations from different contexts (Somalia, Poland, Mexico) brought different perspectives on inclusion. People Powered's role was "to help those organisations together figure out what should be done with flexibility to adapt that locally." Where there was agreement, they developed training curricula together; where there were disagreements, they respected the difference.

The harder challenge, Learner notes, comes after knowledge production: "how do you then help translate that into practise in a way that is also participatory and flexible, but doesn't lose the values?" He gives a concrete example: if an organisation works in a context "not supportive of LGBTQ communities," the adapted approach should not simply abandon those values. But the resources to support that level of contextual translation are often lacking. "Partly it's resources, just having the capacity to bring people together, to communicate clearly what should be done to support people in doing that."

## Theme 7: The Three Stages of Organisational Evolution — Gestation, Infancy, Adolescence

### Summary
Learner periodises People Powered's short history into three stages: a long "gestation" phase (2–3 years of discussions before formal launch), an "infancy" phase of rapid experimentation with different programmes, and a current "adolescence" phase where successful programmes mature and grow. Each phase brings distinct governance challenges, and the adolescent stage introduces the risk of institutionalisation — of becoming "another international organisation that delivers services" rather than a community-driven network.

### Detailed Explanation
When asked to periodise People Powered's evolution despite its young age, Learner identifies three stages. The first, "pregnancy or pre-birth or gestation," covers 2017–2019, the years of discussion through the Global PB Hub before People Powered formally launched. This phase "gave the space to process different ideas, to bring different people in, to identify what basic organisational structure and form should be."

The second stage, "startup stage for an organisation or infancy," is characterised by experimentation: "if we knew the big problem roughly, and we knew the approach and the mission, okay, what concretely do we do?" People Powered tried different interventions: mentorship programme, accelerators, digital training, research agenda, reports. Some experiments succeeded, others failed. Learner gives a revealing example: he personally voted against the mentorship programme when it was proposed, but it became "one of the best programmes" — a horizontal peer-to-peer support mechanism that is "not very expensive" and highly valued by members.

The third stage, "adolescence," sees the more successful programmes becoming mature and growing. This brings new challenges. Learner observes that this is "a common trajectory for organisations" — successful programmes create their own inertia, shifting focus from community responsiveness to programme sustainability.

### Detailed Explanation
The Global PB Hub began with individual members — a research board and a practitioner board — partly because a legal board of directors was required for the incorporated entity. Learner explains the logic: "at that stage, it became important to have organisations involved for the collective power."

The membership criteria are broad but intentional: "individuals and organisations who have been directly supporting or implementing participatory democracy for at least a year, whether they're an organisation or an individual." ## Theme 9: The European Chapter — Strategic Incorporation and Regional-Global Tension

### Summary
People Powered's establishment of a European legal entity (incorporated in Ireland) was driven by two main factors: the need to directly receive EU research funding (particularly for NETS4DEM) without awkward intermediary arrangements, and member demand for support in accessing European funding opportunities. A secondary driver, following the 2024 US election, was the need to reduce organisational dependence on US incorporation — a risk diversification strategy. The European chapter is explicitly positioned as complementary to, not separate from, the global mission.

### Detailed Explanation
People Powered joined NETS4DEM as an affiliated partner, sharing staff with ProGlobal to circumvent the challenge of US incorporation blocking direct EU funding. Learner describes this as "not the ideal relationship, having, like, long-term staff that are part of the organisation, but not part of the organisation." The European incorporation was designed to solve this structural problem: "to enable People Power to participate as a full partner, without having to kind of bend over backwards awkwardly to do that."

A second driver came from members: "one thing that we heard a lot from members, especially in Eastern Europe, is that they didn't have the capacity to apply to these funding opportunities, and that they needed more support for that." Members asked People Powered to serve as "a kind of incubator, or ultimately like a convener of member organisations, to enable them to apply for these opportunities."

Ireland was chosen after considering several countries, based on practical criteria: "cost of incorporation, language, and the kind of legal requirements… where we had people, either members, or board members, or staff." Learner clarifies that the choice was "never because of like doing work in Ireland. That was just as an administrative and bureaucratic question."

A significant additional driver emerged after the 2024 re-election of Donald Trump: "there were more threats and risks for organisations incorporated in the US. Threats of having bank accounts be frozen, of legal action. So that was an additional benefit from incorporating in Europe, to be less dependent."

Learner frames the European work within a global vision: "it was always from the spirit of, like, how can we do more together? How can we build on what's already happening?" He critiques the tendency of Horizon-funded projects to "reinvent the wheel" for Europe, arguing instead for building on existing global infrastructure.

- **Primary driver**: Direct EU funding access for full partnership

## Theme 10: The Risk of Institutionalisation — Maintaining Community-Driven Governance

### Summary
Learner's most pointed concern for People Powered's future is the risk of mission drift: from a community-driven network to a conventional NGO that sustains its own programmes rather than serving its members. This risk is inherent in the organisational life cycle and is exacerbated by success — successful programmes develop their own inertia, staff develop vested interests, and funding becomes tied to programme delivery rather than community responsiveness.

### Detailed Explanation
Throughout the interview, Learner returns to the tension between organisational structure and democratic values. In the governance evolution narrative, this crystallises as the defining challenge of the "adolescence" phase: "one of the biggest challenges I see for People Power moving forward is that… it becomes kind of another international organisation that delivers services and programmes and takes on a mentality of its own and is more staff-led as opposed to community-driven." He diagnoses this as "a common trajectory for organisations. They develop a programme, and then the focus becomes on sustaining those programmes and growing those programmes and getting funding to do those programmes."

This is not a hypothetical risk but a structural pressure that Learner observes across the field. The tension is between the very things that make programmes successful — they are what members want — and the organisational dynamic they create: "that can be good because those are programmes that people want. But I think it's a challenge just to make sure it's a kind of democratically-run organisation, that it's serving the community and not serving its own interest as an NGO."

The European incorporation, while strategically necessary, introduces additional complexity: managing a dual legal structure (US and Ireland) while maintaining coherence, transparency, and democratic governance across both entities.
