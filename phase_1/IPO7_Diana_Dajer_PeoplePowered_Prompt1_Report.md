# IPO7 – Diana Dajer (Fundación Corona / People Powered) – Prompt 1 Report

## Theme 1: Entry into People Powered – The Research Board as Gateway

### Summary
Dajer entered People Powered's orbit through the participatory budgeting (PB) research board, before People Powered itself existed. She was a PhD student researching PB and peace-building in Colombia who struggled to find global conversations on the topic. The board provided a rare space to meet the scholars she was reading, build the field collectively, and grow with the organisation as it evolved from a PB-focused network into a broader participatory democracy platform.

### Detailed Explanation
Dajer describes her entry as serendipitous: "I was struggling to find conversations that were global around participatory budgeting." A colleague sent her the call for the PB Hub that was being created. She applied skeptically—"I don't think I'm going to be selected"—but was chosen for the research board.

The first in-person meeting was transformative: "seeing in person all the people that I had been reading… people like Stephanie McNulty, who later was one of the judges in my dissertation." The board brought together "a practice board and a research board" working on mapping PB, developing a playbook, and ranking digital tools.

The shift from PB-focused Hub to the broader People Powered was, in her view, "a great idea." Since then, she has held multiple roles—collaborating on the playbook, digital rankings, courses on inclusion for PB—and describes her trajectory as "I have grown with People Powered as well."

### Specific Elements
- **Entry point**: PB Hub research board call (shared by Benjamin Goldfrank)
- **Initial status**: PhD student at Oxford, researching PB, peace-building, and technology
- **First meeting**: In-person in Mexico, alongside OIDP conference
- **Key people met**: Stephanie McNulty, Josh Learner, Brian Wampler
- **Early outputs**: PB mapping, playbook, digital tools ranking
- **Transition**: PB Hub → People Powered (expanded mandate)
- **Personal growth**: "I have grown with People Powered"

### Key Quotations
- "It was great that a colleague sent the call for the participatory budgeting hub that was trying to be created."
- "Seeing in person all the people that I had been reading… I did not know that was very important for me."
- "I have grown with People Powered as well."

## Theme 2: Organic Engagement Through Career Transitions

### Summary
Dajer's relationship with People Powered evolved organically through multiple career transitions: PhD student, founder of her own organisation, public sector watchdog role, and finally Fundación Corona. At each stage, she found a place within People Powered that matched her evolving expertise and position. This adaptability—the network's capacity to accommodate members across career changes—is a distinctive feature of People Powered's model.

### Detailed Explanation
Dajer's professional trajectory is remarkably varied: "during the pandemic, I was actually working for the government in a watchdog agency… I was thinking from other sides." After that, she "joined Fundación Corona as manager of democracy," a position she held for almost five years before recently deciding to take a sabbatical.

At each transition, People Powered accommodated her changing perspective. When she worked in government, "it was very meaningful for me, the conversations that People Powered was having." When she joined Fundación Corona, she brought the organisation into People Powered as a member: "they were interested in also being part of People Powered."

The relationship has been bidirectional. She was elected to People Powered's board four years ago, maintaining "my individual contribution" while also representing her institution. Now, in her transition out of Fundación Corona, the institutional relationship continues: "Fundación Corona is going to still be part of People Powered… Tatiana has already been part of this project from a long time."

The interviewer highlights this as a distinctive feature: "you could always find a place where to inbox… People Powered has that plurality of offers where you could insert your new changing knowledge."

### Specific Elements
- **Career stages**: PhD → Policéntrico (own org) → government watchdog → Fundación Corona → sabbatical
- **Membership evolution**: Individual → institution member → board member → transition
- **Institutional embedding**: Brought Fundación Corona into People Powered
- **Continuity**: Institutional relationship outlasts individual tenure
- **Interviewer observation**: "Plurality of offers" allows members to find their place at each stage

### Key Quotations
- "At some point I was working for the government… and it was very meaningful for me, the conversations that People Powered was having."
- "Now I'm in a double role because four years ago I was elected as part of the board of People Powered."
- "The relationship is already established and really strong."

## Theme 3: Building the Field Together – The Value of In-Person Connection

### Summary
Dajer emphasises that People Powered's most distinctive value is not just connecting people but creating spaces where they can "build the field together." In-person meetings where members can collectively set agendas, produce knowledge, and develop shared projects are what differentiate the network from academic conferences or Horizon projects. The emotional impact of meeting people face-to-face after reading their work for years was transformative.

### Detailed Explanation
The interviewer asks whether the network's appeal was the connections themselves or the events where those connections could happen in person. Dajer affirms both, but adds a crucial third dimension: "the opportunity to build the field together." She explains: "the possibility of putting together all these people that have the same interest, that besides a few academic conferences or Horizon projects every now and then, they don't have the opportunity to really meet and connect and do something together for the field collectively."

This "think and do tank" identity—combining intellectual exchange with collective action—is what attracted and retained her commitment: "that's where the main difference, because usually you connect in a conference, but the space to actually build the field in a collective agenda, that's something that was created there."

The emotional dimension is also important: meeting people she had been reading for years "was very important for me." This personal connection created a sense of belonging that persisted through her career changes.

### Specific Elements
- **Distinctive value**: Building the field collectively, not just connecting
- **Contrast with conferences**: Conferences don't provide space for collective agenda-setting
- **Think and do tank**: People Powered's self-description
- **In-person importance**: Emotional impact of meeting scholars face-to-face
- **Community building**: Shared projects, playbook, digital rankings
- **Longevity**: Personal connections sustained through career changes

### Key Quotations
- "The opportunity to build the field together… that gave a lot of value to the world."
- "Usually you connect in a conference, but the space to actually build the field in a collective agenda, that's something that was created there."
- "I did not know that was very important for me."

## Theme 4: Bridging Peace-Building and Participatory Democracy

### Summary
Dajer's personal background in peace-building has become increasingly relevant as People Powered expands into the intersection of participatory democracy and conflict prevention. She describes a recent convergence driven by global recognition that "the threat of conflict is a threat against democracy." This thematic expansion happened from both directions: network staff reached out to her, and her peace-building network reached out to People Powered.

### Detailed Explanation
The connection between peace-building and participatory democracy developed in two directions simultaneously. First, "Greta from People Powered contacted me… she was interested in starting to explore how participatory democracy could be used for peace-building." Dajer connected her with Tania Pappenholtz, "one of the main scholars in the world on participation and peace-building."

Second, Dajer's existing peace-building network—Build Peace / Build Up—had independently started exploring deliberation and technology. When Build Up learned of her connection to People Powered, "they have already heard about People Powered but they have not had the connection, and since I knew them from so much time before… they made a connection."

Dajer's analysis of why this convergence is happening now: "we're in a state of the world where now more than ever the threat of conflict is a threat against democracy. We used to take democracy for granted, and we're seeing now that democracy is not for granted anymore."

An in-person workshop in Kenya earlier that year marked a concrete step. Dajer sees this as the beginning of connecting "these two worlds" that she has inhabited separately throughout her career.

### Specific Elements
- **Staff initiative**: Greta (People Powered) contacted Dajer about peace-building
- **Scholar introduced**: Tania Pappenholtz (participation and peace-building expert)
- **Network bridge**: Build Peace / Build Up connected to People Powered via Dajer
- **Kenya workshop**: In-person event on participation and peace-building (2026)
- **Timing**: Last 6 months—very recent development
- **Structural driver**: Global perception that conflict threatens democracy
- **Dajer's role**: Personal bridge between two professional worlds

### Key Quotations
- "Now more than ever the threat of conflict is a threat against democracy."
- "The peace-building field lacks the connection that it needs with the participatory democracy field."
- "It has been an amazing opportunity of seeing how these two worlds are starting to connect."

## Theme 5: North-South Asymmetries – Systemic Constraints on Inclusion

### Summary
Dajer offers a nuanced assessment of People Powered's efforts to address global North-South inequalities. She credits the network with genuine attempts at inclusivity from the start—careful board composition, attention to gender and geographic balance—but acknowledges the deep systemic barriers that persist. Crucially, she insists that "within the global South, there are many global Norths and global Souths," rejecting a monolithic category and recognising her own position of privilege.

### Detailed Explanation
Asked about three asymmetries (North-South, paid/unpaid, gender/ethnicity), Dajer begins by noting: "it's very difficult to expect a high level of equality and inclusion when you're in a world that is not inclusive." People Powered has "wanted to be inclusive since the start," with careful attention to "women participation, global North and all that."

But systemic barriers persist: "being able to even hear about the call or talk the language." Even when inclusion is intended, the structures of global inequality reproduce themselves within the network.

Her most incisive point is about internal differentiation: "I'm from the global South and I'm a woman, but I'm also a woman from Bogota that has had a lot of opportunities that was able to study in Oxford. So I'm not the same global South person that a global South person from Medellín, from Comuna 13, who has never been able to finish high school."

On the knowledge economy, she agrees with the interviewer's concern about Northern researchers extracting ideas generated collectively by the network. People Powered's participatory budgeting process within the organisation is "a good way" to address this, but the deeper problem of "payment and retribution for collaboration when there are so many different cultures" remains largely unsolved.

### Specific Elements
- **Intentional inclusion**: Board composition, gender balance, geographic diversity
- **Systemic barriers**: Language, awareness of calls, structural inequality
- **Internal differentiation**: Not all global South positions are equal
- **Idea extraction risk**: Northern researchers taking collective ideas without reciprocity
- **Volunteer culture**: Participatory democracy field runs on unpaid labour
- **Funding constraints**: USAID cuts exacerbated inequalities
- **Unspoken expectations**: Cultural differences in how contributions are valued

### Key Quotations
- "One thing is to be founded with principles of equality… it's very difficult to do it in a world where there are so many systemic differences."
- "Within the global South, there are many global Norths and global Souths."
- "The participatory democracy field runs a lot with volunteers… that culture makes it difficult to address money talks."

## Theme 6: Political Positioning – The Wisdom of Silence on Controversial Issues

### Summary
Dajer takes a deliberate stance against expecting networks like People Powered to issue public positions on controversial geopolitical issues (Israel-Palestine, Ukraine). She argues that public positioning can undermine a network's future capacity to mediate and that back-channel dialogue is more valuable than press releases. She also expresses skepticism about online safe spaces for such discussions.

### Detailed Explanation
When asked about how People Powered handled the Israel-Palestine issue, Dajer is reflective: "when you have a global network with people from so many different places and political positions, it's very difficult to expect your organization to take a position."

She draws on her experience building a Colombian democracy alliance: "reaching a consensus on certain press releases… it's super difficult." Her conclusion is that networks with diverse membership should think carefully about whether public positioning serves their purpose.

She is skeptical about performative statements: "I'm very skeptic sometimes about strong positioning in social media when I know that social media is a mess… I prefer the work that is like back office, more about mediating and trying to find common ground." A premature public statement can prevent the organisation from "being a mediator at some point."

She does believe in internal spaces for dialogue: "it might be useful to have spaces within the organization to have safe discussions about this." But she is realistic about the difficulty: "even because I work on tech, I don't trust safe spaces online… it's difficult to have a safe space that is private and in person when you have so much diversity and difficulty in terms of funding."

### Specific Elements
- **Position on positioning**: Networks with diverse members should be cautious about public stances
- **Preference**: Back-channel mediation over press releases
- **Risk**: Public positioning can eliminate future mediator role
- **Internal dialogue**: Valuable but difficult to implement safely
- **Online skepticism**: Distrust of safe spaces online
- **Funding constraint**: In-person safe spaces are expensive

### Key Quotations
- "When you have a global network with people from so many different places and political positions, it's very difficult to expect your organization to take a position."
- "I prefer the work that is like back office, more about mediating and trying to find common ground."
- "I don't trust safe spaces online."

## Theme 7: Generational Transition – Healthy Evolution or Crisis?

### Summary
Dajer reframes the interviewer's suggestion of a "generational crisis" in People Powered as a healthy evolution. The transition from the original research/practice boards to the current structure, and from the founding generation of scholars to newer voices from the global majority, was challenging but necessary. She sees particular value in bringing together established scholars with grassroots practitioners.

### Detailed Explanation
The interviewer notes that an entire generation of founding scholars (Stephanie McNulty, Brian Wampler, Michael Menser) seemed to "eclipse themselves" after COVID, replaced by a new generation. Dajer does not see this as a crisis: "I wouldn't call it a crisis. I don't know what happened."

She characterises the transition as natural organisational evolution: "one of the main richness that can be created by these kind of networks can come through actually when new and old members meet and start to talk." The elimination of the original boards "was awkward" because they were created with a specific PB focus that the organisation had outgrown. "It was more about changes that need to happen and how change takes place."

The positive outcome is greater diversity: "that generational change has allowed more and more to bring voices that are not mainstream and from organizations that are perhaps small in certain countries." She gives the example of Éxito (Colombia), which grew from "a very local organization… to leading work on deliberation worldwide" through People Powered connections.

### Specific Elements
- **Not a crisis**: Natural organisational evolution
- **Board elimination**: Awkward but necessary reflection of expanded mandate
- **Generational value**: New members bring new voices from global majority
- **Example**: Éxito (Colombia) grew from local to global through the network
- **Mutual benefit**: Big names + grassroots practitioners = valuable mix
- **Intergenerational dialogue**: Important but not fully resolved

### Key Quotations
- "I wouldn't call it a crisis."
- "One of the main richness that can be created by these kind of networks can come through actually when new and old members meet."
- "Being able to bring big names with people that are working on the ground… that's very valuable."

## Theme 8: Transparency and Safe Spaces – The Limits of Accountability

### Summary
Dajer argues against total transparency in network governance. Not everything should be public because "people are not real when everything is so scrutinised." People Powered has sufficient transparency through its participatory budgeting process, strategic planning, and committees. The network is entering a phase where governance policies may need revisiting, but it is not a public institution.

### Detailed Explanation
The interviewer raises the dilemma that some powerful networks (like ALDA) use small committees to filter sensitive topics before they reach larger bodies where proceedings become public. Dajer agrees that "not everything should be public."

Her reasoning is practical and psychological: "people are not real when everything is so scrutinised." Transparency can inhibit honest discussion. She does not believe this contradicts People Powered's values: "I don't think that that's the spirit of People Powered, to have everything publicized."

She assesses current transparency as adequate: "it has its participatory budgeting process… it's even now experimenting with deliberation to do the strategic planning." The network is "transparent and open enough."

However, she acknowledges that People Powered is "not in the startup mode anymore" and "it might be interesting to revisit some of the policies." This openness to evolution suggests the current balance is functional but not permanent.

### Specific Elements
- **Transparency limits**: "People are not real when everything is so scrutinised"
- **Current mechanisms**: Participatory budgeting, deliberative strategic planning, committees
- **Assessment**: "Transparent and open enough"
- **Evolution**: Not a startup anymore—may need governance updates
- **Not a public institution**: Different standards apply
- **No known complaints**: "I don't know if there are cases of people that want People Powered to be more transparent"

### Key Quotations
- "Not everything should be public because people are not real when everything is so scrutinised."
- "I don't think that that's the spirit of People Powered, to have everything publicized."
- "It's transparent enough."
