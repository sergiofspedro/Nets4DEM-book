## Theme 1: From Consultant to Activist — The Genealogy of AfroLeadership

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
Charlie Ngounou's trajectory runs from computer science engineer (1999) to public finance systems designer for African governments, to leadership trainer (inspired by Goleman's *Emotional Intelligence*), to fiscal transparency activist with the International Budget Partnership, and finally to participatory democracy practitioner. AfroLeadership was founded as a leadership-training NGO, but Charlie quickly realized that "leadership is something very ambiguous — Hitler is a leader for the Nazis." He pivoted to the more technical domain of fiscal transparency, accountability, and participation, where his professional expertise gave him an advantage over typical civil society organizations. "When I get to talk about those things, I'm already an expert."

**Detailed explanation:**
Charlie provides an unusually detailed account of his professional genealogy — the technical foundations that underpin his civic work. He started as a consultant designing enterprise resource planning systems (SAGE) for companies and public institutions. He was recruited by AIMF (Association Internationale des Maires Francophones) to design public finance systems for African municipalities. His system is now used by all municipalities in Cameroon for public finance transparency and program budgeting, and at the state level by the Central African Republic and Comoros. He holds over 50 certificates in public finance from AIMF, UNITAR, and the World Bank.

This professional foundation is the key to understanding AfroLeadership's model. Unlike most civil society organizations that struggle with the technical complexity of fiscal transparency, Charlie's organization has built-in expertise. "The problem that we usually have — when we get to fiscal transparency, it is highly technical, complex for most civil society. We don't have that kind of problem."

The pivot to activism was driven by a desire "to give back to society... to not be working just for money." He initially registered AfroLeadership and funded it entirely through his company: "I didn't even know there was an economy of NGOs. I didn't know that maybe you might be part of call for proposals. My point was to bring my own time."

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Charlie Ngounou (coordinator), AfroLeadership, AIMF (France), IBP (International Budget Partnership), UNITAR, World Bank, Central African Republic, Comoros, Cameroon.
- Practices: Public finance system design; program budgeting; fiscal transparency; company-to-NGO cross-funding; self-funded activism.
- Problems: The ambiguity of "leadership" as a concept; the gap between technical fiscal expertise and civil society capacity.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**
*"I didn't even know that NGOs, there was kind of economy of NGOs. My point was to bring my own time."*

## Theme 2: The Company-NGO Hybrid — Self-Funding as Strategic Autonomy

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
AfroLeadership operates through a deliberate hybrid model: Charlie's private company funds the NGO, and his engineers are contractually required to contribute time and resources to AfroLeadership without additional pay. "When you sign a contract with me to work with me in my company, you have to work for Afro Leadership. You have to give your time and resource." This is not a temporary arrangement but a structural design choice — Charlie conceived it from the beginning, unaware of the "NGO economy." The model provides strategic autonomy: "We don't really, really need to be funded to do what we do, because what we do is actually giving our part, giving our resources, giving our time." Grants (UNICEF, US Embassy) are welcomed but not required — support for partner municipalities continues after contracts end.

**Detailed explanation:**
The hybrid model operates through spatial and organizational separation: the same building houses both the company and AfroLeadership. The engineers work on public finance consulting for the company and on democratic participation for the NGO, without additional compensation for the latter. Charlie describes a specific case: a contract with UNICEF to work with six or seven municipalities ended in December, but "we still support those municipalities. We still train them. We still do maintenance. We still do assistance. We don't let it fall."

This model creates a distinctive relationship with funders. AfroLeadership presents itself to the state not as a contractor seeking business but as a citizen initiative: "We went to the state and we said, this is our own. We are participating. We want to bring this to municipalities. So if you support this, we can cover all the countries." When funding arrives, it accelerates work; when it doesn't, work continues. Charlie frames this as a principled stance: "The issue of money, if it was money, I wouldn't be going to DECIDIM Fest every year because actually I've not won that much money with DECIDIM. I fund myself to go to DECIDIM."

Giovanni draws a parallel with his own experience in Portugal: external funding allows the practitioner to approach municipalities as a "critical friend" rather than a "seller." Charlie agrees: they would reject the platform if he appeared to be selling it, because "they don't want participation."

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Charlie Ngounou, AfroLeadership engineers, UNICEF, US Embassy, municipalities in Cameroon (25 have accepted participation).
- Practices: Contractual obligation for company engineers to contribute to NGO; self-funded conference attendance (3-4 Decidim Fests at own expense); grant-independent support continuity; institutional rather than commercial presentation to government.
- Problems: The model's sustainability depends on the company's health; the boundary between commercial and civic work requires constant maintenance.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**
*"When you sign a contract with me to work with me in my company, you have to work for Afro Leadership. You have to give your time and resource."*
*"We don't really, really need to be funded to do what we do."*
*"With grant or with no grant, we support the municipalities."*

## Theme 3: South-to-North Agency — Actively Seeking, Not Being Recruited

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
Charlie's relationship with Decidim reverses the typical North-to-South outreach narrative. "The CDIM, actually, people didn't come towards me or towards Afro Leadership. We were the one going towards the CDIM." His path to Decidim ran through his existing networks: following Josh Lerner's participatory budgeting work in New York, connecting through the Global Hub for Participatory Democracy, meeting D-CENT/D-CENT Lab at a MySociety event in Oslo, being introduced by Josh to Sana Godi (then at CONSUL Madrid), and finally discovering Decidim. Giovanni identifies an "elective affinity" — Charlie's combined identity as a tech professional and social activist made Decidim, the most technically sophisticated open-source participation platform, a natural destination.

**Detailed explanation:**
Charlie's path to Decidim began years before he ever heard of the platform. He had already worked with MySociety (UK) to build a Cameroonian version of FixMyStreet (2016-2017). At a MySociety-organized democracy event in Oslo (2018-2019), he met people from D-CENT Lab and connected to the broader European digital democracy ecosystem. Back in Cameroon, he contacted Josh Lerner, asking for introductions to Decidim. Josh connected him to Sana Godi, who was then involved with CONSUL Madrid and the D-CENT EU program.

From D-CENT, Charlie saw both CONSUL and Decidim. His choice between them was research-based: "I went on the Internet and I tried to Google it and do research to make sure we choose the one that has the greater prospect." His analysis: "DECIDEM had a community that was a bit vibrant... CONSUL was a more reserved type of platform. It was not that vibrant at the time." Factors included the active partner ecosystem (Open Source Politics, Octree in Switzerland), the number of visible instances (Helsinki, Bordeaux, universities), and the community's organizational maturity.

Giovanni identifies the "elective affinity" — Charlie's tech-social hybrid identity matched Decidim's technical community. Charlie agrees: "To go toward the CDIM, it's easy for someone who is already a tech and a social tech... If you are not a tech, and in this case a social tech, it will be difficult to get to the CDIM."

- Problems: Decidim's technical complexity — "it was not easy previously even to install DECIDIM because technically speaking, as they came from so many actors, there was a need of organizing interoperability between the modules."

## Theme 4: The Participation Gatekeeper — Technology as Leverage, Not Product

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
Charlie has installed and configured Decidim for all municipalities in Cameroon — the platform is ready on his servers. But he only activates it when a municipality demonstrates genuine political commitment to participation. "We are not selling... We are using the CDM to help participation in our context." This creates a distinctive "gatekeeper" model: technology is a lever to extract political will, not a product to be distributed. Twenty-five municipalities have "accepted" but progress is slow. Activation requires municipalities to release budget data, publish public contracts, and create conditions for citizen engagement — without these, "on what ground are we going to encourage people to be participating?"

**Detailed explanation:**
This model is a direct response to the political conditions in which Charlie operates. "We don't have real local democracy. We don't have real citizenry. We don't have the real political will for participation." The platform pre-installed on AfroLeadership's servers is not a sales asset but a political instrument — the commitment to participation functions as an entry condition. Charlie frames his work as "fighting to open doors": "You can't be fighting to open doors and then you come and sell. It won't work."

The participation gatekeeper model has a specific logic: without information, participation is impossible. "We started with building a platform for transparency, building a platform for public contract, and building DECIDIM as an end. Because without information, you can't have participation." The platforms for transparency and public contracts precede the activation of the participation platform. Extract the data first; then let citizens engage with it.

This also creates a structural conflict with Decidim's membership model. When Decidim shifted to requiring members to pay 1.5% of turnover, Charlie objected: "We don't sell the CDM. We are using the CDM to help participation in our context." His model doesn't generate "turnover" from Decidim — he spends his own money to go to Decidim Fest. The membership fee structure doesn't fit the gatekeeper model.

Giovanni characterizes this as: "You reduce the financial investment you ask to them because you prefer that their investment is political, methodological in terms of transparency, access to data. You show them that you are a co-funder somehow. So you are more a critical friend than a beggar."

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: AfroLeadership, 25 municipalities in Cameroon, Decidim Association, UNICEF, US Embassy.
- Practices: Pre-configured platform activation conditional on political will; transparency and public contract platforms as prerequisites; self-funded conference attendance; institutional rather than commercial relationship with government.
- Problems: Decidim's 1.5% turnover membership fee doesn't fit non-commercial deployment models; slow municipal uptake (25 municipalities but activation is slow); lack of local democracy and citizenry.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**
*"We are not selling... We are using the CDM to help participation in our context."*
*"We actually work as citizens, trying to break the doors of our municipalities. You can't be fighting to open doors and then you come and sell."*
*"We started with building a platform for transparency, building a platform for public contract, and building DECIDIM as an end. Because without information, you can't have participation."*

## Theme 5: The Trust Deficit — Why Social Work in Africa Demands Self-Funding Integrity

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
Charlie articulates a structural problem that shapes his entire operating model: "The first barrier to social work is that most social activities are not trusted." In Africa, social activists are widely perceived as "using us to make money." This is compounded by visible inequality: "When you see social workers coming with four-wheel-drive cars, coming with very well-worn costumes, coming with great phones, and they are coming to tell people that they are coming to help them — nobody trusts you." Charlie's response is radical self-funding: he pays for his own Decidim Fest attendance, prints his own brochures, works without external compensation. "Money is something that corrupts most relationships." His model of giving — not taking — is designed to preserve the integrity of the social relationship with municipalities.

**Detailed explanation:**
Charlie links the trust deficit to a broader dynamic: the commercialization of social work. NGOs that arrive with expensive equipment, take data ("take their pajamas"), and leave without lasting change create a cycle of cynicism. Municipalities have learned to distrust social actors. The trust deficit is not specific to participatory democracy — it affects all social work — but it is particularly acute for participation, which requires citizens to believe their input will matter.

Charlie's response is structural, not rhetorical. He doesn't just say he's not selling — he demonstrates it by funding himself. "If it was money, I wouldn't be going to DECIDIM Fest every year because I've not won that much money with DECIDIM." He's attended three or four Decidim Fests entirely at his own expense, including flights and lodging. He designed and printed promotional brochures for Decidim in French and English at his own cost. He shows these to officials — including the World Health Organization representative in Cameroon — to advocate for Decidim.

The integrity of this model is maintained by clearly separating commercial and civic work. "I try to keep what I do with my company aside. I have my contract with governments. It's okay. I work for that one. So I don't mix what I do with Afro-leadership." The company generates income; AfroLeadership receives volunteered time. The channels are "absolutely separate." When grants arrive (UNICEF, US Embassy), they accelerate work but don't define it.

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Charlie Ngounou, AfroLeadership, municipalities in Cameroon, WHO Cameroon, Decidim Fest, UNICEF, US Embassy.
- Practices: Self-funded international conference attendance; self-produced promotional materials; strict separation between commercial consulting and civic activism; grant money as accelerator, not foundation.
- Problems: Structural distrust of social actors in Africa; the "NGO economy" creating perverse incentives; difficulty of maintaining credibility while seeking funding.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**
*"The first barrier to social work is that most social activities are not trusted. When people see social activists, they think like they are using us to make money."*
*"Money is something that corrupts most relationships."*
*"I didn't get into this to make money. That was not my goal, and it is still not my goal."*

## Theme 6: The WhatsApp API — Peripheral Innovation as Gift to the Center

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
Charlie's most ambitious adaptation of Decidim is a WhatsApp-to-Decidim API, developed in partnership with Octree (Switzerland). The problem: most Africans access the internet exclusively through phones, where Decidim's interface is difficult to use. Social networks like WhatsApp are effectively free on many carrier plans, while visiting a website costs data. The solution: users submit ideas, proposals, and comments via WhatsApp, which flow directly into Decidim without the user ever opening the platform. "If we can use WhatsApp so that someone writes a proposal on WhatsApp and then it goes directly in the platform without him opening the platform, I think we will have done a very great breakthrough." The API has been tested in Tanzania and Brazil. Giovanni identifies this as "your gift to the growth of Decidim as a global platform" — a peripheral innovation that could become mainstream.

**Detailed explanation:**
The WhatsApp API responds to a set of interlocking constraints that Charlie describes in detail. **Digital literacy**: computers are expensive and rare; most people access the internet only through phones. But "the phone doesn't give you digital literacy... phone will help you to go on social media. Phone won't help you to use real serious applications." **Infrastructure**: electricity is unreliable and expensive. **Internet cost**: $1-2 per gigabyte makes website access prohibitively expensive for many users. **Carrier economics**: many African telecom providers offer free or heavily discounted access to WhatsApp, Messenger, and other social networks, while charging full data rates for website access. This creates a structural incentive for social-network-based participation.

The partnership with Octree (Switzerland) is itself notable. Charlie describes them as "great guys" who "don't ask any money. They are supporting us... in a volunteer mode." A European organization and an African organization collaborating on equal terms to solve a problem that affects both but is acute in Africa. The API is designed to capture "participatory instances" — Charlie's concept of using Decidim not just for active deliberation but as a "participatory archive" that formalizes the informal participation already happening in communities. This is a conceptual innovation as much as a technical one.

Charlie also connects the API to the Pan-African ambition: "We call it Decidim for Africa, you know, just to show you the Pan-African view of what we design." He has approached UCLGA Africa (United Cities and Local Governments of Africa) about using Decidim as a continental tool.

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: AfroLeadership, Octree/VOCA (Switzerland), Guillaume (Octree), municipalities in Cameroon, Tanzania (testing site), Brazil (testing site), UCLGA Africa.
- Practices: WhatsApp-to-Decidim API design; phone-first participation design; "participatory archive" concept; volunteer-mode international technical collaboration; Pan-African branding ("Decidim for Africa").
- Problems: Digital literacy gap; electricity and internet infrastructure; carrier data pricing structures; Decidim's desktop-oriented interface; low NGO digital adoption.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**
*"If we can use WhatsApp so that someone writes a proposal on WhatsApp and then it goes directly in the platform without him opening the platform, I think we will have done a very great breakthrough."*
*"The only way you can meet people using the CDEM is when they have their phone."*
*"We call it Decidim for Africa... just to show you the Pan-African view of what we design."*

## Theme 7: Digital Literacy and Infrastructure — The Real Barriers Beyond Language

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
When asked about language barriers — a common concern about Decidim's deployment in Africa — Charlie unexpectedly dismisses the issue. Colonial languages (French, English, Portuguese, Spanish) are the official languages of instruction and administration across Africa. "You don't go to school and you see people learning in their own language, learning how to write and read in their own language. So in my view, that's not really a problem." The real barriers are digital literacy and infrastructure: computers are prohibitively expensive, electricity is unreliable and costly, internet is $1-2 per gigabyte, and most Africans' only digital device is a phone that provides "weak digital literacy" — adequate for social media, inadequate for "real serious applications." Language, he argues, is a distraction from the deeper structural barriers.

**Detailed explanation:**
Charlie's position on language is counterintuitive and analytically significant. The standard Global South critique of Northern technology emphasizes translation and localization. Charlie inverts this: "If you give the CDIM to me in my own language, I'm not sure I will be able to use it because I don't write and read my language." Colonial languages function as the *lingua franca* of technology and administration. Translation into local languages — which are primarily oral, not written — would paradoxically make the platform *less* usable, because users were never taught to read those languages. "Even if you had 50% colonial language and 50% noncolonial, it's already a success to at least gain the 50% colonial."

The deeper barrier is digital literacy — assumed as a baseline in European contexts but absent in much of Africa. Computers are "very, very expensive." Public policies on taxation and customs "are exclusive of people when it comes to technology." Most Africans' only digital device is a phone, and the phone provides "weak digital literacy": good for social media, inadequate for "deep research, deep work, deep use of AI, deep use of platform." The screen is too small, the interface isn't designed for complex tasks, and "there are things that you don't do on phones because you know that it's best to have a bigger screen."

The infrastructure constraints compound the literacy gap. Electricity is "not cheap. And even when it is available, and it is not that available." Internet costs $1-2 per gigabyte. And the political context — "democracy is almost inexistent" — means citizens lack the motivation to overcome these barriers: "Why am I going to bother myself going on a platform to give my ideas when the mayor is not even providing information, he's not even accountable?"

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: AfroLeadership, municipalities in Cameroon.
- Contexts: Africa (Francophone and Anglophone), Cameroon, Central African Republic, Comoros.
- Practices: Colonial languages as administrative lingua franca; phone-based digital access; social network as primary digital habit.
- Problems: Digital literacy gap; computer cost and availability; electricity unreliability and cost; internet data cost; "weak digital literacy" from phone-only access; democratic deficit reducing citizen motivation.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**
*"In Africa, when kids go to school, they don't learn their local language. So whenever people say this language is not translated, I ask myself: you are in a country where no paper is translated in local languages. Why would you want digital systems to be translated?"*
*"The critical problem I have with the CDIM or with any kind of participatory technology is the digital literacy. That is a barrier to using technology."*
*"We are talking of participation in context where democracy is almost inexistent. So people will ask themselves, why am I going to bother myself going on a platform to give my ideas when the mayor is not even accountable?"*

## Theme 8: Center-Periphery — Decidim Association's Limited Receptivity to Africa

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
Charlie's relationship with the Decidim Association is characterized by one-directional effort: he goes to Decidim Fest, he does outreach, he promotes the platform, he builds infrastructure — but the association does not reciprocate. "The CDEM as the association has not really been intensely reaching out to drink from the periphery, at least from the peripheral Africa." The emblematic incident: when Decidim changed its membership structure, Charlie's logo was removed from the website without notice. "I opened the CDEM platform and I didn't see my logo. And I said, how come? And then they said, okay, the thing has changed." He contextualizes this not as malice but as resource constraint — "they are struggling. They are struggling with resources" — and as a product of the Catalan-centric culture. Giovanni diplomatically summarizes: "The capacity, not the will, but the capacity of recognition of the peripheral work is still under the level you would wish to have." Charlie agrees: "It's still under construction."

**Detailed explanation:**
Charlie's center-periphery analysis is notable for its lack of resentment. He acknowledges the association's resource constraints: "I have been to the DECIDEM Fest and I saw clearly that they were trying to look how to diversify funding." He understands the membership fee shift as a survival strategy. But the structural gap remains: he is "the only African organization working on DECIDEM, trying to promote DECIDEM," attending Decidim Fest at his own expense, designing and printing brochures, building server infrastructure, and maintaining three developers who know the platform — all without equivalent institutional investment from the center.

The organizational culture compounds the structural gap. At Decidim Fest, "I know two, three people, but I'm the one to do the outreach." The atmosphere is Catalan-European: "We are in a space where they speak Spanish for some of them." Charlie compares this to Helsinki — another peripheral European city — where "it's the same type of atmosphere. You are one or two blacks with people that are really white." The problem is not individual racism but structural homogeneity: the community reflects its origins and hasn't yet built the bridges to diversity.

Charlie's response is not to withdraw but to persist. He has already booked his flight for the next Decidim Fest. He continues promoting the platform in Africa — to UCLGA, to the WHO representative, to municipalities. "My view is that maybe years back, as I keep pushing for the CDM, we will reach more engagement, especially as we build this sort of API that might get to create some sort of massive impact."

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: AfroLeadership, Decidim Association, Decidim Fest, UCLGA Africa, WHO Cameroon.
- Practices: One-directional outreach (periphery to center); self-funded conference participation; membership fee incompatibility with non-commercial models; logo removal incident.
- Problems: Association resource constraints; Catalan-European cultural homogeneity; membership model that doesn't fit African deployment; lack of institutional outreach to Africa.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**
*"The CDEM as the association has not really been intensely reaching out to drink from the periphery, at least from the peripheral Africa."*
*"I'm the one imposing my presence in that sense."*

## Theme 9: Belonging — People Powered's Human Touch vs. Decidim's Technical Distance

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
Charlie draws a sharp contrast between the organizational cultures of People Powered and the Decidim Association. People Powered has a "human touch" rooted in Josh Lerner's authentic leadership, structural coherence between participatory methods and organizational governance, and an affirmative action charter (50% of funding to Africa/Southern continents, 50% of research funding to women). Decidim, by contrast, is technically impressive but relationally distant — a space where you know few people, where Spanish is spoken, where "one or two blacks" attend. At People Powered, Charlie feels "belonging — not to belong theoretically or procedurally, but actually more concretely, because you can vote for this, vote for that." The difference is so pronounced that Charlie has "never refused to be part of any initiative" at People Powered.

**Detailed explanation:**
Charlie's comparison operates at multiple levels. **Leadership**: "Josh is really somebody who believes in what he says, in what he thinks." The coherence between stated values and organizational practice radiates from the founder. **Structure**: People Powered's participatory budgeting for its own resource allocation — "you can vote for this, vote for that" — embeds its methodology in its governance. This creates "structural coherence" (Giovanni's term) between what the organization teaches and how it operates. **Diversity**: the charter commits 50% of resources to the Global South and 50% of research money to women — not rhetorically but structurally. "At times when you see people talking about that, they do it rhetorically. But concretely, they are not structured for that. But you see really that Josh... things are really, really aligned."

Charlie's deep engagement with People Powered reflects this culture. He serves on the board, is a member of the governance committee, serves on the Digital Participation Technical Committee, and is a mentor using his coaching skills. He attends all meetings. "I think sometimes they are even surprised." The contrast with his Decidim engagement is structural: at People Powered, he is pulled in; at Decidim, he pushes himself in.

Giovanni identifies the historical-political context that explains part of the difference: "Barcelona or Catalan is not really, really Paris. If it was Paris or maybe London, it would be more open." Catalan-centricity — the product of a specific municipalist political project — creates a particular kind of insularity that is different from metropolitan cosmopolitanism. Charlie nods to this: "They have been doing that for Europe. And they have been doing that for Catalan, for Barcelona."

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: People Powered (Josh Lerner, Greta), Decidim Association, Charlie Ngounou.
- Practices: People Powered: participatory budgeting for resource allocation, affirmative action charter (50/50), board service, mentorship, Digital Participation Technical Committee. Decidim: Decidim Fest, self-funded attendance, solo outreach.
- Problems: Catalan-centric cultural homogeneity vs. People Powered's structural diversity commitments; the different kinds of "belonging" produced by two organizational cultures.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**
*"You don't have this kind of arrogance that you find in so many organizations. There is a very real human touch."* (on People Powered)
*"You don't see that at the CDM. Even when I get there, I know two, three people, but I'm the one to do the outreach."* (on Decidim)
*"Josh is really somebody who believes in what he says, in what he thinks... There is kind of structural coherence."*

## Theme 10: AfroLeadership as Network Node — From Organization to Pan-African Hub

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**
Charlie describes AfroLeadership's evolution from an organization to a network hub. The model is explicitly inspired by People Powered: "We do like People Power does with us. We don't go to build the administration. No. We partner." AfroLeadership is positioned as "a central node of a network of partners in countries that carry out all the work that we do." The hub builds systems, products, and APIs (Decidim installations, transparency platforms, the WhatsApp API, e-learning platforms); partner organizations in countries do the local implementation. This vision extends to new domains: an "African Child Online Protection Program" launching June 16 (International African Child Day), with 90 e-learning courses and a platform to protect children from online harm.

**Detailed explanation:**
Charlie articulates a network theory of scale that mirrors People Powered's model. AfroLeadership does not seek to build country offices or become a large organization — it seeks to build technical infrastructure that enables country partners. The WhatsApp API is the most concrete example: built collaboratively with Octree in Switzerland, it creates a low-barrier entry point for Decidim adoption across Africa.

The network vision extends to institutional partnerships. Charlie has approached UCLGA Africa (United Cities and Local Governments of Africa) about using Decidim as a continental tool. He carries his self-made Decidim brochure to meetings with the WHO. He is the IODP Africa focal point for Central Africa, covering 11 countries in the ECCAS region. In each case, AfroLeadership is the technical partner, not the implementing organization.

The African Child Online Protection Program represents a diversification beyond participatory democracy into digital rights — but the network logic remains the same: build the platform, the courses, and the filtering technology centrally, then distribute through country partners. Charlie plans to integrate People Powered's playbook curricula into the e-learning platform: "That's my plan, to introduce the participatory aspect so that we can have... that's a strategic move that I have."

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: AfroLeadership, People Powered (model inspiration), Octree/VOCA (Switzerland), UCLGA Africa, IODP Africa, ECCAS (11 countries), WHO Cameroon, US-based digital rights partner.
- Practices: Hub-and-spoke network model; technical infrastructure as shared resource; partnership-based scaling; Pan-African branding ("Decidim for Africa"); e-learning platform for digital literacy and safety.
- Problems: The network model's dependence on the hub's technical and financial resources; the challenge of maintaining partner engagement across multiple countries with different languages and political systems.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**
*"We are a central node of a network of partners in countries that carry out all the work that we do."*
*"We do like People Power does with us. We don't go to build the administration. We partner."*
*"We call it Decidim for Africa... just to show you the Pan-African view of what we design."*
