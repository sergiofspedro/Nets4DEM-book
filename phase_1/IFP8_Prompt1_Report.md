## Theme 1: KNOCA as an Informal, Deliberately Bounded Network

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
KNOCA (Knowledge Network on Climate Assemblies) is an informal, project-funded network of individuals focused exclusively on improving the practice and impact of climate assemblies. It has no legal status, no institutional members, and is coordinated by Graham Smith. Its success is attributed precisely to its deliberate self-limitation — staying focused on a single methodology (climate assemblies with democratic lottery and deliberation), refusing to broaden scope or formalise legally, and maintaining a European geographic focus dictated by its sole funder, the European Climate Foundation.

**Detailed explanation:**  
Graham describes KNOCA as having been funded by the ECF for five years with the explicit mission of improving climate assembly practice in Europe. The network runs online events (9–10/year at peak, now 2–3), produces guidance documents, and curates a knowledge base. A critical strategic choice was rejecting scope expansion: when funders and community members proposed broadening KNOCA's focus beyond assemblies, Graham and others resisted. "Once you start opening up beyond one method, it's really hard to hold the network together. Everyone gets really confused about what you're focusing on." When ECF considered wider thinking about participatory climate governance, the decision was "KNOCA should be part of it, but it shouldn't be the vehicle of it." KNOCA is now deliberately reducing activity — not due to failure, but because the community it built is becoming self-sustaining. "We've done a lot of the work we feel we need to do. The community of practice is more mature and people know each other because of the role that KNOCA played."

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Graham Smith (Chair, University of Westminster), European Climate Foundation (sole funder), Extinction Rebellion (informal relationship), FIDE (training partner), UniDeLib (university assembly network inspired by KNOCA).
- Contexts: Europe (primary focus), Turkey (Izmir case), UK.
- Practices: Online events (now 2–3/year), guidance documents (permanent assemblies, knowledge curation), YouTube knowledge library, open-access materials.
- Problems: No legal status creates vulnerability — "a lot of it has really depended on me playing that role. If I'm wrong, then we're kind of screwed."

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**  
*"Once you start opening up beyond one method, it's really hard to hold the network together."*

## Theme 2: Individual Membership as Political Neutralizer

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
KNOCA deliberately rejected organisational membership. Graham fought for this: allowing organisations with logos and political identities (Extinction Rebellion Bologna, City of Milan) to be listed as members would create reputational risks and political tensions. By admitting only individuals, KNOCA created a space where "we will have a public official, a conservative public official... and someone from Extinction Rebellion, and they will be having a conversation about how best to facilitate." The network's glue is shared practice, not shared ideology.

**Detailed explanation:**  
Graham describes how the ECF originally wanted an organisational membership model with logos on the website. He refused: "I said, right from the start, I don't want any institutional memberships. We are a network of people who are interested in asking the question of how can we make assemblies have more impact?" This choice allows radical actors and conservative public officials to be in the same virtual room, focusing on facilitation techniques rather than political disagreements. The network has ~1000 newsletter subscribers and 40–120 per event, with a hardcore of 10–20 regulars handling different topics. The voluntary nature is key: "The value of the network is only the value of those people who actually turn up and make it valuable. The network is utterly involuntary."

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Extinction Rebellion Bologna, City of Milan (hypothetical conflict), European Climate Foundation (wanted organisational model).
- Practices: Individual-only membership, open events, no formal membership fees or obligations.
- Problems: Managing political diversity within a values-neutral space.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**  
*"Right from the start, I don't want any institutional memberships. We are a network of people."*  
*"If people didn't come to the events or people didn't find the materials valuable, then we would have withered."*

## Theme 3: The Turkey Case — KNOCA as Catalyst for Assembly Adoption

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
Graham describes the case of a Turkish practitioner who discovered assemblies through KNOCA, received training (with FIDE), used KNOCA materials to legitimise the approach, and successfully ran Turkey's first climate assembly in Izmir. KNOCA played a catalytic role: the practitioner "found out about assemblies by being asked to be a global [assembly host], joined KNOCA, found out about the training, used material from the KNOCA website to show that this was legitimate... If KNOCA hadn't been there, it's not clear that he would have been able to actually move from the idea to actually running an assembly."

**Detailed explanation:**  
This detailed case illustrates KNOCA's primary impact mechanism: enabling practitioners in contexts without existing assembly infrastructure to move from interest to implementation. The Izmir practitioner discovered assemblies through KNOCA's global assembly, received training from KNOCA and FIDE, used KNOCA materials as legitimising documents with Turkish civil servants, then approached Graham for connections to experienced practitioners in London. Graham served on his advisory board and arranged study visits. The case demonstrates the network's brokering function between isolated practitioners and the wider community of practice.

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Turkish practitioner (unnamed), KNOCA, FIDE, civil servants (Izmir).
- Contexts: Izmir, Turkey; London (study visit).
- Practices: Training (2-day workshops with FIDE), study visits, materials as legitimacy tools, advisory board membership.
- Problems: Without KNOCA, the practitioner would likely not have succeeded. The dependency on Graham personally (advisory board) illustrates the person-centric model's strengths and vulnerabilities.

## Theme 4: Temporary by Design — Downsizing as Success, Not Failure

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
KNOCA was never meant to be permanent. Graham is "quite surprised to still be doing this now" — the network was conceived as a 1–2 year project. Its current downsizing (from 3 days/week Graham's time to 1, from 9–10 events/year to 2–3) is framed as success: the knowledge base is mature, the community of practice is self-sustaining, and the baseline for assembly practice has been raised. "If we feel that there is suddenly need for us, we can reappear and do more work." This represents a model of network-as-catalyst: create the conditions for a community of practice, then fade.

**Detailed explanation:**  
Three factors converge in the downsizing decision: (1) the knowledge base is "pretty decent" — "we've got a huge library of events which our YouTube channel is used by a lot of people who matter"; (2) ECF's governance programme shut down, reducing funding; (3) Graham's own readiness to step back — "Part of me all the time was thinking, why am I spending all my time focusing on climate assemblies on their own?" The network's legacy is embedded in the community it created and the knowledge baseline it shifted. "People are having those conversations direct with each other rather than having to be brokered by KNOCA."

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Graham Smith (coordinator, now spending 1 day/week), European Climate Foundation (reduced funding), community of practice (now self-sustaining).
- Practices: Event reduction (9–10 → 2–3/year), maintenance mode, legacy knowledge infrastructure (YouTube, website, guidance docs).
- Problems: ECF shut down its governance programme — "I think it was a really bad idea personally." Decreased funding environment "around climate and democracy."

## Theme 5: Contingency — The Unreplicable Conditions That Made KNOCA Work

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
Graham is explicitly reflexive about the conditions that enabled KNOCA's success — conditions he believes are not easily replicable. His academic wage (University of Westminster) meant he didn't need to generate income from the network. His established reputation in the field gave him credibility with both radicals and policy-makers. A stable funder (ECF) provided five years of consistent support. "There are a bunch of really weird contingencies" — the model worked because of a specific constellation of factors that may not constitute a portable "recipe."

**Detailed explanation:**  
Graham identifies several contingencies: his academic position provided a salary independent of the network; his reputation — "I was kind of quite established in the field already and I was recognized by radicals and recognized by policy people as helpful" — gave KNOCA immediate credibility; his personal ability to "somehow manage that tension" between radicals and policy actors was unusual; the ECF's consistent funding for five years was critical; and his openness to learning — "I also am really interested in hearing from the field and learning from the field" — sustained his engagement. These conditions suggest the KNOCA model may be difficult to replicate: "I can't imagine sort of being able to kind of say, oh, you need a bit of this and you need a bit of that and you'll get another KNOCA."

## Theme 6: The Extinction Rebellion Relationship — Radicals, Assemblies, and Strategic Misunderstandings

**Theme summary (3–6 lines):**  
KNOCA maintained a complex relationship with Extinction Rebellion (XR) — Graham was involved in XR's early thinking about assemblies and later discovered "the people at the centre were reading a lot of my work." He analyses XR's strategic error: local activists asked governments to run assemblies without first securing the political commitment (2035 decarbonisation) that was integral to XR's original assembly demand. This led to local assemblies being "run on... how do we reach our net zero commitments in 2050" rather than the radical decarbonisation framing XR had intended. Graham's analysis reveals the gap between a movement's strategic theory and the practical compromises of local implementation.

**Detailed explanation:**  
The original XR idea was: government commits to 2035 decarbonisation, then citizens' assembly decides how to get there. The radical outcome was embedded in the prior political commitment. But local XR groups, acting independently, asked local governments to run assemblies without this prior commitment. Governments then ran assemblies on the existing policy framework rather than the radical decarbonisation demand. "The local activists made a mistake because they forgot that actually... the assembly demand of Extinction Rebellion was tied to a sort of an initial political commitment." Graham notes he was not surprised they were disappointed. His own relationship with XR remains collegial — he continues to engage with radical actors who now look at assemblies through a post-collapse governance lens: "they can see assemblies as being part of that radical democratic solution."

**Specific elements mentioned:**
- Actors: Extinction Rebellion (Roger Hallam, unnamed local activists), Graham Smith, local governments.
- Practices: Assembly demand as political strategy, connection between radical demands and mainstream governance mechanisms.
- Problems: Strategic misalignment between central movement theory and local activist practice; the risk of assemblies becoming depoliticised governance tools rather than radical democratic instruments.

**Key quotations (relevant for book context):**  
*"Local activists made a mistake because they forgot that actually the assembly demand of Extinction Rebellion was tied to a sort of an initial political commitment to... 2035 decarbonisation."*  
*"I think those people who are really interested in this space, who are radicals... are thinking about how do we develop post-crisis forms of governance... and for them, the idea of assemblies again plays that role."*
