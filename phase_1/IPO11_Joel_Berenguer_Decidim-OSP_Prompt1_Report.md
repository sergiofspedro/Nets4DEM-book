# IPO11 – Joel Berenguer Moncada (Open Source Politics / Decidim) – Prompt 1 Report

## Theme 1: Decidim as a Common Good – Technology Is Political

### Summary
Berenguer presents Decidim as fundamentally political technology, not neutral software. The community explicitly understands itself as political, with the conviction that "technology is political" as a core tenet. This identity shapes all governance decisions, membership boundaries, and the community's readiness to take controversial political stances—even at the cost of losing members.

### Detailed Explanation
Berenguer states unambiguously: "this community is profoundly political, and they understand themselves without any nuance… they understand themselves as this and project themselves as this." The association does not attempt to remain neutral on political issues. When Decidim issued a communiqué supporting Palestine and condemning Israel's actions, a Finnish actor (My New Tech) left the community within 24 hours. Berenguer notes this was a significant contributor that "had made very, very significant contributions."

The community's political identity is grounded in the foundational text Ciudades Democráticas: "the constant leitmotif… is the understanding that technology is political." This is "written by fire" in the collective mindset. The association acts as a "guardian of the common good," setting "the terms of the social contract, the rules of participation."

This political positioning has real consequences: it attracts actors who share the values and repels those who do not. The Chile case under President Piñera exemplifies this—when his government wanted to use Decidim to "whitewash his presidency," the community refused, stating the use was "not democratic at all, not compliant with our social contract."

### Specific Elements
- **Core belief**: Technology is political (from Ciudades Democráticas)
- **Self-understanding**: Profoundly political, no ambiguity
- **Palestine case**: Decidim issued statement supporting Palestine; Finnish member My New Tech left
- **Chile case**: Piñera government denied community endorsement for undemocratic use
- **Association role**: Guardian of the social contract, sets participation rules
- **Boundary**: Pluralism accepted but within a progressive political framework

### Key Quotations
- "This community is profoundly political, and they understand themselves without any nuance."
- "The understanding that technology is political… it's written by fire."
- "The association can bless your deployment of Decidim… this deployment uses the core software, so the social contract applies."

## Theme 2: The Organic Ecosystem – Beyond the Developer/User Binary

### Summary
Berenguer reframes the interviewer's developer/user binary as an oversimplification. The Decidim ecosystem is more organic: actors inhabit multiple roles simultaneously, and the real mediation happens within hybrid organisations that translate between technical possibilities and user needs. The ecosystem grew out of necessity rather than design.

### Detailed Explanation
The interviewer initially pictures "two communities: developers and users." Berenguer corrects this: "these two groups… they are not very shaped like that. The relation between both is far more organic and their existence is more conceptual than practical." The real dynamic involves "actors in the community, within them there's these two profiles."

Open Source Politics exemplifies this hybrid role: "we have developers but we have consultants like me that are in direct contact with the users, so we know what they want and we have the expertise in-house to translate their wishes into something that may or may not fit the interests of the community."

The ecosystem grew organically "out of need." When Open Source Politics wanted to use Decidim, they "directly contacted the association and said, hey, we want to use this, tell us how." The first client relationships and association relations developed simultaneously. Berenguer stresses: "the different interactions, the different processes that have formalized over time appeared not as something very specifically planned… it was more out of need."

### Specific Elements
- **Reframing**: Not two separate communities but a spectrum of actor roles
- **Hybrid actors**: Companies with both developers and user-facing consultants
- **Translation function**: Mediators between user wishes and community standards
- **Organic growth**: Relations formed out of necessity, not planning
- **Open Source Politics**: Started with Decidim in 2017, contacted association directly
- **Preceding software**: DemocracyOS (used before switching to Decidim)

### Key Quotations
- "The relation between both is far more organic and their existence is more conceptual than practical."
- "I think it was something very, very organic, very out of need."
- "I use the term organic… is that relations between actors flourish, and then there are some necessities."

## Theme 3: The Mediation Chain – From User Demand to Core Integration

### Summary
Berenguer describes a two-level filtering process that moderates between individual user demands and the common good of the software. First, service companies like Open Source Politics "self-moderate" user requests, translating specific needs into potentially replicable features. Second, the association decides whether to integrate developments into the core code—balancing innovation against long-term maintainability.

### Detailed Explanation
**Level 1 – Company mediation**: When a user demands something very specific ("this specific button in this specific page"), the company acts as "a first filter." Berenguer explains: "our users say, oh, I would like this, we say, hmm, no… what if we do something similar but that can be more interesting for other users apart from you." This is not self-censorship but "self-moderation"—finding the middle ground between individual customisation and generalisable features.

**Level 2 – Community decision**: The company then brings the development to the community: "hey, we have done this, will this fit? Are you interested in integrating this into the core software?" The association evaluates whether the feature serves the broader community or creates a maintenance burden: "everything that gets integrated into the main software will have to be maintained by the community and not anymore by the people who created them."

This two-level filtering is the mechanism that "deals with the complexity and the excesses of tailorization and customization" while keeping the core software coherent and maintainable.

### Specific Elements
- **Level 1 filter**: Company mediates between user and community
- **Level 2 filter**: Association decides on core integration
- **Maintenance burden**: Core integration = ongoing community responsibility
- **User expectation**: "Everybody wants to have a made-to-measure tool"
- **Company interest**: "We are a company, we need to make money"
- **Association resource constraint**: "The association has limited resources"

### Key Quotations
- "We already do this… adjusting so that we can adapt this, how we can sell this so that it matches the individual user's expectation."
- "Everything that gets integrated into the main software will have to be maintained by the community."
- "This is how the thing should work… and how it worked for a good number of years."

## Theme 4: Converging Interests – The Wisdom of Balancing Individual and Common Good

### Summary
The health of the Decidim ecosystem depends on the continuous work of converging individual organisational interests toward the common good. This is not automatic but requires skill in both aligning interests and moderating irreconcilable clashes. Berenguer compares this to the fundamental dynamic of any democratic system: maintaining unity in plurality.

### Detailed Explanation
When asked about the wisdom of the Decidim model, Berenguer identifies two key skills: "the ability and the skillfulness to make this interest converge and the skillfulness to moderate when there are irreconcilable clashes." He describes this as "a very classical… relationship scheme in any democratic system… that wants to maintain a community united in its plurality."

The network does not assign networking as a divided labour. Each actor contributes to community growth through their own prism of interests: "everybody does networking as we do networking because we bring new users into the community, but we do it from the perspective of a company… we need more clients because we need money." The association does its own networking under a different logic—institutional partnerships, community growth.

The convergence happens not despite but through the pursuit of self-interest: "every actor is working in their own interests and it's this pursuit of their own interests that converges in this growing of the community."

### Specific Elements
- **Two skills**: Converging interests + moderating clashes
- **No division of labour**: Each actor networks through their own logic
- **Company logic**: Brings clients, generates revenue, donates 3% of turnover to association
- **Association logic**: Institutional partnerships, community governance
- **Democratic analogy**: Maintaining unity in plurality is the core challenge
- **Key risk**: When interests diverge too far, community can fragment

### Key Quotations
- "It's both the ability and the skillfulness to make this interest converge and the skillfulness to moderate when there are irreconcilable clashes."
- "Every actor is working in their own interests and it's this pursuit of their own interests that brings or that converges in this growing of the community."
- "The wisdom of the entire experience is in the capacity of making converge towards the common good interests which could be normally summed in a wrong way."

## Theme 5: Competition and Geography – The Hidden Agreement Among Service Providers

### Summary
Competition among Decidim service companies is muted by an implicit geographical division of markets. Language and local presence create natural territories (France, Spain, Switzerland, Finland) that reduce direct competition. The real competition is not between Decidim providers but between Decidim and other participatory democracy software platforms like Consul.

### Detailed Explanation
Berenguer describes a market that is "not a lot of competition" because "the division has been very geographic." Different actors serve different language markets: Spanish companies dominate Spain, Open Source Politics is the main French provider, Swiss actors serve Swiss clients, and Finnish companies cover Nordic countries.

This geographic division arises from practical necessity: "you need to talk with local communities, you need to talk in their language." There are exceptions—Open Source Politics works with New York, Slovakia, and Germany—but "the main base of our clients is French."

The real competition is inter-platform, not intra-community: "when answering to a public tender, the competition happens between different softwares, not between different Decidim deployers." The common enemy is "whatever is behind" on proprietary or less adaptable platforms.

Berenguer contrasts Decidim's success with Consul (Madrid), which was less adaptable and had a less lively community: "it was not very modular, that meant that it was not very adaptable… the less adaptable the software was, the less people could use it, and the less lively the community was."

### Specific Elements
- **Market division**: Geographic/language-based (France, Spain, Switzerland, Finland)
- **Open Source Politics**: Exceptionally international (France base, but projects in US, Slovakia, Germany)
- **Real competition**: Between platforms (Decidim vs. Consul), not between deployers
- **Consul comparison**: Less modular = less adaptable = less lively community
- **Consul revival**: Supported by German institutional backing—an exception
- **Key insight**: Adaptability and community vitality are mutually reinforcing

### Key Quotations
- "The division has been very geographic… mainly because of a language question."
- "The competition happens between different softwares, not between different Decidim deployers."
- "One thrived, the other one couldn't, because one was adaptable, had already a very large community behind."

## Theme 6: The European Commission Tension – When the Guardian Says No

### Summary
Berenguer reveals a significant tension between Decidim's association and the European Commission, which uses Decidim for its citizens' engagement platform (deployed by Open Source Politics). The Commission has contributed substantial developments, but the association has refused to integrate many of them into the core code, citing excessive specificity. Berenguer considers this a missed opportunity and a sign of the association becoming too rigid.

### Detailed Explanation
The European Commission is "one of our biggest clients." The citizens' engagement platform runs on Decidim, deployed by Open Source Politics and freelance developers in permanent contact with the association. The Commission "has been contributing to the software a lot," but most developments have not been integrated into the core because "the association, the community has said no, this we're not going to integrate, it's too specific."

Berenguer sees a worrying pattern: "I think that lately the association is very, very close-minded about making the software evolve. They won't focus on maintainability. I think that this refusal risks misalignment with big users." He believes "they are losing an opportunity" and that the association "should be more accommodating."

This creates a strategic dilemma for companies like Open Source Politics. Berenguer identifies two possible responses: "the institutional battle or organizational one" (convincing through reason in community forums) and "contest by fact" (developing the modules anyway, spreading them among deployers, and pressuring the association through usage numbers). He notes that Open Source Politics, which was "in ways of disappearing" after being acquired by a non-open-source competitor, never fought this battle.

### Specific Elements
- **EC client**: European Commission citizens' engagement platform deployed by OSP
- **EC contributions**: Significant but mostly not integrated into core code
- **Association stance**: Developments deemed "too specific" for core
- **Berenguer's critique**: Association becoming close-minded, losing opportunity
- **Two strategies**: Institutional (reason) vs. contest by fact (numbers)
- **Open Source Politics fate**: Acquired by proprietary competitor—battle not fought
- **Key concern**: Big user misalignment risks community relevance

### Key Quotations
- "The association is very, very close-minded about making the software evolve."
- "I think this refusal risks misalignment with big users and I don't think this is a good dynamic."
- "We could have fought the other battle which is contest by fact… by the force of expanding the use of these modules."

## Theme 7: The Common Good – Beyond Open Source

### Summary
Berenguer makes a sharp distinction between "open source" (publishing code) and "common good" (building a community around it). He criticises European institutions for conflating the two—requiring open-source licensing without investing in the community infrastructure that makes code genuinely useful and sustainable. This misidentification, he argues, is why many EU-funded digital public goods fail.

### Detailed Explanation
Berenguer observes that many EU-funded projects mandate open-source licensing but produce "dead" code: "they put on GitHub the code, but then they forget to put together also some rules for deploying, some guidelines, some visuals." The result is code that only specialists can use.

The deeper problem is conceptual: "what they don't understand… is the dimension of common good. They don't want an open source product. What they want is a common good. And they don't formalize it." The language itself is wrong: "they misidentify the product and the language that they should be using."

The key missing element is community: "for a product to be a common good that is useful, that is used, that is worth the investment, you need a community that keeps it relevant." In "this turbo-techno-capitalist world where… things are evolving faster," a tool without a community quickly becomes obsolete.

He suggests this misalignment may stem from multiple causes: "a lack of understanding, a lack of familiarization… or a product of the conflict of interest between different institutions, different groups of interest that are lobbying."

### Specific Elements
- **Open source ≠ common good**: Publishing code is insufficient
- **EU failing**: Mandates open-source licensing without community-building
- **Requirements for common good**: Code + deployment rules + guidelines + community
- **Turbo-techno-capitalist context**: Rapid evolution makes community-maintained relevance essential
- **Possible causes**: Lack of understanding, institutional conflicts, lobbying
- **Decidim counter-example**: Community was built from the start, not as an afterthought

### Key Quotations
- "What they don't understand… is the dimension of common good."
- "They misidentify the product and the language that they should be using."
- "For a product to be a common good that is useful… you need a community that keeps it relevant."

## Theme 8: Loyalty and Exit – The Ethics of Public Disagreement

### Summary
Berenguer articulates a norm of loyalty within the Decidim community: even when disagreeing with association decisions, members do not air disputes publicly. This is not coercion but a shared commitment to "the permanence of the community as the final goal." Alternative approaches exist—some actors leave the community over political disagreements—but Berenguer's philosophy favours internal debate.

### Detailed Explanation
When asked whether he would publicly challenge association decisions he disagrees with, Berenguer explains: "I think that's more of a personal point of view… I think that Open Source Politics and me personally believe in the permanence of the community as the final goal." Public disagreement would undermine the community's cohesion.

He contrasts this with actors who have left: "I know that there are members that for less… they have given a kick to the community." The most notable example is My New Tech (Finland), which withdrew within 24 hours of Decidim's Palestine statement, despite having "made very, very significant contributions."

The interviewer notes that this loyalty dynamic is important for the research: "what you told about Palestine for us is important, because there are technical networks… that put aside their political vision because they don't want to lose any part of their supporters. So… it means that this remains somehow a political community."

### Specific Elements
- **Loyalty norm**: Internal debate, not public confrontation
- **Rationale**: Community permanence as the ultimate goal
- **Exit case**: My New Tech (Finland) left after Palestine statement
- **Contributions lost**: Significant code contributions exited with them
- **Contrast**: Some networks avoid political stances to retain members; Decidim does not
- **Interviewer's framing**: This shows Decidim is a "political community" not a neutral technical one

### Key Quotations
- "I think that Open Source Politics and me personally believe in the permanence of the community as the final goal."
- "They have given a kick to the community and we don't want to be part of the community anymore because of the community positioning themselves."
- "There are technical networks… that put aside their political vision because they don't want to lose any part of their supporters."
