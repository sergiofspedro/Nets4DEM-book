## Theme 1: From Project to Organisation – The Legal Structuring of a Network

### Summary
ALDA was born not from a founding vision but from the necessity to preserve a Council of Europe programme (Local Democracy Agencies) after its project phase ended in 1999. The founders chose a French association model (Loi 1901), transforming an informal network into a formal legal entity. For Valmorbida, this legal step was not merely bureaucratic—it provided rules of the game, accountability mechanisms, and an internal democratic structure that became the organisation‘s backbone.

### Detailed Explanation
Valmorbida recounts that ALDA is "the continuation and the structuring of a project of the Council of Europe called the Local Democracy Agencies." When the project cycle ended, the participants felt the experience should continue "not as a project but as something more stable." The chosen form was a simple French association—"Loi 1901"—with members, a General Assembly, a governing board, and a director.

The legalisation had two primary drivers: first, to sustain the programme itself; second, to give a "structured voice to the cities that were part of the project," who previously had no formal say in decision-making—the Council of Europe decided unilaterally. This inclusiveness was foundational.

Valmorbida strongly argues that "the structuring of a network in an association… gives the rules of the game," which becomes essential when "you start having resources"—questions of accountability ("How do you spend, to whom are you accountable?") force clarity. Registration answers fundamental questions: "Who are you? Where is your statute registered? To which law are you responding to?"

Crucially, she sees the legal framework as a positive enabler rather than a constraint. Asked whether formalisation risks turning a militant network into a service provider, she disagrees: "the militant part is not really connected to structure… the added value of the structure is much higher than the rest." The militant impulse lasts "one year, two years, three years"; after that, structured governance is needed "to keep it alive, so not to become a ministry."

### Specific Elements
- **Predecessor**: Council of Europe‘s Local Democracy Agencies programme (pre-1999)
- **Legal form**: French association Loi 1901 – simple, flexible
- **Motivation**: Continuity + giving cities a voice in governance
- **Key function**: Rules for resource allocation, accountability, decision-making
- **Counterpoint**: Valmorbida rejects the idea that legal form kills militancy—structure sustains it over time
- **Statute**: Described as "a very simple document for an organization very complex like us" (~40 articles)

### Key Quotations
- "ALDA indeed was born on a project. ALDA is the continuation and the structuring of a project of the Council of Europe."
- "I think that the structuring of a network in an association in our case gives the rules of the game."
- "This very militant moment doesn't last forever… it lasts one year, two years, three years. And after this… you need to do it differently to keep it alive."

## Theme 2: Internal Democracy as an Organisational Anchor

### Summary
Valmorbida considers ALDA‘s internal democratic process—General Assembly, Governing Board, Bureau—as the organisation‘s core strength. The board meets three times yearly in person and nearly monthly online. Decisions are documented in minutes that serve as binding regulations. This structure enables ALDA to navigate funding crises and political challenges, but also creates a clear hierarchy of rules: statute (hard to change), board decisions (medium), and operational adaptations (flexible).

### Detailed Explanation
Valmorbida states directly: "if there is a solid democratic process in the organization… it‘s super important. And that gives us a chance to navigate also difficult period or where funding is less." The governance has three tiers:

1. **Statute** (~40 articles): Foundational rules about membership, governance bodies, dissolution. Difficult to change but adaptable—recent changes include expanding ALDA‘s geographic scope from "Europe" to "Europe and the world" and removing the Council of Europe as a statutory member.
2. **Board decisions**: The Governing Board meets three times per year in person and almost monthly for approvals (new members, emergencies). Board minutes, duly signed, constitute binding regulations: "if someone is asking us, why did you decide this? I say this was the decision of the board with the minutes, the registered minutes signed."
3. **Operational adaptations**: Membership fees, thematic hubs, LDA recognition criteria—all set by board decision and validated by the General Assembly annually.

The Bureau (President, Vice President, Treasurer) acts as a filtering mechanism for sensitive decisions before they reach the full board. This layered structure allows ALDA to balance democratic deliberation with the need for discretion on politically explosive matters.

### Specific Elements
- **Governing Board**: Three in-person meetings/year + monthly virtual meetings
- **General Assembly**: Annual, validates fees and major decisions
- **Bureau**: President + VP + Treasurer – pre-filter for sensitive issues
- **Rule hierarchy**: Statute (hard) → Board minutes (medium) → Operational decisions (flexible)
- **Recent statute changes**: Geographic scope expansion; removal of Council of Europe as statutory member
- **LDA recognition process**: Annual board review of all 19 Local Democracy Agencies

### Key Quotations
- "If there is a solid democratic process in the organization… it‘s super important."
- "The decisions of the board are also the provisions of ALDA."
- "We changed the statute as well. It‘s not something written in stone… anything is created by man and by human being can be changed."

## Theme 3: The Hybrid Identity – Bridging Local Government and Civil Society

### Summary
ALDA occupies a unique position as a hybrid organisation: simultaneously an association of local authorities and a civil society network. Its governing board is formally split half local governments and half NGOs. This dual identity is both a source of richness—it allows ALDA to operate across institutional and grassroots spheres—and a challenge, as it sometimes confuses partners who expect a single-profile organisation.

### Detailed Explanation
Valmorbida describes ALDA‘s composition as deliberately mixed: "we have different stakeholders. So we have local authorities and NGOs which are working in the same direction… but on the other hand responding to different logics and different modalities of work." The board is "composed half by local governments and half by civil society."

This hybridity gives ALDA unique access: it is present in institutional forums (Council of Europe Congress, Committee of the Regions) and simultaneously in civil society platforms (Civil Society Europe, EPD). Valmorbida notes that "both the civil society level and the local government level know about that. They do know that we are both. And sometimes it has been a problem and sometimes an added value."

ALDA‘s relationship with the Council of Europe Congress exemplifies this bridging role. The original statute was "a sort of copy-paste of their internal rules," and ALDA still consults the Congress on sensitive political matters (e.g., Georgia). The Congress was a statutory member until a recent statute change removed this provision—replaced by an MOU and permanent observer status.

Similarly, ALDA has a "very tight MOU with the Committee of the Regions," whose representatives open ALDA‘s General Assemblies. Valmorbida frames this as being a "bridge between institutional forum somehow and also the civil society."

### Specific Elements
- **Board composition**: 50% local government, 50% civil society
- **Institutional partners**: Council of Europe Congress, Committee of the Regions
- **Civil society platforms**: Civil Society Europe, EPD
- **Challenge**: Some partners struggle with a hybrid identity—"what is this organization which is hybrid?"
- **ALDA‘s stance**: "We get along… we choose our own way"
- **Historical tie**: Statute originally copy-pasted from Congress rules; now modified

### Key Quotations
- "We have both the civil society level and the local government level… sometimes it has been a problem and sometimes an added value."
- "They do know that we are both… this is what we are. So we cannot transform."
- "In times where there were a lot of reluctance to… work in silo… we kept going. And to certain extent, it proved to be a good idea."

## Theme 4: Internal Networking Structures – Thematic and Geographic Hubs

### Summary
ALDA has developed a multi-layered internal networking architecture: thematic hubs (by area of expertise) and geographic hubs (by region), on top of the formal governance structure. These hubs are not enshrined in the statute but are established through board decisions, giving them flexibility to evolve. They allow ALDA to specialise, distribute expertise, and maintain cohesion across a geographically dispersed membership of over 300 institutions.

### Detailed Explanation
Valmorbida describes how ALDA has "over structured internally" by creating "thematic hubs" and "geographic hubs." Members are divided into these hubs to specialise and share knowledge. The hubs are not legally formalised—they exist through board decisions rather than statute—because "the statute doesn‘t talk about this; it‘s not the place to discuss this kind of thing."

In addition to hubs, ALDA maintains a recognition system for Local Democracy Agencies (LDAs)—currently 19 active LDAs that are reviewed annually by the board. "We are giving labels to 19 local democracy agencies every January. So we need to check their partners. We need to check their sustainability, if they are doing well, if they are relevant." Some LDAs have been closed; new ones are opened based on board deliberation.

ALDA has also created legally registered entities in multiple countries beyond France: "We have registered ALDA in Skopje, in Moldova, in Kiev," giving it autonomous operational capacity in those contexts. An internal company, ALDA Plus, was created for technical assistance delivery, adding another layer to the organisational architecture.

The overall picture is of a network that has responded to complexity by multiplying structures—hubs, LDAs, national registrations, a company—all held together by board-level coordination.

### Specific Elements
- **Thematic hubs**: Specialisation by topic area
- **Geographic hubs**: Regional coordination
- **LDAs**: 19 Local Democracy Agencies, annually reviewed and certified by the board
- **LDA closures**: Some closed due to inactivity or loss of criteria compliance
- **National registrations**: France (HQ), North Macedonia, Moldova, Ukraine
- **ALDA Plus**: Internal company for technical assistance delivery
- **Governance**: Hubs established by board decision, not statute

### Key Quotations
- "This networking in ALDA has been structured… we have also over structured internally."
- "The board every year decides which local democracy agencies are recognized… we need to check their partners, their sustainability, if they are doing well, if they are relevant."

## Theme 5: External Networking – The Ecosystem of Competition and Cooperation

### Summary
ALDA operates within a dense European ecosystem of local government associations, democracy support networks, and civil society platforms. Valmorbida describes a landscape of constant competition—for project funding, visibility, influence—tempered by a mission-driven approach that prioritises "the cause" over competitive positioning. ALDA navigates this field through a strategy of intentional selectivity, cross-membership, and MOUs.

### Detailed Explanation
Valmorbida maps the ecosystem: in Brussels, ALDA is "very present in all the possible mapping of these stakeholders… well known in the EU level, referees." The organisation pays particular attention to Italy, France, Spain, and the Balkans.

Competition is described as constant: "competition exists. And we are constantly in competition. Both with our partners." Her colleague Marco spends "half of his time to build consortium"—deciding which partners to include in project proposals is a strategic skill.

Valmorbida distinguishes ALDA from more rigid organisations like the Council of European Municipalities and Regions (CMR) and United Cities and Local Governments (UCLG). These organisations, she says, are "more rigid" and have a narrower instrument set—primarily representation and advocacy—while ALDA has "an armada of instruments" (projects, LDAs, technical assistance, multiple legal registrations). The CMR recently lost its EU operating grant, which Valmorbida views as a consequence of rigidity.

The antidote to destructive competition is mission-focus: "what guides us and helps us from this war of competition… is somehow, let‘s try to think about the cause. If we completely forgot about the cause… then you enter in a loop of competition which is just crazy." This cause-driven approach guides partner selection and consortium-building.

ALDA also practises external networking through "cross memberships" and "memorandum of understanding" with organisations that can "bring our voice into a certain direction." Valmorbida summarises: "networking is, I would say, 99% of the existence of ALDA."

### Specific Elements
- **Key ecosystem actors**: CMR (more rigid, lost EU grant), UCLG (political/advocacy), Assembly of European Regions, CRPM
- **ALDA‘s differentiation**: Broader instrument set, hybrid nature, project-driven
- **Consortium-building**: Marco (development colleague) spends 50% of time on this
- **Cross-membership**: ALDA is member of other networks (Civil Society Europe, EPD)
- **MOUs**: Formalised cooperation with select organisations
- **Mission-driven competition**: "if you are driven by the cause… then everything becomes more clear"
- **Geography**: Focus on Italy, France, Spain, Balkans; growing in Mediterranean and Africa

### Key Quotations
- "Competition exists. And we are constantly in competition."
- "They have been more rigid… they have less instruments."
- "If we completely forgot about the cause… then you enter in a loop of competition which is just crazy."

## Theme 6: Ethical Boundaries and Political Risk Management

### Summary
ALDA operates in highly politicised environments and has developed explicit red lines—countries and partners it refuses to work with—alongside sophisticated risk management practices. These include intelligence screening in high-risk countries, Bureau-level filtering of sensitive membership decisions, and a "do no harm" principle that prioritises local partners‘ safety over organisational ambitions.

### Detailed Explanation
Valmorbida describes a series of politically charged decisions. On **Israel**, ALDA had to sever ties despite internal debate: "our governing board was a little bit naive… we had to present them the reality of fact that that was a no-go, because we were working with Lebanon, we were working in Algeria, we were working in Tunisia." The choice was presented as a zero-sum game: "either you are with one part or with another part."

On **Russia**, ALDA would like to support opposition activists but cannot: "in the case of Alda, it will be a suicide, because we work so much in Ukraine that it‘s a suicide even to publish a post." Despite personal sympathy for the cause, organisational risk to the entire network prevents engagement.

Risk management has become a significant part of Valmorbida‘s role: "I spend 50% of my time to go around… these potential bombs." She recounts a current case—the Mayor of Chisinau seeking ALDA membership—that triggered intelligence gathering about his pro-Russian affiliations and Schengen ban. For four high-risk countries (Ukraine, Moldova, Turkey, and another), ALDA contracts an intelligence company for partner screening: "we ask an intelligence company to check who they are."

The Bureau plays a key role as a pre-filter. Sensitive issues are discussed among the President, VP, and Treasurer before reaching the Governing Board, to avoid premature public exposure: "the most delicate thing… any political process has a preparation phase."

### Specific Elements
- **Red lines**: Israel (working with Lebanon/Tunisia/Algeria makes it impossible), Russia (Ukraine conflict makes engagement suicidal)
- **Risk screening**: Intelligence company contracted for Ukraine, Moldova, Turkey
- **Bureau filter**: President + VP + Treasurer pre-discuss sensitive cases
- **Current case**: Mayor of Chisinau membership application – risk assessment ongoing
- **Do no harm**: ALDA will not put local partners in danger for the sake of values
- **Georgia example**: EU call on LGBT rights backfired politically by being poorly timed
- **Ethical charters**: Child protection, anti-corruption, anti-harassment; one member expelled for corruption, one board member sanctioned for harassment

### Key Quotations
- "In the case of Alda, it will be a suicide… because we work so much in Ukraine that it‘s a suicide even to publish a post."
- "I spend 50% of my time to go around… these potential bombs."
- "We need to have the sentiment about how to do this… the elaboration is made before with some people, together."

## Theme 7: Gender Balance – Recognition of a Structural Weakness

### Summary
Valmorbida candidly acknowledges that ALDA has a gender imbalance in its political leadership: the Governing Board has fewer women than men. Despite a gender charter and ethical codes, there is no written rule mandating gender balance in board elections. This contrasts sharply with the staff, which is 80% female—a pattern Valmorbida recognises as a broader societal issue in local government representation.

### Detailed Explanation
When asked about gender rules, Valmorbida lists existing instruments: "several ethical charters" covering child protection, corruption, harassment. An abuse of power case involving a board member was addressed through these mechanisms. But on gender balance specifically: "I think that in our governing board today, we have fewer women than men… it‘s not written in the rule of procedures."

She has personally intervened to push female candidates: "I pushed women candidates to the board because we realized that there were really too few." But the structural issue persists.

She acknowledges the irony that the staff is "80% women" while leadership is male-dominated: "it‘s not compensating because the political level is something and the staff is something else." She agrees with the interviewer that this is a common problem in city networks—"you have 99% of women workers and the director is a man"—reflecting the broader male-dominated culture of local government.

### Specific Elements
- **Board gender ratio**: More men than women currently
- **Written rules**: No legal requirement for gender balance in board elections
- **Ethical charters**: Exist for harassment, corruption, child protection—but not specifically gender balance
- **Staff composition**: ~80% women
- **Personal intervention**: Valmorbida actively recruited female board candidates
- **Broader pattern**: City networks inherit the male-dominated leadership of their member cities

### Key Quotations
- "It‘s not written in the rule of procedures. And the candidates were mostly men. So that‘s something which is visibly painful in our organization."
- "It‘s not compensating because the political level is something and the staff is something else."
- "In the association, you have 99% of women workers and the director is a man."

## Theme 8: Global South Engagement – Anti-Colonial Praxis and Value-Based Expansion

### Summary
ALDA‘s expansion beyond Europe—into the Mediterranean, Africa, and beyond—is guided by a deliberate anti-colonial approach. Valmorbida insists on demand-driven engagement: ALDA supports what local partners want to do, not what ALDA thinks they should do. The European value framework remains foundational, but it is offered as a shared horizon rather than imposed. This creates tensions around sensitive issues like LGBTQ+ rights, where ALDA prioritises partner safety over advocacy.

### Detailed Explanation
ALDA‘s geographic expansion is periodised: Balkans until 2004, Eastern Europe from 2006, Mediterranean from 2009–2010. The most recent phase is engagement with Africa and other continents. Valmorbida frames this as value-based rather than accession-driven: "in Senegal, you don‘t have the narrative of Europe. Europe remains as a value… respect of minorities and differences is important."

On sensitive cultural issues, Valmorbida articulates a careful "do no harm" principle: "we cannot put our partners there in danger. So, do not harm our partners. We need to support what they want to do and not what we want to do." This means ALDA does not impose LGBTQ+ agendas but responds to local demand: "I‘m not going to land with my plane and I go into a super five-star hotel in Dakar and I deliver my speech and I leave."

She critiques EU approaches that have been counterproductive—notably a Georgia call for proposals focused exclusively on LGBT rights timed close to national elections, which was weaponised by anti-European forces. "This call has been used for demonizing the European presence in Georgia… they said, look, this is what Europe wants to do with us."

### Specific Elements
- **Expansion phases**: Balkans → Eastern Europe → Mediterranean → Africa/global
- **Value framework**: European democratic values, not EU accession narrative
- **Anti-colonial approach**: Demand-driven, partner-led, not imposed
- **LGBTQ+ example**: Turkey project had to hold events in embassies for safety; Senegal demand-led
- **Georgia critique**: EU call on LGBT rights backfired due to poor timing and narrow focus
- **Principle**: "We need to take care of the political dimension, to help the people how they want to be helped"

### Key Quotations
- "I‘m not going to land with my plane and I go into a super five-star hotel in Dakar and I deliver my speech and I leave. These people are there. They live there."
- "We need to take care of the political dimension, to help the people how they want to be helped, and not perceive our wish as their wish."
- "We are strengthening this value-based approach… creating an environment which is navigating in the same types of values."

## Theme 9: The European Partnership for Democracy (EPD) – A Practitioner Platform

### Summary
ALDA has been a member of EPD for approximately ten years, and Valmorbida served as its chair for six years. She values EPD as a unique space where democracy support practitioners from different fields—local governance, media, political parties, parliaments—meet as peers. The multi-thematic composition allows EPD to respond to democracy challenges from multiple angles, and Valmorbida advocated for EPD to work within Europe as well as externally.

### Detailed Explanation
Valmorbida describes joining EPD when it was "going through a total restructuring… a Dutch NGO that was then brought to Brussels." She saw its value as "an environment of practitioners… the practitioners‘ support for democracy." As chair, she helped shape EPD‘s identity as multi-thematic and practice-oriented, intentionally distinct from a think-tank.

The original core was political parties (with NIMD of Finland), and ALDA contributed the local democracy angle. Over time, EPD assembled "a group of recognized practitioners, special on different facets, to be able to respond to challenges in the world."

A distinctive feature was that board members attended as organisational directors, not delegates: "we have never delegated our role as board members to others. So I met director of this, director of this, director of this… we were talking among peers." Valmorbida notes this has changed as EPD has grown larger.

She actively supported EPD‘s shift toward working within Europe when democratic backsliding became evident: "I was one of the supporters to say yes, because if it‘s needed, then yes." She argues this is also a credibility issue: "if you say, okay, Europe, we don‘t care, but we are going to work on democracy support in Kenya, and they tell you, but look, what do you do at home?"

### Specific Elements
- **Membership duration**: ~10 years
- **Valmorbida‘s role**: Chair for 6 years
- **Original form**: Dutch NGO, restructured as Brussels-based partnership
- **Initial focus**: Political parties (NIMD), later expanded
- **ALDA‘s contribution**: Local democracy angle
- **Peer model**: Directors attended board meetings personally
- **EU internal work**: Valmorbida advocated for EPD to work within Europe
- **Credibility argument**: "if you don‘t do it at home… you want to preach here what to do"

### Key Quotations
- "We have never delegated our role as board members to others… we were talking among peers."
- "If you say, okay, Europe, we don‘t care, but we are going to work on democracy support in Kenya, and they tell you, but look, what do you do at home? And you say nothing… It‘s not credible."
- "It was an extremely high value group of people, discuss, very powerful."

## Theme 10: Periodisation of ALDA – Five Macro-Phases

### Summary
Valmorbida, who has written a book on ALDA‘s 30-year history, periodises the organisation into five macro-phases: (1) the pre-ALDA Council of Europe project, (2) the Balkans-only phase (until 2004), (3) European enlargement and Eastern expansion (2004–2009), (4) Mediterranean expansion and consolidation (2010–2020), and (5) the post-Ukraine war period (2022–present). Each phase reflects a geographic expansion and a corresponding increase in organisational complexity.

### Detailed Explanation
**Phase 1 – Before ALDA (until 1999)**: The Local Democracy Agencies programme of the Council of Europe. A project, not an organisation.

**Phase 2 – Balkans only (1999–2004)**: ALDA "was only in the Balkans for five, six years, working with the local democracy agencies." The 2003 Thessaloniki Summit, where the Balkans began the EU enlargement process, marked a transition.

**Phase 3 – European enlargement (2004–2009)**: ALDA expanded beyond the Balkans, supporting EU enlargement in Poland, Latvia, Lithuania, and beginning Eastern work (Georgia, 2006).

**Phase 4 – Mediterranean and consolidation (2010–2020)**: Members requested work in the Mediterranean area. This decade saw "a big consolidation of all those dimensions, including a growth of our organization and membership ideas."

**Phase 5 – Post-Ukraine war (2022–present)**: "The war in Ukraine… started a new period of our organization." It required "a focus there, challenges, etc., where we have also organically restructured a bit." The war affected not just Ukraine programming but the entire European geopolitical context—"the weaponization of words, the propaganda… the weapon backs to Europe."

### Specific Elements
- **Source**: Valmorbida wrote a book on ALDA‘s 30-year history
- **Phase 1**: Pre-ALDA (Council of Europe project)
- **Phase 2**: Balkans only (1999–2004)
- **Phase 3**: EU enlargement + Eastern expansion (2004–2009)
- **Phase 4**: Mediterranean + consolidation (2010–2020)
- **Phase 5**: Post-Ukraine war (2022–present)
- **Key transition**: Each phase corresponds to geographic expansion and organisational growth
- **Current challenges**: Polarisation, propaganda, geopolitical risk management

### Key Quotations
- "There is a period before ALDA… the program of the local democracy agencies."
- "The last watershed is the war in Ukraine, which… started a new period of our organization."
- "Ukraine, it means also the whole dynamics in the EU, the positioning of some organizations, the work in the East in general… the weaponization of words, the propaganda."
