**Chapter 4**

**Navigating multiplicity**

**1. Types of networking efforts: which focus?**

As already mentioned in the introduction, in this volume our attention will not go to all forms of networking, but specifically to the cluster of those whose identity and DNA are specifically related to the goal of promoting, defending and deepening democracy. To reduce the potentially vast universe of networks concerned to a manageable and analytically coherent field of inquiry, this book focuses on organisations that simultaneously display four characteristics. None of these characteristics is particularly original when considered in isolation; rather, the analytical value of the selected cases lies in their combination, which remains comparatively underexplored in the existing literature. The organisations considered in this volume, therefore, combine the four following features:

1. A transnational scope, operating primarily at the European level while maintaining an active presence and partnerships beyond Europe, thus facilitating exchanges across continents and between different democratic traditions.
2. A clear democratic mission, explicitly oriented towards strengthening democratic governance by promoting, supporting, or disseminating participatory and deliberative practices, whether through promoting and supporting direct experimentation and capacity-building activities, or creating spaces of exchange and cross-fertilisation for the development, refinement, and circulation of methodologies.
3. A multi-actor composition, bringing together more than one single typology of homogeneous actors (local and supra-local governments, civil society organisations, universities and other research-driven or educational institutions, practitioners, international organisations, and, where appropriate, private-sector actors). Such networks mobilise complementary knowledge, resources, and institutional capacities to co-create responses to complex societal challenges that no single organisation could effectively address in isolation.
4. Networking is their core organisational function. Rather than merely using networks as instruments for implementing projects or advocacy campaigns, these organisations exist primarily to connect actors, circulate practices, and generate collaborative learning; thus, to build, maintain, and activate relationships among their members. They function as platforms for mutual learning, the circulation of practices and ideas, peer exchange, and the generation of collaborative initiatives, making networking itself their principal mode of action.

In the last two decades a large number of networking efforts has been devoted to create and consolidate networks and other type of organizations with a strong (although not prevalent) networking component. Many could be very interest objects of analysis, and would deserve attention for several reasons: for the innovative methods they use to threat their primary mission-object, for the extension of their impacts, for the size and rhythm of their growth or for the coherence between they act externally and they organize their functioning and internal governance, among other reasons.

To appreciate the breadth and diversity of the field—and to understand the difficult choices the authors had to make in delimiting the object of this study—it is useful to briefly discuss four clusters of networks that, although highly relevant to the themes addressed in this book, do not simultaneously satisfy the four selection criteria outlined above. Had the need for analytical coherence and feasibility been less pressing, each of these clusters could have constituted a worthwhile object of study in its own right.

The purpose of the overview that follows not to provide an exhaustive inventory, but to position the cases analysed in this volume within a broader ecology of democratic networks. Doing so highlights both the richness of the field and the analytical specificity of the organisations selected for closer examination.
