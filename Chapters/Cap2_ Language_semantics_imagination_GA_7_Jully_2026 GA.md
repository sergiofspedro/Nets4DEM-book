**Networking as a Common Good for Democracy: a Pattern-Based Exploratory Analysis**

**Chapter 2**

**Language, Semantics and Political Imagination**

**1. Magic words? A premise on performative languages**

This book opens with a clarification of the key terms appearing in its title, as they help frame the authors' position on a number of concepts that underpin the reflections developed throughout the volume and also shape their everyday work as academics and practitioners. The four key concepts that deserve a brief introduction — because the meanings attributed to them in this book are closely connected to the fifth term, Democracy, which appears in the title — are Networking, Commons, Pattern-Based Analysis, and Exploratory Analysis.

By "magic words" we do not refer to empty buzzwords, but to concepts whose performative power lies precisely in their capacity to mobilise collective imaginaries and political action. They are "magic words" not only because they attract and persuade through the promises they evoke, but also in the deeper sense discussed by scholars such as Paul Blokker and John Boswell[[1]](#footnote-0) in relation to the vocabulary of participatory and deliberative democracy. Their work invites us to unpack these terms in order to understand how they generate expectations of empowerment and resonate with politicians, public institutions, and civic actors alike.

Indeed, these concepts possess an unusually strong conceptual and political force because they do not merely describe reality—they also evoke horizons that transcend it, helping to bring those horizons into being by shaping how actors understand cooperation, legitimacy, and collective action. Each condenses an entire normative vision into a single expression, making it persuasive, portable, and capable of connecting different audiences.

This understanding is consistent with the reflections developed by several authors[[2]](#footnote-1), included the Swiss linguist Lorenza Mondada in *Décrire la ville* (2000). Drawing on her studies of urban discourse and participatory approaches to public policy[[3]](#footnote-2), Mondada argues that neither individual words nor more elaborate descriptions of objects, places, or social dynamics constitute neutral acts of observation. Rather, they function as context-dependent performative practices: they help construct the very realities they describe by making them intelligible through interaction, instead of merely reflecting an external world that already exists independently of language.

Adopting this constructivist perspective, we argue that narrating networking experiences aimed at deepening democracy — particularly at a time of profound political, social, technological, and environmental transformation — goes well beyond describing existing realities. It becomes a performative practice that contributes to the production of knowledge while simultaneously generating new maieutic questions about networking itself. In much the same way that cities become more intelligible through the ways in which they are described, narrated, and textually organised in Mondada's work, networks also become intelligible through the stories told about them. Description, therefore, does not simply mirror reality; it actively participates in shaping it.

This explains why, in Chapter XXX, we adopt a narrative approach grounded in interviews and participant observation. These methods are understood not simply as tools for documenting reality, but as means of co-framing and, to some extent, co-transforming it, rendering certain dimensions more visible, discussable, and actionable while seeking to understand them[[4]](#footnote-3). From this perspective, the organisation of language possesses an important ordering capacity and an “organizing power” and is part of the social production of the central object of this book, not just of its representation. Words do not merely represent one of the objects of this book; they participate in its social production.

For this reason, clarifying the meanings attributed to the central concepts employed throughout this volume—concepts that are far from being neutral labels—is both an intellectual necessity and an act of respect towards the reader and towards the phenomena under investigation. This is particularly true from a commoning perspective, in which language matters not only because the field itself is conceptually contested, but also because participation and co-production—of plans, practices, institutional arrangements, interpretative frames, and meanings—constitute fundamental ethical and epistemological dimensions of both democratic practice and transformative action.

**2. About networking**

Speaking of networks refers to a set of relationships or connections among people, organisations, devices, or groups—that is, to an object, a structure or a system that, however dynamic it may be, possesses identifiable characteristics and dimensions that can be described with relative objectivity. By contrast, networking refers to something broader: the ongoing practice of creating, maintaining, activating, and nurturing relationships, exchanging information, and sustaining social ties.

If a network is the outcome, networking is the process—the relational work through which patterns of connection are created, strengthened, reconfigured, and continuously adapted. This distinction explains why the title of this book privileges the latter term. Our interest lies less in describing the relatively stable forms that networks assume than in understanding the practices through which connections are established, sustained, transformed, and made meaningful. This distinction becomes even more significant once networking is interpreted through the lens of commoning, where relationships are valued not only as strategic assets but also as constitutive elements of collective action and democratic production.

A glance at the major English dictionaries confirms this distinction[[5]](#footnote-4). The noun *network* is generally defined in relatively neutral terms, referring to a group of people or organisations connected to one another, or to a system of interconnected elements working together. The Cambridge Dictionary goes a step further by describing it as "a large system consisting of many similar parts that are connected together."

Indeed, the definitions of networking (a gerund tense indicating an action in progress) are considerably less neutral. Most dictionaries describe it as the exchange of information or services among individuals, groups, or institutions (…) and the cultivation of productive relationships for employment or business purposes”[[6]](#footnote-5), or "the activity of meeting people who might be useful to know, especially in your job" [[7]](#footnote-6). In contrast to this predominantly instrumental interpretation, Dictionary.com defines networking as "a supportive system of sharing information and services among individuals and groups having a common interest"[[8]](#footnote-7). This definition shifts the emphasis towards reciprocity and mutual support, opening the possibility of understanding networking not merely as strategic relationship-building but also as a practice inspired by solidarity and mutual aid[[9]](#footnote-8).

Looking beyond English further enriches the concept. In French, terms such as *réseautage*, *mise en réseau*, and *réseauter* are generally associated with a rather utilitarian understanding of networking[[10]](#footnote-9). Italian, by contrast, offers the expression *fare rete* ("to make a network"), which most dictionaries associate with sharing information, resources, and opportunities in pursuit of common goals[[11]](#footnote-10); only occasionally do they emphasise the more instrumental dimension of establishing useful contacts. German expressions such as *Vernetzung* and *Netzwerken* foreground interconnection and systemic interdependence, highlighting the construction of durable frameworks of cooperation[[12]](#footnote-11), although the verb *Netzwerken* may sometimes carry a slightly strategic or instrumental connotation. Spanish, which usually renders the concept through periphrastic expressions such as *trabajo en red* or *crear redes*, tends to stress horizontal collaboration oriented towards shared objectives rather than the mere accumulation of contacts[[13]](#footnote-12).

Other linguistic traditions add further nuances. The Finnish term *verkostoituminen*, which could be translated as "becoming networked," implicitly describes networking as a process through which actors become embedded within broader relational structures and it implies the transformation of an actor. Dutch term “Netwerken similarly highlights continuity and maintenance rather than the mere initiation of contacts, while Swedish (*nätverkande*) places particular emphasis on reciprocity and the creation of trust-based relationships[[14]](#footnote-13).

These examples are admittedly only a small sample of the remarkable cultural diversity surrounding the concept of networking, but they become relevant altogether, and especially for this book, that focus on networking experience which are “transnational” in nature, and – thus – have to cope with an important multi-lingual and pluri-cultural dimension. In fact, they illustrate the fluid and evolving character of an ever-changing concept – like networking - in constant potential flux. If we integrate these different linguistic nuances, networking may be understood as the deliberate process of creating, entering, structuring, and maintaining reciprocal relationships among actors, fostering trust and reciprocity in order to promote cooperation, coordination, mutual support, collective action, or, in some cases, more explicitly instrumental objectives within broader systems of interdependence.

This understanding resonates strongly with the literature on Social Network Theory, which has examined, particularly since the 1970s, the combinations of time, emotional intensity, intimacy, and reciprocal services that shape interpersonal ties and determine their strength and durability[[15]](#footnote-14). As will be discussed in greater detail in the following paragraphs, these studies largely confirm what many languages already suggest through common usage: networking is far more than simply meeting people. It also requires several other complementing actions, as it involves positioning oneself within relational structures, gaining access to non-redundant information, distributing informational advantages, negotiating trust and power, and establishing explicitly horizontal forms of cooperation.

Social Network Theory has likewise conceptualised networks as open structures capable of continuous expansion[[16]](#footnote-15), integrating new nodes as long as they are able to communicate through shared codes and relational logics. Building upon this perspective, we argue that any definition of networking should also incorporate a dimension of intentionality. Networking involves the conscious adoption of relational and organisational strategies that both operate within—and potentially transform—the dynamics of inclusion, exclusion, communication, and power[[17]](#footnote-16) embedded in network architectures.

Indeed, although some authors primarily emphasise the strategic dimensions of networking — its capacity to generate brokerage positions, informational advantages, or favourable structural locations[[18]](#footnote-17) — others have convincingly shown that networking is not merely an instrumental activity. Rather, it contributes to the construction of identities, the formation of social order, and the generation of different forms of social and relational capital[[19]](#footnote-18).

Taken together, these different layers of reflection suggest a richer conceptualisation of networking as the dynamic construction and maintenance of relational structures composed of interconnected actors embedded within patterned ties that vary in strength, reciprocity, durability, and strategic positioning. Through these relational structures, actors gain access to informational, social, symbolic, and political resources; coordinate collective action; and contribute to the reproduction—or transformation—of institutional arrangements, power relations, and broader social fields.

**3. About “commons”, “commoning” and exploring patterns**

The choice of the opening words in this book’s title stems from the observation that, over the past decade, the growing body of scholarship on the commons has increasingly intersected with analyses of the networks that, across diverse sectors and territorial scales, enable commons to emerge, evolve and fulfil their functions. It is therefore necessary to clarify the conceptual meaning that the authors attribute to the expression *common good* when referring to processes of network formation and diffusion specifically aimed at defending and strengthening democratic values.

Since Elinor Ostrom’s seminal studies[[20]](#footnote-19) the understanding of the commons has progressively evolved from referring primarily to shared natural resources towards encompassing broader forms of collectively produced and governed social value. More recent contributions have expanded this perspective by arguing that commons should be understood not merely as resources, but as dynamic social processes — or practices of *commoning* — through which communities continuously create, maintain and reproduce shared goods while constantly redefining the interpretative frameworks through which those goods acquire meaning.[[21]](#footnote-20) From this perspective, commons are defined not by ownership but by the relationships, governance arrangements and collaborative practices that enable collective value creation. As historian Peter Linebaugh famously observed, "there is no commons without commoning", emphasising that the essence of the commons lies in the ongoing "acts of mutual support, conflict, negotiation, communication, and experimentation"[[22]](#footnote-21) required to sustain it.

This understanding provides a particularly fruitful theoretical lens through which to examine transnational collaborative networks and the efforts undertaken by civil society actors to establish, nurture and sustain them over time. As anticipated in the opening section of this chapter, our interest does not lie in considering these networks simply as organisational structures or institutional partnerships. Rather, we approach them as spaces in which diverse actors collectively produce and steward shared knowledge, democratic practices, relational capital and collective capacities that benefit a community extending well beyond any individual organisation or participant. It is precisely this continuous process of collective production, learning and governance that justifies interpreting transnational networking not merely as an instrument for cooperation, but as a form of common good emerging through ongoing practices of commoning.

As David Bollier argues in his book *Think Like a Commoner*, focusing on the common good is not simply a matter of imagining a "social system for the long-term stewardship of resources that preserves shared values and community identity (and) generates value in ways that are often taken for granted — and often jeopardized by the Market/State". It also requires recognising that "our collective wealth includes the gifts of nature, civic infrastructure, cultural works and traditions and knowledge”[[23]](#footnote-22). Consequently, "*The commons is not a resource*. It is a resource *plus* a defined community and the protocols, values and norms devised by the community to manage needed resources", since "commons arises whenever a given community decides it wishes to manage a resource in a collective manner, with special regard for equitable access, use and sustainability". Likewise, the concept of *commoning[[24]](#footnote-23)* encompasses "the social practices and norms that help a community manage a resource for collective benefit (that) naturally vary from one commons to another because humanity itself is so varied".

From this complexity, Bollier concludes:

"[…] There is no “standard template” for commons, just shared patterns and principles. The commons must be understood, then, as a verb as much as a noun. A commons must be animated by bottom-up participation, personal responsibility, transparency and self-policing accountability (…) The contemporary struggle of commoners is to find new structures of law, institutional form and social practice that can enable diverse sorts of commons to work at larger scales; to protect their resources from market enclosure; and to ensure the generative power of their commons. New commons forms and practices are needed at all levels — local, regional, national and global — and there is a need for new types of federation among commoners and new linkages between different tiers of commons. Transnational commons are especially needed to help align governance with ecological realities and serve as a force for reconciliation across political boundaries. To actualize the commons and deter market enclosures, we need innovations in law, public policy, governance, social practice and culture. All of these endeavors will give rise to a very different worldview than the one that now prevails in established governance systems, particularly those of the State and Market"[[25]](#footnote-24).

This reflection helps introduce many of the key concepts embedded in the title of this book. It reminds us that *commoning* should be understood not as a static condition, nor as the management of a resource alone, but as a dynamic social system composed of three inseparable elements: a shared resource, a community of users, and the social protocols through which that community governs the resource. If democracy itself is regarded as the commons to be protected, strengthened and continuously renewed, networking may likewise be understood as a form of commoning based on horizontal collaboration that privileges use value over exchange value.

This interpretation becomes even more compelling when networking unfolds across national boundaries and relies on the worldwide web as a central infrastructure for communication, knowledge exchange and collective action between people and organisations scattered around diferente locations. Under these conditions, "widely distributed, loosely connected individuals... cooperate with each other" to generate shared knowledge and value through processes of "commons-based peer production"[[26]](#footnote-25). By creating and consolidating networks, participants contribute to a "social morphology"[[27]](#footnote-26) that persists because it proves meaningful and effective for those involved, ultimately producing a "single, integrated system" that can itself be understood as a common good[[28]](#footnote-27).

From this perspective, transnational networking — through the creation of network structures characterised by fluid alliances and evolving partnerships — also calls for forms of polycentric governance. Polycentricity should not be understood as a single institutional model, but rather as a family of governance arrangements sharing one fundamental principle: authority is distributed across multiple, interconnected layers of decision-making, ranging from the local to the regional and ultimately the global level. Such arrangements seek to preserve both "internal transparency" and "popular control", even while addressing the complexities inherent in global politics. Through intelligent networking, different "islands" of commoning can progressively grow into broader "continents" of democratic resistance capable of responding to multiple forms of democratic erosion and institutional enclosure. Democracy itself, together with the institutions that sustain it, constitutes one of those "democratic goods" that, according to Graham Smith's analytical framework on Democratic Innovations, should be identified, protected and strengthened through any collective process aspiring to embody the principles of meaningful participatory democracy[[29]](#footnote-28).

To function as a democratic commons on an international scale, networking should therefore seek to realise at least four fundamental democratic goods: Inclusiveness, ensuring both "presence and voice" for those traditionally marginalised, including across linguistic and geographical barriers; Popular Control, enabling participants to exercise genuine influence over problem definition, option development and collective decision-making; Considered Judgement, fostering what Smith describes as an "enlarged mentality", whereby participants are encouraged to "think in the place of every other person" and transcend their own situated perspectives; and Transparency, ensuring both internal clarity among participants and broader public visibility beyond the network itself. Seen in this light, the affinity between networking as an intentional act of commoning and the practical implementation of participatory democracy becomes particularly evident.

Speaking of networking for democratic deepening as a democratic commons in itself therefore means recognising that actors operating from diverse cultural traditions, thematic perspectives and territorial scales collectively explore multiple pathways for advancing these democratic goods through forms of stewardship, collaboration and shared decision-making.

Because the solutions emerging from these processes are inevitably culturally embedded and context-sensitive, identifying and mapping their specificities, differences, convergences and tensions constitutes an essential first step. Such an endeavour requires acknowledging both diversity and transformability over time. It therefore calls for the adoption of a contextual and differential approach[[30]](#footnote-29) to analysing the various ways in which the contemporary democratic crisis is being addressed, as well as the solutions proposed to reverse its trajectory. Such an approach must simultaneously account for the specificity of individual contexts — their histories, actors, institutions, local resources and relational configurations — and for the meaningful differences across cases, periods and places. Only by holding these dimensions together can we better understand why democratic challenges assume different forms and why particular responses appear more appropriate in certain contexts than in others.

As stated in the introduction, this book adopts an inductive approach grounded in the identification of these dynamics and in the effort to represent them in the most plural and faithful manner possible. More specifically, this approach rests upon three fundamental assumptions. First, the dynamics under consideration cannot be understood independently of the environments in which they emerge and therefore need to be interpreted in relation to the contextual conditions and sociopolitical conjunctures that shape them. Second, while remaining sensitive to context, the analysis seeks to identify systematic variations across cases and subgroups in order to uncover both specific causal mechanisms and broader recurring dynamics. Third, any conclusions must be advanced with caution, recognising the inherent limits of generalisation imposed by contingency and by the fact that the democratic landscape itself continues to evolve rapidly.

Could a pattern-based approach provide the most appropriate framework for simultaneously addressing these assumptions? Bollier and Helfrich – in their book *The Patterns of Commoning* - suggest that it can:

"[…] Commoning involves so much idiosyncratic creativity, improvisation, situational choices, and dynamic evolution that it can only be understood as aliveness. It defies simple formulas or analysis. Any theoretical framework for understanding commons must therefore enter into a deep and ongoing engagement with the everyday practices and experiences of commoning. Theory and practice must be in intimate conversation with each other. They might be compared to the fluid complementarity of a musical score and orchestral performance, which exist only in relation to one another and together produce a symphony (…) The commons also is about complex, living systems and how they emerge and grow. Just as a footpath may emerge over time because it serves the interests of thousands of walkers, so a commons may emerge, and persist, because its specific “social morphology” is pleasing and effective for participating commoners. In a commons, (…) a resource – a forest, a body of water, an energy source, emotional energy, labor, knowledge – becomes co-mingled with social practice and diverse forms of institutionalization, producing a single, integrated system that must be considered as a whole. Patterns help us to identify similarities among diverse commons without homogenizing or oversimplifying them. Patterns of commoning help us avoid analytic frameworks that are generally too rigid, reductionist, totalizing or alienated from the messy dynamics of life.”[[31]](#footnote-30).

We strongly share this perspective. If commoning is fundamentally constituted by dynamic relationships rather than static institutional arrangements, a pattern-based approach[[32]](#footnote-31) appears particularly well suited to describing, interpreting and communicating such dynamics.

Indeed, theories of pattern-based analysis — which owe much to the seminal work of Christopher Alexander (1977) — propose recurring configurations of problems, practices and solutions as the primary unit of inquiry, rather than isolated variables or static institutional forms. In Alexander's formulation, patterns are reusable relational structures that connect recurrent problems with context-sensitive solutions while remaining embedded within a broader language of interconnected patterns. Building on this foundation, Helmut Leitner has extended the notion of pattern languages into a broader scientific methodology for understanding complex living systems and organising experiential knowledge in ways that are reusable, communicable and adaptable. His work shifts the emphasis from controlling and predicting systems towards supporting their continuous development through participation, cooperation and learning. From this perspective, pattern-based analysis is especially valuable when the objective is to understand how durable organisational forms emerge, travel, adapt and transform across different cultural, institutional and temporal contexts. We therefore consider it particularly appropriate for investigating transnational collaborative networks and the complexity that characterises their evolution[[33]](#footnote-32).

This perspective also resonates with the reflections on performative languages presented at the beginning of this chapter. If patterns are understood not simply as descriptive categories but as forms of practical knowledge capable of supporting action within complex and evolving social environments, then pattern-based analysis becomes more than an analytical instrument. It also becomes a language through which recurring forms of democratic problem-solving can be recognised, compared and communicated across actors possessing different experiences, institutional positions and cultural backgrounds. As Douglas Schuler (2008) suggests, while "liberating voices", pattern languages may themselves contribute to a broader "communication revolution". In the chapters that follow, drawing upon the XX stories of transnational networks presented throughout the volume, we shall attempt to identify a first set of recurring patterns capable of generating the maieutic questions discussed in Chapter XX. Rather than offering normative prescriptions, these patterns are intended to support future processes of collective self-reflection by the networks themselves, helping them to recognise, explain and further transform both their practices and the challenges they encounter.

Our intention is therefore not to propose a definitive taxonomy, but rather to initiate a conversation. We envisage this work as the beginning of a "pattern collection"—or perhaps more appropriately, a "collection of patterns"—that may gradually evolve into what Leitner describes as a "foundation for dialogue between everyone involved" within a universe of practices "interwoven by conscious and unconscious patterns, whereby each pattern is linked to other patterns"[[34]](#footnote-33). We also share his broader conviction that:

"[…] Changes in our world appear in new patterns emerging or existing ones changing. All design patterns taken together as a whole form humanity’s cultural heritage, which can only belong to all of us together. Pattern descriptions are a form of sharing this heritage with others and making it accessible to all people in their own lives and surroundings. Pattern descriptions are tools for involvement in decisionmaking as well as participation in continually making the world a better place in a common, creative, cooperative and consensual process. However, we have yet to begin doing so rationally and at a larger scale. The path to patterns consists in starting with practical experience and using it as the basis for elaborating useful experiential knowledge in a joint process, and reflecting on it, refining it, and deepening it with reference to theory. Once the patterns exist as a collection of texts and data, also called a repository, they can be worked through and prepared for practical work in different ways."¹ [[35]](#footnote-34).

Seen from this perspective, our work can only be characterised as exploratory — the final and perhaps most demanding concept embedded in the title of this book.

We deliberately chose the adjective *exploratory* instead of *pioneering* for two complementary reasons. The first reflects our understanding of scientific inquiry, which should always combine intellectual ambition with methodological humility. The second is grounded in the fact that exploratory research possesses a long and well-established scientific tradition. [[36]](#footnote-35) It is particularly appropriate when researchers investigate emerging or insufficiently theorised phenomena through qualitative inquiry, seeking to identify recurring patterns, develop conceptual interpretations and formulate propositions capable of opening new avenues for both scholarly research and broader public debate. [[37]](#footnote-36) For these reasons, *exploratory* appeared to us not only a more accurate description of our endeavour, but also a more rigorous and academically robust one.

Rather than seeking definitive causal explanations, exploratory studies aim to broaden understanding by examining complex realities from multiple perspectives while remaining open to unexpected relationships, interpretations and conceptual developments. [[38]](#footnote-37) This orientation appeared especially appropriate for investigating transnational collaborative networks, whose evolving organisational forms, governance arrangements, practices of commoning and relational dynamics cannot yet be fully captured by existing theoretical frameworks. They are themselves evolving realities, characterised by incremental transformations and continuous adaptation.

Our ambition is therefore to move beyond a merely preliminary exploration while consciously preserving the exploratory nature of both our methodology and our analytical perspective. Through the combination of multiple case studies, interviews, documentary analysis and, in some cases, participatory observation, we have attempted to engage in a rigorous process of inductive conceptual development that moves towards what the literature defines as exploratory theory-building research. [[39]](#footnote-38) At the same time, we remain fully aware that developing a more comprehensive interpretative framework will require further comparative analyses, broader empirical material and, above all, continued dialogue with the protagonists of the experiences described throughout this volume. Such dialogue will undoubtedly benefit from additional visual representations, conceptual maps and interpretative frameworks capable of making the emerging patterns progressively more explicit and collectively discussable.

If this book succeeds in making even a modest contribution, it lies perhaps less in offering definitive conclusions than in opening a space for continued reflection. Throughout this research we have gathered considerably richer empirical material than could reasonably be analysed, interpreted and discussed within a single volume. More importantly, we have had the privilege of establishing relationships of trust and collaboration with the Nets4Dem partners and with all those who generously shared their experiences with us during the interviews. Those relationships suggest that this work should not be regarded as the conclusion of a research process, but rather as one step within a longer collective journey.

As the Nets4Dem ecosystem continues to evolve, new experiences, new trajectories and new patterns will inevitably emerge. We hope that the analytical framework proposed here may evolve together with them, remaining sufficiently open to incorporate new voices, reinterpret previous findings and continue learning from the very processes of commoning that this book seeks to understand.

**5. Naming Networks: Contribution of Semantics and Political Imagination**

In the preceding paragraphs, we have often used, as though they were equivalent, a series of terms that are related to the term “network,” but which cannot, strictly speaking, be considered exactly synonymous.

Indeed, throughout the empirical research that informed this volume and while the writing of this chapter was progressing, we realised that one apparently minor observation progressively acquired analytical significance: organisations rarely described themselves simply as networks. Some preferred to identify themselves as alliances, others as federations, hubs, coalitions, platforms, ecosystems, communities of practice, or networks of networks. Initially, these differences appeared to be little more than matters of terminology or institutional branding. As interviews accumulated and the analysis became more systematic, however, it became increasingly evident that the vocabulary adopted by each organisation frequently reflected distinct understandings of collaboration, governance, organisational identity and political ambition. Implicitly or explicitly, this showed us that language often matters more than we can initially can imagine.

This observation invites us to take language seriously, which is what we will try to do in this paragraph, reflecting on some – apparently – “side dimension” of our research, which may reveal substantive aspects of the described dynamics, that adequately fit with the exploratory approach we are proposing.

**5.1 Why names matter**

In the beginning of this chapter, we have argued that words are not merely descriptive devices but performative instruments that contribute to shaping social realities and accompanying their transformation. Quoting Lorenza Mondada, and other authors, we argued that discourse scholars have proved that policy language and political narratives actively frame collective problems and influence the range of imaginable solutions[[40]](#footnote-39). Consequently, if networking itself represents an ongoing process of constructing shared meanings and collective action, the language through which organisations define themselves becomes part of the phenomenon under investigation rather than a merely external description of it.

This consideration appears particularly relevant in the field of democracy. Unlike many sectors characterised by clearly institutionalised organisational models, democratic innovation and transnational civic cooperation have developed through highly diverse trajectories. Organisations often emerge incrementally, evolve through experimentation, and continuously redefine their identities according to changing political opportunities, available resources and emerging partnerships. Their names therefore frequently crystallise particular moments within these evolutionary processes. Choosing to present oneself as an alliance rather than as a federation, or as an ecosystem rather than as a platform, rarely constitutes a neutral semantic preference. More often, it reflects implicit assumptions regarding autonomy, hierarchy, participation, permanence, institutionalisation and the desired relationship between individual members and the collective whole.

At the same time, semantic precision should not be confused with semantic stiffness and conceptual rigidity. We cannot deny that the boundaries separating many of these organisational forms remain fluid and elastic, and the same organisation may legitimately describe itself through different labels according to context, audience or strategic objectives. Indeed, institutional identities often emerge through historical contingencies, communication strategies, funding opportunities or political positioning, rather than through explicit theoretical reflection. Consequently, this chapter does not seek to establish rigid classifications or normative definitions: rather, it proposes an exploratory reading of the principal terms currently employed within the universe of transnational democratic networking, identifying the organisational logics, governance cultures and political imaginaries that they most frequently evoke, but paying respect to ‘plasticity’ as a guiding principle in shaping, reading and discussing about names.

In this perspective, the objective of such reflection is neither taxonomic nor prescriptive. Instead, consistent with the exploratory and pattern-based approach adopted throughout this volume, the following pages attempt to identify recurring “semantic and language patterns”[[41]](#footnote-40) that emerge from both the literature and the empirical material collected during the Nets4Dem project. Rather than asking which definition is ultimately "correct", we are interested in understanding what each denomination reveals about the ways in which organisations imagine cooperation, distribute authority, construct collective identities and position themselves within broader democratic ecosystems. Listening carefully to the language used by practitioners may become, in this sense, another way of listening to the field itself…

**5.2. Beyond semantics: organisational labels as implicit theories of governance**

Before examining the main concepts that populate the vocabulary of transnational networking, it is worth to reflect on a broader question that progressively emerged throughout this research: are these terms simply different names describing essentially the same organisational reality, or do they reveal more profound differences in the ways collective action is imagined and organised?

Our analysis suggests the latter. Each organisational label appears to embody an implicit theory of governance. Choosing to describe a collaborative initiative as an alliance, a coalition, a hub, a federation or an ecosystem does more than communicate a particular organisational form. It also expresses assumptions—sometimes explicit, often implicit—about how authority should be distributed, how coordination should occur, how collective identities are constructed, what degree of institutionalisation is considered desirable, and how relationships between autonomy and interdependence ought to be balanced. Seen from this perspective, organisational vocabularies become more than semantic choices. They represent different political imaginaries of cooperation. An alliance evokes strategic convergence among autonomous actors; a federation suggests a more stable constitutional architecture; a hub privileges brokerage, facilitation and connectivity; a coalition emphasises mobilisation around shared objectives; an ecosystem foregrounds co-evolution, diversity and adaptive interdependence. None of these conceptions is inherently superior to the others. Rather, each reflects a different understanding of how collaboration should emerge, evolve and produce collective value.

This observation also resonates with the theoretical framework developed in the previous paragraphs. If commoning can be understood as an ongoing process through which communities collectively organise, negotiate and govern shared resources, then the language through which organisations describe themselves becomes part of that process of commoning. Likewise, if patterns are conceived as recurring relational configurations rather than fixed institutional models, the semantic choices adopted by organisations may themselves be interpreted as patterns worthy of investigation. If we would like to exploit this line of reflection, we could get inspired by the work of the Virginie Malles-Viard, a French literary journalist and critic, who coordinated a work on languages related to Participatory Budgeting, during a series of public webinars organised by *Decider Ensemble* and the *French National Network of Participatory Budgets (RNBP)* between March and July, 2025[[42]](#footnote-41).

**5.3. Some recurrent terms used in self-description, and their nuances**

**5.3.1. Alliance: cooperation without fusion?**

Among the many terms employed to describe transnational collaboration, “alliance” is perhaps one of the most widespread across both political science and international relations. Its origins lie primarily in diplomatic and military studies, where alliances have traditionally been understood as voluntary agreements among autonomous actors pursuing common strategic objectives while preserving their independent identities[[43]](#footnote-42). Although initially developed to analyse interstate cooperation, the concept has progressively expanded into civil society studies, advocacy research and transnational governance, where it now frequently describes collaborative arrangements among organisations seeking to influence public policies or address shared societal challenges. The defining characteristic of an alliance is that cooperation does not require organisational integration. Members voluntarily coordinate their actions because they recognise that collective objectives can be pursued more effectively together than individually. At the same time, each participant retains its own governance structures, strategic priorities, financial autonomy and public identity. In this respect, alliances occupy an intermediate position between informal networking and more institutionalised organisational forms such as federations. They create commitments without dissolving diversity and personal trajectories.

Political science has long interpreted alliances as strategic responses to external opportunities or threats. Stephen Walt (1987), for example, argued that alliances emerge not merely in response to concentrations of power but more specifically in response to perceived threats. Although his work focuses on international security, this analytical intuition appears surprisingly transferable to contemporary democratic cooperation. Numerous transnational democratic alliances have emerged (with a visible acceleration during and after COVID pandemic) precisely because individual organisations increasingly perceive that challenges such as democratic backsliding, disinformation, attacks against civil society, scientific denialism, restrictions on academic freedom or shrinking civic space cannot be effectively addressed in isolation.

Within the field of democracy, alliances therefore tend to combine three complementary characteristics. First, they preserve organisational pluralism by allowing highly diverse actors — including NGOs and community-based organisations, municipalities, universities, philanthropic foundations and advocacy organisations — to cooperate without requiring institutional homogenisation. Second, they remain strongly purpose-oriented, generally emerging around shared normative objectives rather than around common administrative structures. Third, alliances usually privilege coordination over hierarchy, relying more on negotiation, mutual trust and voluntary commitment than on formal authority. From this perspective, alliances can also be interpreted through the lens of commoning discussed in the previous paragraphs. Rather than constituting merely contractual arrangements among independent organisations, they frequently represent collective efforts to steward democratic goods that transcend individual institutional interests. In this light, democracy itself becomes the shared resource around which different actors organise common action while preserving their own organisational autonomy. The alliance thus functions less as a fixed institutional structure than as a governance arrangement enabling multiple organisations to produce collectively what none of them could achieve individually.

European democratic cooperation offers numerous examples of this organisational logic. Several transnational initiatives explicitly adopt the language of alliances to emphasise strategic convergence while avoiding the institutional rigidity often associated with federative structures. Their legitimacy derives less from formal representation than from their capacity to mobilise complementary expertise, facilitate coordination among heterogeneous actors and amplify collective advocacy at the European level. The terminology itself therefore communicates a specific political philosophy: cooperation based upon voluntary convergence rather than institutional integration. It can be worth to note that, from a pattern-based perspective, the concept of alliance appears to evoke a recurring organisational configuration characterised by high organisational autonomy, moderate coordination, strong normative cohesion and strategic flexibility. It represents cooperation without fusion, collective action without centralisation, and shared political purpose without the creation of a single institutional identity.

### 5.3.2. Coalition: mobilisation around a common purpose

If alliances emphasise strategic cooperation among autonomous actors, the concept of “coalition” places greater emphasis on collective mobilisation around a specific objective or policy agenda. Although the two terms are often used interchangeably in everyday language, political science has traditionally distinguished coalitions by their stronger orientation towards coordinated action in response to a defined challenge or opportunity. Rather than constituting permanent organisational arrangements, coalitions generally emerge to achieve particular political, legislative or societal goals, bringing together actors whose broader identities and long-term priorities may remain substantially different.

The classical literature on coalition formation originates in political science and game theory. William Riker (1962), for example, analysed coalition formation as a rational process through which actors seek to maximise collective gains while minimising unnecessary commitments. Subsequently, Paul Sabatier's Advocacy Coalition Framework (1988)[[44]](#footnote-43) broadened this perspective by showing that coalitions are not merely strategic arrangements but communities of actors sharing normative beliefs, policy objectives and long-term learning processes across institutional boundaries. Within this framework, governmental institutions, researchers, civil society organisations and advocacy groups may all become members of the same coalition despite preserving their organisational autonomy.

Applied to transnational democratic cooperation, the concept of coalition highlights the capacity of diverse organisations to converge around specific democratic priorities without necessarily sharing a broader institutional identity. Electoral integrity, media freedom, civic participation, anti-corruption initiatives or the defence of the rule of law frequently become focal points around which temporary or long-lasting coalitions emerge. Their cohesion derives less from organisational integration than from the recognition of a common public problem requiring coordinated responses. From the perspective of governance, coalitions privilege negotiation over institutionalisation. Their legitimacy depends upon their capacity to articulate shared agendas while accommodating considerable internal diversity. This characteristic also makes coalitions particularly adaptive. New actors may enter or leave according to changing political circumstances without fundamentally altering the collective identity of the initiative itself.

Within the broader argument developed throughout this chapter, coalitions may therefore be interpreted as expressing a governance imaginary centred upon collective mobilisation. Whereas alliances privilege strategic cooperation, coalitions foreground coordinated action directed towards common political objectives. Their organisational flexibility often makes them particularly suitable for responding to rapidly evolving democratic challenges.

### 5.3.3 Hub: governance through connectivity

Among the concepts that have gained increasing prominence during the last two decades, particularly following the digital transformation of collaborative governance, “hub” occupies a distinctive position. Unlike alliances or coalitions, it does not primarily describe a membership structure. Rather, it identifies a relational function within a wider network.

Originally employed in transportation and communication studies (and later in Social Network Analysis[[45]](#footnote-44)) to designate “central nodes” connecting multiple routes, the concept has progressively expanded into organisational theory, innovation studies and network governance. Manuel Castells (1996; 2009) demonstrated that contemporary societies increasingly organise themselves around networks whose effectiveness depends upon strategic nodes capable of facilitating the circulation of information, resources and knowledge. Similarly, Burt's (1992) work on structural holes highlighted how actors occupying brokerage positions frequently generate innovation by connecting otherwise disconnected communities.

Within transnational democratic cooperation, hubs perform precisely this brokerage function. Rather than representing or coordinating members through formal authority, they facilitate encounters, circulate information, promote mutual learning, support capacity-building and stimulate new collaborative initiatives. Their influence therefore depends less upon hierarchical power than upon relational centrality. This organisational philosophy reflects a governance model based on “connectivity rather than control”. Hubs generally seek to reduce fragmentation by connecting actors operating across different geographical, institutional or thematic domains. Their success depends upon maintaining openness, trust and accessibility rather than imposing common strategies or binding decisions.

From the perspective developed in the previous chapter, hubs may also be interpreted as infrastructures of commoning. By enabling the circulation of knowledge, practices and relational capital, they contribute to producing common goods without necessarily owning or governing them directly. Their role resembles that of facilitators who enable other actors to cooperate more effectively while preserving their autonomy. Consequently, the organisational imaginary embedded in the concept of hub privileges mediation, brokerage and distributed intelligence. Governance emerges through the quality of connections rather than through institutional integration[[46]](#footnote-45).

### 5.3.4. Ecosystem: governance through co-evolution

Perhaps no organisational metaphor has expanded more rapidly during the last fifteen years than that of the “ecosystem”. Borrowed from ecology, the concept has progressively entered economics, innovation studies, public administration, democratic governance and even democratic innovations[[47]](#footnote-46), becoming one of the preferred ways of describing highly complex environments characterised by multiple autonomous yet interdependent actors.

James F. Moore (1993) first introduced the notion of the business ecosystem to explain how organisations increasingly evolve through cooperation as much as through competition. More recently, Adner (2017) refined the concept by emphasising that ecosystems are defined less by organisational boundaries than by patterns of interdependence among heterogeneous actors jointly producing value. Transferred to the field of democracy, the ecosystem metaphor offers an important conceptual shift. It encourages moving beyond the idea of a single network towards recognising a broader environment composed of public institutions, universities, media organisations, civic associations, municipalities, philanthropic foundations, social movements, research centres and international organisations whose interactions collectively shape democratic resilience.

Unlike alliances or federations, ecosystems possess neither a clearly identifiable centre nor fixed membership. Actors continuously can enter, leave and redefine their relationships. Governance therefore emerges through processes of mutual adaptation rather than through formal authority. This understanding strongly resonates with Ostrom's notion of polycentric governance (2010), according to which complex societal problems are often addressed more effectively through multiple interacting centres of decision-making than through single hierarchical institutions. Likewise, it converges with contemporary understandings of commoning as an evolving process sustained by continuous interaction among diverse communities rather than by static institutional arrangements.

Within this organisational imaginary, diversity becomes a source of resilience rather than fragmentation. Innovation emerges through interactions occurring across organisational boundaries, while long-term sustainability depends upon maintaining the overall health of relationships rather than strengthening individual organisations in isolation.

Among all the concepts discussed in this chapter, ecosystem perhaps comes closest to describing democracy itself as a living, adaptive and continuously evolving common good.

### 5.3.5. Network of networks: second-order collaboration

If ecosystems describe complex environments, the concept of a “network of networks” introduces a further level of organisational complexity. Here the object of collaboration is no longer constituted by individual organisations but by already existing collaborative structures.

As we will better detail in the next chapters, the emergence of networked societies, analysed by Castells (1996) and other authors, and the growing importance of transnational advocacy networks documented by Keck and Sikkink (1998), progressively revealed that many contemporary governance processes increasingly operate through interconnected constellations of pre-existing networks rather than through isolated organisations. More recently, digital commons, open-source communities and global knowledge infrastructures have further reinforced this tendency[[48]](#footnote-47). A network of networks therefore represents a sort of form of “second-order governance**”**. Its principal objective is not to substitute existing networks but to facilitate coordination among them while preserving their identities, expertise and organisational autonomy. It creates opportunities for mutual learning, joint advocacy, resource sharing and strategic alignment without requiring institutional fusion.

From a governance perspective, this organisational model embodies perhaps the highest degree of polycentricity among the concepts discussed in this chapter. Decision-making remains distributed across multiple organisational levels, while coordination emerges through negotiation, reciprocity and continuous communication rather than through centralised authority. This organisational philosophy appears particularly relevant for contemporary democratic cooperation. The growing complexity of global democratic challenges—including disinformation, declining trust, democratic backsliding, climate governance and digital transformation—often exceeds the capacities of individual networks acting alone. Under these conditions, connecting existing collaborative infrastructures may become as important as creating new organisations.

The concept of a network of networks seems also to reflect one of the central arguments advanced throughout this volume. If networking is understood as a continuous practice of commoning, then connecting networks capable of producing democratic goods may itself constitute a higher-order form of commoning. Common goods are no longer generated only within individual collaborative initiatives but increasingly through the relationships established among them, but networks themselves become resources for further networking.

### 5.3.6. Platform and Community of Practice: enabling interaction and collective learning?

Two additional concepts deserve brief consideration because of their growing relevance within European programmes and transnational democratic initiatives. They are “platform” and “community of practice**”**.

A “platform” differs from the concepts discussed above in that it primarily denotes an enabling infrastructure rather than a collective actor. Whether digital or organisational, platforms create conditions under which interaction, collaboration and knowledge exchange can occur without necessarily directing or governing these processes. Recent studies of platform societies[[49]](#footnote-48) have highlighted both the opportunities and the risks associated with platform-based governance. Within democratic cooperation, however, the term is often employed more neutrally to indicate spaces facilitating dialogue among otherwise disconnected actors.

“Communities of practice”, by contrast, place learning at the centre of collaboration. Introduced by Lave and Wenger (1991)[[50]](#footnote-49), the concept describes groups of practitioners who collectively develop knowledge, competences and shared repertoires through sustained interaction around common practices. Unlike coalitions, communities of practice are not primarily oriented towards advocacy; unlike federations, they do not require formal membership structures. Their cohesion derives from continuous learning and mutual engagement.

From the governance perspective proposed throughout this chapter, platforms embody an imaginary centred upon enabling interaction, whereas communities of practice privilege collective learning as the principal mechanism through which cooperation evolves. Both concepts remind us that democratic networking involves not only coordinating action but also creating the relational and cognitive infrastructures through which future collaboration becomes possible.

### 5.3.7. Federation: a way of institutionalising cooperation

Among the concepts examined throughout this chapter, “federation” probably represents the highest degree of organisational institutionalisation. Unlike alliances or coalitions, which preserve broad strategic flexibility, federations establish relatively stable governance arrangements through which autonomous organisations voluntarily delegate part of their authority to a common institutional framework while maintaining their own identities.

The concept has deep roots in constitutional theory and political science. Daniel Elazar (1987) defined federal arrangements as systems that reconcile self-rule with shared rule, combining autonomy and integration through negotiated constitutional principles. Similarly, Ronald Watts (1999) emphasised that federative systems distribute authority across multiple levels of governance while preserving the legitimacy of each participating entity.

Transferred to civil society, federations generally consist of organisations that formally become members of a common structure, adopting shared statutes, governance procedures and representative institutions. Decision-making therefore becomes more stable, predictable and institutionalised than in alliances or coalitions, while preserving the plurality of the participating organisations. From the perspective adopted throughout this volume, federations embody a governance imaginary centred upon “institutional integration”. Cooperation is no longer based exclusively upon voluntary convergence around common objectives, but progressively develops into shared institutional architectures capable of ensuring continuity, representation and collective accountability over time.

For transnational democratic cooperation, federative arrangements may prove particularly valuable whenever long-term representation, stable advocacy and institutional legitimacy become priorities. At the same time, they inevitably require greater organisational commitments, more formal governance procedures and stronger collective identities than many emerging collaborative initiatives are willing — or able — to assume.

### 5.3.8. Front: the language of collective defence?

Unlike the concepts discussed so far, “front” immediately evokes a different semantic universe. Its historical origins are deeply rooted in military vocabulary, where the front identifies the space in which opposing forces confront one another. During the twentieth century, however, the term progressively entered political language through expressions such as *Popular Front*, *United Front* and numerous anti-fascist, anti-colonial and pro-democratic fronts, gradually becoming associated with broad civic mobilisation against perceived threats.

Political discourse has long recognised that metaphors of conflict shape the way societies interpret political challenges. Lakoff and Johnson (1980) demonstrated that metaphors do not merely embellish language but structure political reasoning itself, while Snow and Benford (1988) showed how collective action frames construct shared understandings of injustice, urgency and mobilisation within social movements. Consequently, choosing the denomination *front* rarely constitutes a neutral communicative decision. Rather, it signals the perception that cooperation has entered a defensive phase requiring exceptional levels of solidarity and coordinated action.

This observation appears particularly relevant in the contemporary democratic context. During the last decade—and especially following the COVID-19 pandemic, the intensification of democratic backsliding, growing disinformation campaigns, the growth of several forms of environmental and historic/cultural denialisms, together with renewed geopolitical tensions — several initiatives have increasingly adopted vocabularies emphasising protection, resilience and collective defence. Although Europe has generally preferred less confrontational organisational language than other world regions, similar tendencies have progressively emerged around initiatives devoted to defending academic freedom, media pluralism, electoral integrity and civic space.

Comparable developments can be observed beyond Europe. In Brazil, particularly during the Bolsonaro administrations, numerous collaborative initiatives connected universities, independent media, scientific institutions, legal organisations and civil society actors to defend democratic institutions and counter disinformation. Similar forms of broad democratic mobilisation emerged in the United States during the Trump presidencies around electoral integrity, constitutional guarantees, environmental issues and the protection of independent journalism. More recently, democratic developments in countries such as Hungary, Slovakia, Serbia, Georgia and Moldova suggest that analogous forms of collective coordination may increasingly characterise contexts experiencing democratic erosion and growing pressure upon civil society.

Whether these organisational denominations merely reflect changing political environments or actively contribute to constructing them remains an open empirical question. Nevertheless, the growing diffusion of expressions such as *Front*, *Shield*, *Resilience* or *Defence* appears to indicate that contemporary democratic networking increasingly imagines itself not only as a space for innovation but also as an infrastructure for democratic protection. From the governance perspective proposed in this chapter, the concept of *front* embodies an imaginary centred upon “collective defence”. Cooperation is organised not primarily around institution-building or knowledge exchange, but around the perception of common threats that require coordinated civic responses.

### 5.3.9. Community, and community of communities

Although frequently employed interchangeably with *network*, the concept of “community” introduces a rather different understanding of collective action. Whereas networks primarily emphasise relationships among actors, communities foreground shared identity, belonging and mutual recognition. Their cohesion derives less from formal organisational arrangements than from the existence of common values, narratives and practices.

This understanding has been extensively explored within the literature on communities of practice[[51]](#footnote-50), but also resonates with broader sociological traditions extending from Ferdinand Tönnies' distinction between *Gemeinschaft* and *Gesellschaft[[52]](#footnote-51)* to more recent discussions of civic communities and social capital[[53]](#footnote-52).

Within transnational democratic cooperation, community-oriented vocabularies often emerge when organisations seek not only to coordinate activities but also to cultivate long-term cultures of collaboration, mutual trust and shared learning. Increasingly, one also encounters expressions such as “community of communities”, particularly within international philanthropic and participatory initiatives, where the emphasis shifts from coordinating organisations towards connecting pre-existing communities while preserving their cultural and organisational diversity. Such formulations reinforce an understanding of governance grounded less in representation than in belonging, reciprocity and continuous relationship-building.

### 5.3.10. Constellations, landscapes and other emerging cosmologies of collaboration

Perhaps the most intriguing development observed during this research concerns the gradual emergence of a new family of organisational metaphors that no longer describe organisations themselves but broader configurations of collaborative experiences. Expressions such as “constellation”**, “**landscape”**, “**galaxy”**, “**universe”**, “**archipelago”**,** and, more occasionally,even **“**firmament”, increasingly appear within democratic innovation, philanthropy, social innovation and commons-oriented initiatives.

Unlike alliances, coalitions or federations, these expressions generally do not designate formal governance structures. Rather, they seek to represent the plurality of experiences coexisting within broader relational environments. Their objective is not to define membership but to render diversity visible. They leverage on the idea of something that illuminates, enlightens and guides, like a beacon or a lighthouse would do. And is not by chance that a similar language is used frequently in description of participatory practices.

Among these metaphors, *constellation* appears particularly significant. Astronomically, a constellation does not constitute a physical object but a meaningful pattern perceived among stars that remain independent of one another. The metaphor is remarkably suggestive for understanding contemporary democratic collaboration. Individual initiatives preserve their autonomy while becoming intelligible as parts of larger relational configurations whose significance emerges less from organisational integration than from the patterns connecting them.

Interestingly, this metaphor resonates strongly with the pattern-based perspective adopted throughout this volume. Christopher Alexander's (1977) understanding of patterns as recurring relational configurations offers an unexpectedly fruitful parallel. Just as architectural patterns do not eliminate diversity but reveal meaningful relationships among heterogeneous elements, constellations invite us to recognise recurring organisational configurations without imposing rigid classifications. The analytical emphasis shifts from identifying organisational categories towards recognising relational patterns.

Similar observations may be formulated regarding expressions such as *landscape* or *galaxy*. Increasingly employed within philanthropy, democratic innovation and systems thinking, these concepts suggest that collaborative initiatives should be understood less as isolated organisations than as components of broader relational environments characterised by coexistence, visibility, emergence and mutual influence. Unlike institutional metaphors, they rarely presuppose hierarchy, centrality or fixed organisational boundaries. Instead, they privilege plurality, adaptive evolution and distributed agency.

Although these emerging vocabularies have not yet generated a consolidated theoretical literature, they may indicate a broader transformation in the ways democratic collaboration imagines itself. Earlier organisational concepts largely sought to answer questions concerning coordination, authority and institutional design. These newer metaphors seem to pose different questions altogether: How do heterogeneous experiences become mutually visible? How do they coexist without necessarily integrating? How do they collectively compose larger democratic horizons while preserving their diversity?

Whether this semantic evolution merely reflects communicative (and branding) creativity or signals a deeper epistemological transformation remains an open question. Nevertheless, from an exploratory perspective, the growing diffusion of these cosmological metaphors deserves attention. They may suggest that contemporary democratic networking is progressively moving from organisational thinking towards relational thinking — from institutions towards configurations, from structures towards patterns.

## 5.4. “*Nomen Omen*”: when "the name is a sign"

In Roman culture, it was believed that names are not arbitrary, but somehow anticipate or reveal the nature, vocation or destiny of persons and things. For this reason, we opened these final remarks, using the classical expression “*Nomen omen*” (that literally means "the name is a sign" or "the name carries a destiny") that, today, it is commonly used to mean that each name tells us something essential. That is exactly your argument here: can self-definitions reveal organisational identity, governance philosophy, political imagination, and/or collective aspirations, instead of being neutral descriptors?

The objective of this paragraph has not been to establish a definitive taxonomy of transnational collaborative organisations. Such an ambition would probably prove both unrealistic and theoretically counterproductive. The empirical reality observed throughout this research reveals organisational forms that constantly evolve, overlap and redefine themselves according to changing political contexts, strategic opportunities and historical trajectories.

At first sight, one might therefore question whether distinguishing between alliances, coalitions, federations, hubs, ecosystems or constellations really matters. After all, organisational names often emerge from practical considerations, communication strategies, historical contingencies or branding decisions rather than from explicit theoretical reflection. From this perspective, insisting upon semantic precision might appear unnecessary — or even artificial.

Yet, the exploratory approach adopted throughout this volume suggests a different interpretation. If the purpose of exploratory research is to listen carefully to emerging realities before imposing analytical categories upon them, then the names and the language used by practitioners becomes valuable empirical evidence. During our interviews and documentary analysis, organisational self-definitions repeatedly emerged as meaningful indicators of how actors understood collaboration, authority, participation and democratic action. Listening to these words therefore became another way of listening to the field itself.

This observation leads us to formulate a broader interpretative hypothesis. Perhaps the vocabulary of transnational networking should not primarily be understood as a collection of alternative labels describing similar organisational realities. Rather, each organisational denomination may be interpreted as expressing a sort of “implicit theory of governance”. Alliances privilege strategic convergence among autonomous actors. Coalitions foreground coordinated mobilisation around common objectives. Federations institutionalise cooperation through shared representative structures. Hubs imagine governance through connectivity and brokerage. Platforms create enabling infrastructures for interaction. Communities cultivate belonging and mutual learning. Ecosystems emphasise adaptive co-evolution among heterogeneous actors. Networks of networks promote second-order collaboration among existing collaborative structures. More recent metaphors such as constellations or landscapes move one step further by representing democratic cooperation as broader relational configurations whose coherence emerges through recurring patterns rather than organisational integration.

None of these organisational imaginaries should be regarded as intrinsically superior to the others. Each simply offers a different answer to a series of fundamental questions. How should authority circulate? How should diversity be coordinated? What balance should exist between autonomy and integration? How should democratic cooperation evolve over time? Organisational language therefore becomes more than a descriptive instrument. It becomes part of democratic practice itself. Ultimately, perhaps transnational democratic networks should not primarily be classified according to their formal legal structures, but according to the governance imaginaries they enact through both their organisational practices and the language through which they describe themselves. If this hypothesis proves fruitful, the distinction between alliance, hub, ecosystem, coalition, federation, constellation or network of networks becomes less a question of terminology than one of political ontology. These concepts express different ways of imagining how democracy itself should be organised in order to confront increasingly complex transnational challenges.

The empirical chapters that follow do not seek to verify this proposition conclusively. Rather, they invite readers to observe how these different governance imaginaries materialise within concrete experiences of transnational networking. If, as argued in the previous chapters, languages are performative and patterns emerge through practice, then the words organisations choose to describe themselves should also be understood as part of the democratic realities they collectively construct.

**5.5. Listening to the language of networks? A small disclaimer.**

To introduce a lighter and slightly ironic note into the discussion, it is worth quoting an interview with one of the European most internationally networked organisations in the field of participatory and deliberative democracy, which itself hosts – under its umbrella – some networks, particularly among senior public officials working on citizen participation. The story, recounted in the first person by one of its leaders, explains how the term *federation* was ultimately chosen to define the organisation:

If you ask me how was chosen the name of FIDE [Federation for Innovation in Democracy] it is a funny story…Initially, we were thinking about building a foundation for innovation in democracy Europe, copying the idea of the New Democracy Foundation. But (…) we are an international non-profit, which is different from a non-profit in Belgium. An international non-profit has to be declared by a Royal Decree, which means, you know, the Department of Justice will look at all your paperwork (…) It's a much higher bar. And, so, when filing for our papers, we received after months a reply from the Department of Justice (…) the word foundation (…) was not possible because a foundation is a separate legal form in Belgium. And we would confuse people that we were a foundation, which we were not. And so, whilst already having set the name and started working with this name (…) we just used “Federation” (…) In Latin, the word FIDE means trust (…) is beautiful. We had to keep the F, and we became a federation. It was. (…) more interesting to (have) discussions about the governance rather”.[[54]](#footnote-53)

At first sight, this anecdote seems to contradict the argument developed throughout this chapter, suggesting that the specific organisational label may be of secondary importance. On closer examination, however, it highlights a different and equally significant dimension of naming. Rather than focusing on the typological definition itself, the interview insists on the importance of choosing a memorable and evocative name that clearly reflects the organisation's mission and the centrality of its democratic purpose. In this sense, the organisational category almost becomes a mere “subtitle” of a more important “headline”, while the primary name functions as the principal vehicle of identity and public recognition.

Far from undermining the reflections developed in the previous pages, this episode actually broadens them. It suggests that attention should not be limited to the typological denomination — whether alliance, federation, coalition or network — but should also extend to the relationship between the organisation's distinctive name and the labelling of its organisational identity. If anything, the anecdote reinforces the broader argument that, in many cases, the choice of a name is itself a performative act through which an organisation begins to shape—at least in part—its own destiny…
