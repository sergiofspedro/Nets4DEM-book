# How this book was made: a note on AI and research methods

This short chapter is for readers who want to understand *how* the research behind this book was actually done. And in particular, what role artificial intelligence (AI) played in the process. We have chosen to be transparent about our methods not because AI use is unusual (it is increasingly common in research), but because we believe readers deserve to know what was done by humans, what was done with AI, and how the two interacted.

If you are not interested in methodology, feel free to skip this chapter and go straight to the analysis. But if you have ever wondered whether the interview quotes in this book are real, whether the reports were written by a machine, or how fourteen very different networks were studied in a single research project, this chapter is for you.

---

## The challenge: fourteen networks, many hours of conversation

The Nets4DEM project set out to study fourteen transnational networks working on democratic participation across Europe and beyond. To understand how these networks actually function, the research team conducted in-depth interviews with key actors in each network: coordinators, board members, practitioners, and partners. The interviews explored how networks govern themselves, how they fund themselves, how they make decisions, and how they deal with crises.

These interviews were conducted between May and July 2026 by experienced researchers. Each interview lasted between one and two hours, covering topics such as the network's history, governance structure, funding model, membership policies, external relationships, and how it had adapted to challenges over time. All interviews were recorded with the consent of the participants.

The result was dozens of hours of recorded conversation, rich with detail, nuance, and first-hand experience. The challenge was how to handle this material responsibly. To extract its value without losing its richness, and to analyse it systematically across fourteen very different cases, all within the constraints of a time-limited research project.

This is where AI entered the picture.

---

## Transcription: from speech to text

The first step was turning audio recordings into written text. A process called transcription.

Manual transcription (a human listening to a recording and typing it out) is extremely time-consuming. One hour of interview can take four to six hours to transcribe by hand. For dozens of hours of interviews, this would have consumed weeks of the research team's time.

We used an AI-powered transcription tool to convert the audio recordings into written transcripts automatically. The tool produces a text file with speaker labels and timestamps, capturing the words as they were spoken. This gave the research team a readable, searchable text version of each interview within minutes, rather than days.

It is important to be clear about what transcription AI does and does not do. AI transcription is remarkably good at converting clear speech in English or other major languages into text. However, it can make mistakes, especially with accented speech, overlapping conversation, technical terms, or names of organisations and people. For this reason, every AI-generated transcript was reviewed by a human researcher who corrected errors, added speaker identification where the AI had confused voices, and checked that the transcript faithfully represented the original recording. No transcript was used in its raw, unedited form.

In other words, AI made the process faster, but a human researcher remained responsible for the accuracy of every transcript used in this book.

## Phase 1: extracting themes from interviews (the prompt 1 reports)

Once the transcripts were ready, the research team faced a second challenge: how to extract meaningful content from each interview in a systematic way, without reducing the interviewees' complex accounts to simple summaries.

The method chosen was structured thematic analysis, assisted by AI. For each interview transcript, a researcher wrote a detailed prompt (a set of instructions) for a large language model (the same type of AI that powers tools like ChatGPT). The prompt asked the AI to read the transcript and produce a structured report organised by theme. Each theme was to include: a short label, a summary of 3 to 6 lines, a more detailed explanation, specific examples mentioned by the interviewee, and direct quotations from the interview transcript.

But this was not a case of "press a button and get a report." The prompt itself was carefully designed by the research team, drawing on their expertise in qualitative research methods. It included specific instructions to avoid common AI pitfalls: do not invent information that is not in the text, do not oversimplify, preserve contradictions and nuances, and if something is unclear, say so rather than guess. The prompt also explicitly instructed the AI to note implicit themes (ideas that the interviewee suggested without stating directly) but only when these could be supported by good evidence from the transcript.

The AI produced an initial structured report, known as a "prompt 1 report." This report was then read, checked, and edited by a human researcher. In many cases, the researcher added themes that the AI had missed, corrected misinterpretations, merged overlapping sections, and removed any passages where the AI had produced generic or inaccurate content. The final prompt 1 report thus represents a human-AI collaboration: the AI provided the first structured pass at a very long text, and the human researcher refined, corrected, and completed the analysis.

This approach allowed the research team to process a large volume of interview material in a reasonable timeframe while maintaining analytical quality. But it also required constant human oversight. We never treated the AI's output as final without review.

## Phase 2: from theme reports to case reports (building the analytical framework)

The prompt 1 reports gave the research team a detailed thematic catalogue of each interview. But to compare networks systematically, a more structured format was needed.

The research team designed an analytical framework consisting of nine standard dimensions, chosen because they capture the key structural features of any transnational network:

1. **Organisational identity** (what does the network call itself, and why?)
2. **Membership** (who belongs, and on what terms?)
3. **Governance** (how are decisions made?)
4. **Resources** (how is the network funded and staffed?)
5. **External relations** (who does the network partner with?)
6. **Knowledge** (how does the network produce and share knowledge?)
7. **Adaptation** (how has the network changed over time?)
8. **Commoning** (does the network create shared resources?)
9. **Impacts** (what has the network actually achieved?)

For each of the fourteen networks, the relevant prompt 1 reports were given to the AI, again accompanied by a detailed prompt asking it to produce a phase 2 case report structured around these nine dimensions. The AI was instructed to map the interview content to each dimension, preserve quotations, identify tensions and contradictions, and note where evidence was strong or weak.

Once again, every AI-generated case report was reviewed and revised by a human researcher. The researcher verified that quotations were accurate, that the AI had not invented claims, that the report captured the specificity of each network rather than producing generic descriptions, and that the analysis reflected the richness of the original interviews. In some cases, entire sections were rewritten because the AI had misinterpreted the evidence or missed a key dynamic.

The result was fourteen analytical case reports (each typically 6 to 12 pages long) that formed the empirical backbone of the research. Each report is signed by the research team, not by an AI. The AI was a tool; the analysis is human.

---

## Phase 3: cross-cutting patterns (from fourteen cases to nine patterns)

The final analytical phase was the most intellectually demanding. Having studied fourteen networks individually, the research team now asked: what patterns appear *across* these cases? Are there recurring structural dynamics that show up in multiple networks, despite their differences in size, geography, funding model, and governance style?

This is the kind of synthetic, interpretive work that AI cannot do on its own. Pattern recognition requires theoretical judgement: deciding whether two superficially similar phenomena are actually driven by the same underlying mechanism, or whether two apparently different dynamics are in fact variations of the same structural tension.

The process was researcher-led, but AI played a supporting role. The research team used the AI to help cluster similar themes across the fourteen case reports, to generate candidate patterns, and to test whether a proposed pattern was supported by evidence across multiple cases. But every pattern was formulated, named, defined, and validated by the research team.

The initial set of twelve candidate patterns was refined to nine through a process of consolidation (merging overlapping patterns), elimination (removing patterns with insufficient evidence), and sharpening (clarifying the mechanism that distinguishes each pattern from related but distinct dynamics). The final nine patterns (presented in chapter 5 of this book) are the result of human analytical work, not machine output.

## Phase 4: writing the book

The chapters of this book were written by the research team. AI was used for specific supporting tasks: checking references, suggesting alternative phrasings, helping to structure complex arguments, and occasionally generating initial drafts of descriptive sections. Every chapter, every argument, and every conclusion was developed, debated, and finalised by human authors. The AI was a writing assistant, not a co-author.

The theoretical chapters (2 to 4) draw on academic literature that was identified, read, and interpreted by the research team. AI assisted with literature searches and summaries, but the theoretical framework is the authors' own. The empirical chapters (5 and 6) are based on the phase 2 case reports and the phase 3 pattern analysis described above. The concluding chapter (7) synthesises the findings and draws out their implications for theory and practice. A task that requires the kind of judgement, experience, and normative commitment that only human researchers can bring.

## What AI did and did not do

It may be helpful to summarise this clearly.

**AI did:**

- Convert audio recordings into written transcripts (later checked by humans).
- Produce a first structured pass at thematic analysis of each interview (later edited by humans).
- Produce a first structured pass at case-report writing organised by analytical dimensions (later edited by humans).
- Help cluster themes across cases and test candidate patterns against evidence.
- Assist with literature searches, reference checking, and phrasing suggestions during writing.

**AI did not:**

- Design the research methodology or the analytical framework.
- Conduct the interviews.
- Decide which themes were important and which were not.
- Formulate, name, or validate the nine cross-cutting patterns.
- Write any chapter of this book as a final product.
- Draw conclusions or make recommendations.
- Replace the judgement, experience, or ethical responsibility of the research team.

## A note on transparency and trust

We recognise that the use of AI in academic research raises legitimate questions. Can we trust analysis that was produced with AI assistance? Are the quotations real? Did the AI "hallucinate" evidence that does not exist?

These are fair questions, and we have structured our workflow specifically to address them:

- **Every quotation** in this book that comes from an interview has been traced back to the original transcript, checked against the recording, and verified by a human researcher. No AI-generated quotation was used without verification.
- **Every claim** about a network's structure, funding, governance, or history has been cross-checked against interview evidence. Where evidence was thin or contradictory, we say so explicitly.
- **Every analytical claim** (every pattern, every comparison, every interpretation) was formulated and debated by the research team. AI helped us process information faster, but it did not make analytical decisions for us.

We believe that AI, when used transparently and with appropriate human oversight, is a legitimate research tool. Not fundamentally different from using a search engine, a spreadsheet, or a statistical software package. The difference is that AI can *generate text that sounds authoritative*, which makes its output potentially more misleading if not carefully checked. We have taken that risk seriously.

This chapter is our way of being honest about how this book was made. We hope it helps readers trust what they are about to read. Not because AI was used, but because AI use was never hidden, and because every step of the process was guided by human judgement and responsibility.
