**Chapter 3**

**Conceptual Foundations and Analytical Framework**

As noted in Chapter 2, the study of networks across different territorial scales has a long intellectual tradition and has occupied a prominent place in twentieth-century interpretations of societal change.

This chapter seeks to reconnect the discussion of transnational multi-actor networks and their variable geometries of alliance, using this connection to refine more fully the conceptual core of the volume and to bring into sharper focus democratic networks that engage, to varying degrees, with democratic innovations as a distinct object of analysis.

The opening section repositions the volume’s research agenda within the field of network theory, drawing on both its foundational contributions and its more recent developments. In so doing, it places democratic networks within the wider debate on networks as organizational forms and infrastructures of coordination, while also clarifying the way in which this volume positions itself within this body of literature and enters that debate in order to extend its conceptual reach.

The chapter then offers a critical examination of the field, identifying four interrelated sources of analytical fragility. These concern, first, the conceptual indeterminacy of the key categories commonly employed; second, the still limited empirical understanding of how networks operate, adapt, and reinvent themselves under conditions of structural change, pressure, and resource dependence; third, the tension between externally voiced democratic and participatory commitments and internal organizational practices and routines; and fourth, the contextual instability of networks as adaptive formations undergoing continuous reconfiguration. Taken together, these fragilities point to the limits of approaches that treat networks as stable or self-evident objects of analysis.

Against this background, the chapter closes by advancing a methodological contribution: a minimal analytical framework designed to move beyond purely descriptive and prescriptive accounts and to support systematic comparison in the field. Organized around a set of analytical dimensions, this framework is intended to guide the examination of democratic networks, with the aim of understanding, among other things, what they are, how they are structured, how they perceive themselves and how they evolve across different contexts and over time. It, thus, provides the conceptual groundwork for the empirical chapters that follow.

**1. Some Preliminary Remarks**

The field of democratic innovation has expanded significantly over recent decades, developing a range of instruments for the study of participatory mechanisms, practices of collaboration and citizen engagement, and administrative reforms, intersecting to varying degrees with the field of *empowered participatory governance*, which focuses in particular on involving citizens in more robust forms of defining and solving public problems[[1]](#footnote-1). A substantial body of this literature has also assessed democratic innovations in light of democratic goods such as inclusiveness, popular control, and transparency, examining both their diffusion and their substantive effects and democratic quality[[2]](#footnote-2). It nevertheless remains less well equipped to explain the organisational infrastructures that sustain these practices, connect them across different contexts, and reproduce them over time. These “infrastructures” — which are described in various ways as alliances, hubs, observatories, umbrella organisations, digital communities, transnational coalitions, hybrid formations, and so forth[[3]](#footnote-3) —constitute the relational fabric through which democratic actors coordinate, exchange knowledge, undertake advocacy, and ensure continuity. Yet they have been only partially theorised as objects of analysis in their own right.

Governance must also be understood as the transformation of the rationalities and practices through which public authority is exercised in interdependent and plural contexts, rather than solely as state retrenchment or the neoliberal outsourcing of government[[4]](#footnote-4). Governance networks may be defined as relatively stable and horizontal articulations of operationally autonomous yet interdependent actors, who interact through continuous negotiation in order to pursue public purposes in contexts of high complexity[[5]](#footnote-5). This entails moving beyond interaction among actors in order to highlight their capacity to contribute to the definition of orientations, rules, and visions endowed with public relevance—that is, valid beyond the immediate boundaries of the individual participating organisations[[6]](#footnote-6).

One may observe that a first source of analytical fragility lies at the conceptual level. As we saw in Chapter 2, networks use a wide range of terms to name themselves, terms that may, to some extent, reveal implicit understandings of their mission, internal relations, and modes of governance. Such self-designations are rarely problematized in the literature, which tends instead to accept the self-definitions adopted by the networks themselves. As a result, the field continues to rely on a broad and often interchangeable vocabulary, frequently without clarifying the distinctions among these terms or the interpretive and performative role of labels and forms of naming. Yet, as we have already shown in detail, terminological differences are meaningful and consequential, since each label refers to specific modes of identity and behaviour, ranging from self-definition to the organization of coordination, from the structuring of membership to the formal distribution of power[[7]](#footnote-7). A highly networked organization, for example, is not the same as a network of organizations; nor is a network of organizations the same as a network of networks. A platform designed to facilitate exchange does not operate in the same way as an alliance formed for joint action, and a project-based consortium may temporarily exhibit features similar to those of a network without evolving into a durable organizational form. Without clearer distinctions, the category of “network” becomes too elastic to sustain precise analysis and it risks to gradually lose explanatory capacity.

A second fragility concerns the empirical study of networks. Their relevance within contemporary democratic ecosystems is widely recognised, yet systematic knowledge remains limited once the question shifts from acknowledging their importance to understanding how they actually function, endure, and evolve under conditions of crisis. Networks are often described—through what amounts to an almost a priori simplification—as collaborative arenas, but collaboration captures only part of their internal organisation and external relations. Many networks of different kinds in fact operate through a continuous tension between cooperation and competition, that literature named as “coopetition”[[8]](#footnote-8). Actors may share knowledge, coordinate advocacy activities, or collaborate in dissemination while simultaneously competing for funding, visibility, partnerships, and institutional recognition, particularly in sectors strongly shaped and sustained by public or project-based resources[[9]](#footnote-9). One of the central analytical questions therefore concerns the ways in which networks manage this constitutive ambivalence and how they relate to internalised and institutionalised conflict. What requires explanation is their capacity to sustain a shared orientation among actors despite unequal resources, divergent strategies, and competing interests.

A third source of fragility concerns democratic coherence, more specifically. One of the most significant lessons emerging from the Social Network Analyses conducted within Nets4Dem — which will be discussed in greater detail in the chapter 6 of this volume — is that organisations committed to democratic strengthening in their external action should also be examined in relation to their internal functioning. A network may advocate participation, pluralism, citizen engagement, inclusion in the public sphere, while at the same time reproducing internally restricted decision-making structures, managerial authority and hierarchies, or asymmetrical forms of representation and governance. In this respect, membership assumes particular importance: it is far more than an administrative function, as it shapes actors' access, voice, and influence. It determines who may join, who may remain, who may participate in decision-making, and whose constraints are to be regarded as politically relevant. Where membership depends pragmatically upon the capacity to pay fees, travel regularly, provide co-funding, or maintain a stable presence, influence tends to become concentrated among actors endowed with greater resources, such as large organisations, public authorities, highly professionalised bodies, and those situated closer to the dominant circuits of recognition and funding[[10]](#footnote-10). The architecture of membership therefore provides a particularly revealing indicator of whether the internal configuration of a network reinforces or weakens its declared democratic commitments.

This architecture of membership renders visible a tension between the logic of membership and the logic of influence: organisations are required to maintain a constant balance between fostering internal participation and pursuing external impact through institutional access and agenda-setting. This dynamic intersects with the processes of NGOisation[[11]](#footnote-11) and professionalisation that characterise contemporary societies[[12]](#footnote-12). Although these developments may strengthen operational effectiveness, they also risk weakening members' control—understood as decision-making power—over strategic choices. Professionalisation does not preclude engagement with the membership base; rather, it transforms it. Indeed, it may encourage member involvement by mobilising members as an operational and functional resource, while at the same time reducing their political autonomy. Bureaucratisation — understood as the adoption of procedures oriented towards efficiency and technical effectiveness — acts in turn as a catalyst that widens the gap between staff control and members' control, enabling professional apparatuses to “colonize” decision-making domains traditionally reserved for the membership base. This dynamic extends beyond the technical sphere and raises a question of internal democratic coherence and legitimacy[[13]](#footnote-13).

A fourth fragility is rooted in context. Democratic networks, often compelled to undergo continual transformation, operate within an environment that is itself subject to profound and ongoing change. As highlighted by the second Social Network Analysis of Nets4Dem, the current period is marked by democratic backsliding, the shrinking of civic space, and increasing financial constraints. Under such conditions, networks are more appropriately understood as evolving configurations situated within a changing organisational field, far from being stable organisational entities across time and space. They face pressures to revise their legal forms, recalibrate alliances, and renegotiate the relationship between formalisation and flexibility. Formalisation, in particular, may strengthen stability and public recognition in some contexts, while in others it may introduce rigidity or increase political exposure. Any analytical framework capable of adequately studying networks today must therefore be able to trace processes of change and adaptation, rather than remaining confined to static description alone.

Building upon these premises, the analytical framework developed in this chapter is organised around a set of analytical dimensions intended to provide a detailed, albeit not exhaustive, account of democratic networks as organisational and political formations. These dimensions are designed to capture both the more enduring characteristics of a network and the ways in which it positions itself, operates, adapts, and changes.

Among the principal dimensions to be considered are: organisational form; actor composition; membership architecture; democratic mandate; temporal trajectory; degree of formalisation and legal structure; financial structure and resource dependence; internal governance and decision-making; internal democratic coherence; the role of knowledge and learning capacity; external alliances and positioning within the organisational field; and relations with public institutions.

Organisational form concerns whether the actor takes, for example, the form of a single organisation, a network of organisations, a network of networks, or a hybrid configuration. Actor composition refers to the types of actors involved and their relative balance, including, for example, civil society organisations, local authorities, academic institutions, professionals, foundations, digital communities, and advocacy groups. Membership architecture examines the conditions of access, the rights associated with participation, and the financial or organisational costs of membership. Democratic mandate clarifies which dimensions of democracy the network seeks to strengthen and which issues it places at the centre of its public action. Temporal trajectory examines the network diachronically, tracing its origins, key turning points, crises encountered, and processes of thematic or geographical expansion. Degree of formalisation and legal structure indicate whether the network operates through a formal legal entity, informal arrangements, or intermediate configurations, and how these choices affect flexibility and durability.

Financial structure and resource dependence concern the composition of economic resources and the extent to which dependence upon specific funding sources shapes strategic orientation and autonomy. Internal governance and decision-making examine how authority is distributed, how inclusive decision-making processes are, and how power asymmetries are managed or concealed. Internal democratic coherence concerns the relationship between the principles promoted externally and the practices actually adopted internally. The role of knowledge and learning capacity investigates whether the network primarily functions as a knowledge intermediary, produces evidence, facilitates exchange, undertakes advocacy, develops tools, or combines these functions, while simultaneously examining its capacity for reflective learning. External alliances and positioning within the organisational field concern the relationships a network establishes beyond its membership, the position it occupies within a broader organisational ecosystem, and the effects of these connections upon influence, legitimacy, and strategic capacity. Relations with public institutions examine patterns of interaction with authorities at the local, national, and supranational levels, taking into account both constraints and opportunities.

These dimensions are not intended merely to capture and describe a network at a single moment in time. Rather, their simultaneous consideration is intended to provide a heuristic framework through which different cases may be compared—among many other factors—in terms of organisational form, geographical scope, and historical trajectory, while also making it possible to trace processes of adaptation and resilience across changing democratic contexts.

**2. Reconnecting with Network Theory**

Reconnecting with network theory is necessary in order, first and foremost, to clarify the analytical foundations of this contribution.

It is important to emphasise that the objective is neither to retrace in detail well-established canons, nor to provide an exhaustive review of a field that is at once remarkably extensive and internally differentiated. Beginning from what might be described as a practical awareness, when the general term *network* remains at a purely descriptive level, it readily becomes an elastic and overextended label applied to organisational realities that differ from one another in multiple respects. Such usage obscures — or, at the very least, fails to bring into focus — the forms of coordination, mediation, power, exchange, and adaptation that structure democratic action. Moreover, the absence of full theoretical clarity, together with the lack of a proposal for categorising the actors that currently exist within the context under study in its various forms, results in a pragmatic approach to the subject that is not easily sustained. Returning to, and drawing upon, the principal strands of network theory helps to avoid — or at least mitigate — the reduction of the complexity of these actors to a single term. Networks have long been studied as complex relational formations, and the themes addressed in this volume — such as organisational identity, membership, governance, knowledge production, external positioning, resilience, and change — belong to a well-established field of inquiry.

The purpose of this section is therefore to recover the conceptual tools that are most useful for organising the analytical dimensions developed in this chapter, which will constitute the principal analytical lens through which the empirical material presented in the following chapters will be examined. Once the term *network* ceases to be treated as a descriptive shorthand, the central question becomes which dimensions of network life can be rendered visible through particular conceptual resources. From this perspective, network theory is invaluable because it offers a differentiated repertoire of categories and analytical lenses through which democratic networks may be studied as organisational and political formations operating under pressure and evolving within changing contexts.

A first dimension concerns relational configuration. Networks differ in the number of ties they contain, although, for analytical purposes, the more significant question concerns the way in which those ties are distributed and whether they connect groups that would otherwise remain separate. The literature suggests that the political significance of a network depends less upon the simple density of its internal ties than upon its capacity to connect actors across institutional, organisational, and territorial boundaries, thereby reducing fragmentation and expanding opportunities for coordination[[14]](#footnote-14). Concepts such as weak ties, bridges, and inter-clique linkages are particularly useful in this regard because they refer to connections that enable ideas, resources, and forms of coordination to circulate between segments of the network that would otherwise remain isolated. By contrast, dense clusters built primarily around strong ties may generate cohesion at the local level while also producing redundancy, since actors embedded within highly cohesive circles frequently share the same information and perspectives (Burt, 1995). It follows that a network may display strong local cohesion and yet remain politically limited where its internal segments are only weakly connected. Granovetter's discussion of Boston's West End remains particularly instructive in this respect, as it demonstrates how a socially cohesive community may nevertheless fail to mobilise effectively against an external threat when weak ties between local cliques are absent and broader coordination cannot be sustained. Thus, where ties between subgroups are weak or non-existent, mobilisation becomes more difficult, confidence in leadership more difficult to consolidate, and collective action struggles to extend beyond restricted circles[[15]](#footnote-15). Relational configuration therefore becomes a key dimension for analysing how networks generate opportunities for cooperation, the extent of their sphere of action, and the effectiveness with which they are able to operate in divided contexts.

A second dimension concerns embeddedness, trust, and the social conditions of coordination. The concept of embeddedness is significant because it directs attention to the social fabric within which coordination takes shape. Networks do not consist solely of formal arrangements, legal statutes, or organisational charts, and their stability frequently depends upon repeated interactions, reputational mechanisms, and expectations of reciprocity built over time within concrete relationships. For this reason, coordination is often grounded in the relational histories through which actors assess one another's trustworthiness and negotiate uncertainty, rather than relying exclusively upon the formal definition of rules[[16]](#footnote-16). What matters, in this respect, is that trustworthiness is filtered relationally through the network itself, as actors evaluate one another on the basis of previous interactions, third-party knowledge, and reputational signals circulating across connected contexts[[17]](#footnote-17). Within democratic networks, the durability of alliances frequently rests upon relationships of trust between individuals and organisations, many of which remain partly informal and cannot be fully codified through procedures or statutes. Such embedded relationships may help to moderate internal tensions, since cross-cutting ties reduce the likelihood that disagreement will crystallise into enduring fragmentation[[18]](#footnote-18). Organisational resilience is therefore linked, at least in part, to a network's capacity to generate credible expectations of cooperation while accommodating pluralism and difference.

The literature on social capital extends this argument by distinguishing between relational resources that serve private advantage and those that generate broader collective benefits, including trust, reciprocity, and shared capacity for action[[19]](#footnote-19). Networking may therefore be understood as a potential common resource whose value lies in sustaining democratic cooperation among heterogeneous actors—including citizens, NGOs, businesses, and local authorities[[20]](#footnote-20) —and in generating “synergistic effects” that make it possible to achieve outcomes superior to those attainable by individual partners acting independently. Naturally, it is not merely the existence of a tie that matters, but also its multiplex nature. According to the relational perspective that interprets networks as the “cement of civil society”[[21]](#footnote-21), a single relationship may simultaneously combine affinity ties (grounded in trust and cooperation) with flow ties (based on the exchange of information), thereby strengthening both the coordinating capacity and the resilience of inter-organisational networks[[22]](#footnote-22).

A third dimension concerns mobilisation, diffusion, and the conditions under which practices spread. Network theory has shown that collective action does not arise automatically from shared preferences: its development depends upon participation thresholds, the visibility of others' engagement, and forms of relational reinforcement that render action perceived as possible or worthwhile. The heterogeneity of thresholds is particularly significant, since mobilisation often depends upon the presence of pioneer actors willing to act before broader participation becomes visible, thereby making subsequent engagement by others more plausible[[23]](#footnote-23). Actors frequently decide to participate when they perceive that others are already involved, when they receive confirmation from multiple parts of the network, and when innovative or risky practices acquire sufficient social validation to become sustainable[[24]](#footnote-24). Visibility may, however, operate as a double-edged sword. Indeed, where information about a practice circulates widely but its adoption remains limited or insufficiently visible, the network may inadvertently generate a social signal of illegitimacy, producing a “backfire effect” in which the success of the information campaign itself provides social evidence against behavioural change[[25]](#footnote-25). This is particularly relevant to the study of democratic innovation, since deliberative and participatory practices rarely spread through mere exposure; their diffusion generally requires relational environments that are sufficiently dense to provide continuous social reinforcement and sustain commitment over time. Diffusion may nevertheless remain selective even in the presence of highly visible pioneer actors, often giving rise to “networks of pioneers for pioneers”[[26]](#footnote-26). Indeed, the success of so-called frontrunners does not automatically translate into easy transferability, since the measures adopted by a leading city can rarely be downscaled to contexts possessing more limited technical, financial, or organisational capacities[[27]](#footnote-27).

The distinction between simple and complex contagion is particularly useful in this regard because it suggests that central nodes do not invariably facilitate diffusion. Whereas simple contagion travels effectively through narrow bridges—individual weak ties functioning as shortcuts between distant groups—complex contagion requires a different architecture. Behaviours, social norms, or risky innovations are subject to barriers of coordination, credibility, and legitimacy that require social reinforcement from multiple sources. Consequently, their diffusion does not rely upon isolated ties but upon wide bridges, namely multiple and overlapping ties connecting communities and providing the validation necessary for adoption. Central nodes (hubs) may at times become obstacles rather than accelerators precisely because they are exposed to multiple and conflicting influences; conversely, more cohesive peripheral clusters may function as incubators of innovation, shielding pioneer actors from external pressures and providing the reinforcement necessary for the initial stabilisation of new practices[[28]](#footnote-28). The literature therefore enables networks to be analysed as environments that shape the credibility, scalability, and durability of collective action, rather than as neutral channels through which practices merely circulate (Borgatti et al., 2018).

A fourth dimension concerns power, brokerage, and asymmetry. Networks are often associated, at least normatively, with predominantly horizontal organisational forms. The literature demonstrates, however, that relational arrangements do not eliminate hierarchy; rather, they frequently relocate it, rendering it less formal, less "traditional", and at times less immediately visible. Positions of centrality, brokerage, and bridging thus become particularly significant when seeking to understand who is capable of defining priorities, connecting otherwise separate domains, and shaping the conditions of exchange within a broader field[[29]](#footnote-29). This entails considering who contributes to “programming” the orientations and objectives of the network and who acts as a “switcher” between different circuits, connecting arenas that would otherwise remain only weakly linked and extending the reach of the network while simultaneously shaping the conditions of coordination among these domains[[30]](#footnote-30).

Brokerage should not be understood as a neutral or merely technical activity. Actors occupying intermediary positions do more than facilitate contact; they may influence which resources circulate, which claims gain visibility, and which connections become effective at the political or organisational level. The work on structural holes is particularly valuable because it highlights the advantages of occupying an intermediary position between otherwise unconnected clusters. On the one hand, there are informational advantages, such as early access to information, opportunities, and referrals; on the other, there are control advantages, which arise when the actor actively intervenes in the relationships among other actors and in the conditions governing their coordination[[31]](#footnote-31). Actors occupying such positions may acquire influence by connecting groups that would otherwise remain separate, acting not merely as intermediaries but as mediators in Latour's (2005) sense: they do not simply convey content or claims, but translate, reformulate, and render them operational across different organisational and political contexts, thereby helping to regulate access across relational boundaries.

This is also the terrain of the classical logic of the “tertius gaudens”[[32]](#footnote-32), according to which the third actor positioned between otherwise unconnected parties may derive strategic advantage from brokerage itself, influencing both the circulation of information and the conditions of coordination. In this case, the advantage lies not only in receiving information before others, but also in the more active capacity to shape preferences, timing, and mutual dependencies among actors who do not directly control their own relationship[[33]](#footnote-33). Within democratic networks, such positions are also significant because they may create new opportunities for gatekeeping and selective influence, insofar as they enable actors to filter, select, or slow the movement of information, resources, and access across the network[[34]](#footnote-34). The resulting asymmetries do not always coincide with unilateral domination, since they may also express forms of complementarity among heterogeneous nodes, whose interdependence contributes to the survival, operational continuity, and coordinating capacity of the network itself (Stalder, 2006). Network theory is particularly valuable here because it enables power to be analysed as an effect of position within a relational structure, rather than as something exhausted by formal authority or direct control over resources.

A fifth dimension concerns identity, meaning, and political orientation. Networks also constitute spaces in which actors define who they are, what they seek to defend, and how they interpret the environments within which they operate. The literature suggests that collective identities frequently emerge through attempts to stabilise an orientation under conditions of uncertainty, turbulence, or institutional discontinuity, rather than through the development of a pre-existing unity[[35]](#footnote-35). Identity formation may be understood as an effort to secure a temporary point of support within unstable environments, enabling dispersed actors to orient themselves and render their collective presence more intelligible. In other words, actors seek a new “footing”, which White (2008) defines as the search for a “stance”—that is, a social foothold or stable point of support necessary for navigating chaos. Footing provides identities with a perspective and a reference point from which to process information and evaluate other actors, making it possible to transform the turbulence of events into ordered and meaningful relationships, and thus to move from a condition of paralysing uncertainty to a position from which control can be exercised and interaction oriented. This point is particularly relevant to democratic networks that emerge in response to backlash or the erosion of representative institutions. Such formations frequently become spaces in which resistance gradually develops into a more explicit project, and within which actors elaborate shared vocabularies and a more recognisable sense of collective direction, thereby constituting themselves as subjects capable of redefining orientations, values, and possibilities for action in relation to the institutional environment. The transition from an “resistance identity” to a “project identity” – as defined by Castells (2010a, 2010b) - therefore assumes particular significance within this framework, as it marks the shift from defensive aggregation towards a more explicit attempt to redefine political horizons and possibilities for institutional dialogue.

The capacity to move across discursive and organisational domains thus becomes central. Democratic networks frequently operate between civic and institutional registers, between local experiences and broader political imaginaries, and between practical coordination and the production of meaning. What is often at stake is the capacity to traverse distinct relational and discursive domains, in forms that may more precisely be understood as processes of “netdom switching”, while maintaining a sufficiently stable footing to navigate between different civic and institutional registers[[36]](#footnote-36). The ability of actors to translate across these levels helps to explain how networks acquire coherence and public legibility over time—a legibility that depends not only upon human discourses and practices but also upon non-human actors such as platforms, protocols, and documents, which contribute to stabilising the organisational continuity and public recognisability of the network[[37]](#footnote-37). This work of translation is particularly significant when democratic actors must connect situated experiences and locally grounded claims with broader arenas of visibility, coordination, and power operating at a greater distance from everyday life.

Actors do not merely transmit information; rather, they actively transform and reinterpret it in order to render it operational across different contexts. Such an effort is necessary to connect situated experiences rooted in the “space of places” with the arenas of the “space of flows”, namely the technological and organisational infrastructure within which global power and wealth are concentrated in real time. Networking itself may therefore also be understood as a response to the “disjunction” between these two spatial logics—a divide that tends to separate the processes of global power from experience rooted in particular places[[38]](#footnote-38). Through inclusion within transnational networks, local realities may indeed become “strategic sites”[[39]](#footnote-39) capable of influencing global governance, mitigating the isolation of local contexts from flows of power while assuming roles of innovation and leadership. This challenge unfolds within a condition of disjunction also described as “structural schizophrenia”[[40]](#footnote-40), in which the dominant functions of society are projected into abstract global networks, whereas experience and identity remain anchored in local territories, producing a profound fracture between power and meaning. Translation must furthermore synthesise the time of lived experience, characterised by the linear and predictable rhythms of everyday life, with the “timeless time” of power flows, in which new technologies compress temporal sequences into instantaneousness or fragment them into an ahistorical present. Democratic networks act here as critical connectors seeking to restore social control and political meaning within global flows that would otherwise remain detached from the lives of citizens[[41]](#footnote-41). Identity formation, far from constituting an ancillary symbolic layer added only subsequently, should therefore be regarded as part of the organisational work through which networks construct internal coherence and legitimacy.

A sixth dimension concerns scale, architecture, and systemic vulnerability. Networks do not expand, consolidate, or endure according to a single pattern. Their resilience depends upon factors such as topology, the distribution of central nodes, and the concentration and distribution of connectivity. The literature demonstrates that highly connected hubs may increase efficiency and extend reach while simultaneously generating forms of systemic vulnerability whenever excessive coordination depends upon a limited number of nodes. This vulnerability is particularly characteristic of “scale-free” architectures, in which the distribution of ties follows a power law that favours the emergence of highly concentrated hubs[[42]](#footnote-42). Such networks possess no average or characteristic node defining their scale: the vast majority of nodes have very few connections, whereas a small elite of highly connected nodes concentrates a large proportion of the available ties. This structure typically emerges through processes of growth and preferential attachment, according to a “rich-get-richer” dynamic in which new nodes tend to connect with those that are already the most visible and best connected. Over time, this dynamic may reinforce existing asymmetries and make entry more difficult for less established actors. The result is a system characterised by an Achilles' heel: on the one hand, the network is highly robust to random failures, since peripheral nodes are statistically more likely to be affected; on the other, it remains extremely fragile in the face of targeted attacks. Indeed, the deliberate removal of even a small proportion of the principal hubs may compromise systemic coordination by fragmenting the network into isolated clusters and drastically reducing its operational capacity. The very architecture that renders a network relatively robust to random losses at the periphery may therefore make it vulnerable whenever central nodes are intentionally targeted or unexpectedly removed[[43]](#footnote-43).

Concepts such as scale-free structures, preferential attachment, and cumulative advantage help to explain why some actors progressively become more central while others remain peripheral, and why emerging initiatives often struggle to obtain recognition, legitimacy, or resources within fields already dominated by established hubs[[44]](#footnote-44). At the same time, the literature shows that many collective formations do not grow through a single centre of command but rather expand through overlapping memberships, shared projects, and indirect ties connecting actors across different contexts and levels[[45]](#footnote-45). Such configurations are particularly significant because affiliation structures and two-mode networks—relational systems composed of two distinct sets of nodes, typically actors and events or organisations, in which ties exist only between the two classes and not within them—tend to generate “small-world” effects[[46]](#footnote-46). This phenomenon, formalised by Watts and Strogatz (1998, in Watts, 2004), refers to networks characterised by high local density and very short global distances, a structure that contracts overall social distances through the presence of relational shortcuts and enables actors that are organisationally or territorially distant to remain connected through only a limited number of intermediary steps[[47]](#footnote-47).

Work on modularity, affiliation structures, and two-mode networks thus clarifies how relatively distinct groups may remain connected without being absorbed into a single fully unified organisation. Such configurations effectively express a network logic in which organisational flexibility and coordination are combined through protocols, interfaces, and shared forms of connection rather than through rigid hierarchy[[48]](#footnote-48). The overlap among projects, hubs, and memberships may therefore be understood not merely as an organisational by-product but also as a source of distributed connectivity that strengthens the network's overall coordinating capacity, not least because shortcuts and long-distance ties can reduce global distances without dissolving local clusters[[49]](#footnote-49). This makes it possible to analyse networks of networks as layered and adaptive formations whose durability depends upon the relationship between local embeddedness, distributed connectivity, and the capacity to coordinate across scales without crystallising into rigid hierarchies. The network-of-networks model is also valuable because it demonstrates how initiatives of global scope may become embedded within specific cultural and linguistic contexts through local nodes performing functions of translation, learning, and collective coordination[[50]](#footnote-50).

A seventh dimension concerns governance, institutional articulation, and the relationship with public authority. Coordination frequently takes shape through relational arrangements that cannot be reduced either to market exchange or bureaucratic command. Governance may therefore develop through negotiation, brokerage, mutual adaptation, and the gradual alignment of operationally autonomous actors operating across different levels[[51]](#footnote-51). The rise of networking may be understood as a functional response to the processes of fragmentation, differentiation, and dynamism that characterise contemporary society[[52]](#footnote-52). Within a context characterised by complex or wicked problems[[53]](#footnote-53), the coordination of a plurality of heterogeneous and interdependent actors can no longer be guaranteed exclusively through bureaucratic hierarchies, often regarded as excessively rigid, or through market mechanisms, which are incapable of independently managing social complexity and negative externalities[[54]](#footnote-54). Networking thus emerges as a polycentric “third way”[[55]](#footnote-55), a governance instrument capable of recomposing the fragmentation of the public sector and society through negotiation and the strategic management of those brokerage positions that have become essential for restoring governability to otherwise fragmented systems[[56]](#footnote-56). Network coordination does not eliminate uncertainty but provides instruments for managing it through mutual learning, strategic interdependence, ad collaboration among actors possessing different resources [[57]](#footnote-57). Democratic networks frequently occupy spaces of intersection between civic action and institutional engagement, where informal practices coexist with formalised procedures and where cooperation and competition can—and to some extent must—coexist. It follows that conflict should not be regarded as a threat to network cohesion but as a potential driver of innovation and reorientation, provided that a minimum degree of convergence remains necessary to sustain collective action over time[[58]](#footnote-58).

The European context renders this aspect particularly visible: public authority is distributed across supranational, national, regional, and local levels, and networks derive part of their significance from their capacity to articulate relationships across these different scales. The European Union may therefore be understood as the most advanced example of the network state, that is, a configuration which does not abolish nation states but incorporates them into a system of shared sovereignty intended to enhance their collective power within global flows[[59]](#footnote-59). This arrangement expresses a variable geometry of sovereignty that corresponds to what Bull[[60]](#footnote-60) termed neo-medievalism, namely a condition in which political power is not recomposed within a fully unified centre but is instead distributed across overlapping authorities and regulatory regimes that must continually negotiate their respective competences. Accordingly, the indeterminacy and overlap of levels of authority become constitutive features of coordination, which is achieved through a multinodal network rather than through a centralised hierarchy[[61]](#footnote-61). This perspective also makes it possible to observe how policy networks may consolidate around already established circles of expertise, funding, and institutional access, thereby facilitating coordination for some actors while quietly restricting the effective inclusiveness of governance for others[[62]](#footnote-62).

Networking thus assumes the character of a functional necessity for political arrangements called upon to govern interdependencies extending beyond state boundaries. This interpretation may be further refined through the concept of metagovernance, which captures the ways in which public authorities and institutions regulate networks of relatively autonomous actors not through direct hierarchical command but through network design, framing, facilitation, and incentives, without absorbing them into a centralised chain of command and while preserving their operational autonomy[[63]](#footnote-63). The European Union may furthermore be described as a network of networks, within which coordination and solidarity are articulated through a plurality of mutually interdependent organisational, institutional, and territorial connections[[64]](#footnote-64). Within this context, the European Commission may be interpreted as an actor engaged in network management insofar as it supports, steers, and coordinates transnational networks through non-hierarchical instruments, such as the funding of umbrella organisations, the establishment of expert committees, and the use of performance technologies including benchmarking and recommendations[[65]](#footnote-65).

This dimension also opens up a broader set of questions that are central to this volume. Governance concerns not only the formal distribution of authority but also the informal arrangements through which decisions are prepared, validated, or discreetly redirected[[66]](#footnote-66). It entails analysing the ways in which strategic choices are made and the effective degree of influence exercised by members, demonstrating how coordination frequently rests with a restricted group of highly professionalised actors. These actors do not merely shape decisions; they also define operational protocols (technical, legal, and financial), establishing the rules of engagement of the network and determining who is able to act and who, being unable to comply with such standards, is effectively excluded from communicative and decision-making flows (Stalder, 2006). Network governance may therefore generate forms of structural closure by imposing access criteria and modes of coordination that prove exclusionary for actors lacking the resources or competences required to decode them fully. This invites greater attention to the management of conflicts, the balance between organisational autonomy and collective coherence, and the risk that network governance reproduces forms of exclusion whenever access is filtered through established circles, large member organisations, professional elites, or actors enjoying privileged access to resources and institutions[[67]](#footnote-67). Consequently, governance may remain formally open while becoming substantively selective, particularly where policy networks consolidate around already recognised actors and where connection does not translate equally into effective influence (Lin, 2001), especially insofar as central positions tend to be occupied by organisations possessing greater resources, professional capacities, or privileged access to funding and expert communities (McLevey et al., 2024). The negotiated and frequently informal nature of many network arenas produces a persistent tension between transparency and confidentiality: whereas confidentiality facilitates mediation and compromise, excessively stringent transparency requirements may reduce actors' capacity to negotiate effectively and to reach shared solutions[[68]](#footnote-68). This dynamic risks diminishing the public visibility of decision-making processes, exposing networks to criticism for operating in a form of technocratic “darkness”, removed from public scrutiny[[69]](#footnote-69).

An eighth dimension concerns knowledge production, mediation, and socio-technical infrastructure. Networks connect different types of actors, but also modes of knowing, coordination devices, and infrastructures that enable and stabilise action at a distance. The literature makes it possible to move beyond the idea that information circulates transparently within networks: what circulates is often the result of constant work of selection and reformulation, whereby documents, protocols, platforms, and technical artefacts decisively shape the conditions under which knowledge is shared and used[[70]](#footnote-70). These supports should not be understood as neutral containers of communication, but as socio-technical infrastructures, understood as material assemblages that shape social relations and provide a grammar of coordination at a distance. Since contemporary democratic networks rely largely on digital infrastructures and operate in geographically dispersed contexts, such design tools define the space of discussion and influence the degree of participation. In some cases, these infrastructures may be interpreted as digital commons, insofar as they are designed and governed as shared resources, oriented towards the public interest and open to collective reuse, in addition to their function as supports for coordination[[71]](#footnote-71). Moreover, a significant part of what sustains coordination may remain tacit, informal, or unrecorded, making the capacity to preserve and transmit situated knowledge a central organisational challenge. Organisational continuity also depends upon the capacity to preserve and reactivate histories, interpretative traces, and schemes of meaning that enable actors to orient themselves in transitions between different domains, maintaining coherence even when much knowledge remains implicit or distributed[[72]](#footnote-72)

This dimension also enables a more precise analysis of functions, since some networks operate primarily as knowledge intermediaries, others as advocacy platforms, service providers, agenda-setters, training environments, or hybrid configurations combining multiple roles. Operational language is also relevant, since it affects accessibility, visibility, and the capacity to build broader publics. Specialist jargon and restricted linguistic codes may produce genuine cultural holes[[73]](#footnote-73), limiting the possibility for some actors to move across environments that are proximate yet socially and symbolically separated, and thereby reducing the possibility of widening participation[[74]](#footnote-74).

This analysis concerns the collective learning capacity of networks, understood as the process through which knowledge is disseminated, assessed, and translated among members[[75]](#footnote-75). Network formations differ significantly in their capacity to manage environmental ambiguity through processes of distributed communication and routine problem-solving. This dynamism enables the network to detect signs of failure in established routines and to move beyond them, building new channels of coordination that render the structure “*ultrarobust*” in the face of uncertainty[[76]](#footnote-76). Equally essential is the capacity to circulate tacit knowledge, preventing it from remaining confined within expert enclaves and transforming it into a fluid resource distributed across nodes[[77]](#footnote-77). This process activates dynamics of learning by using and learning by doing: this cycle of reworking practices enables what Castells[[78]](#footnote-78) defines as “self-programmable labour” not merely to receive instructions, but actively to reconfigure the applications and architectures of the network itself. From this perspective, knowledge is not simply a product inserted into network activity, but operates as a genuine epistemic infrastructure[[79]](#footnote-79) through which the network is maintained and reproduced. This process may be described as “autocatalytic”, insofar as the network self-organises and persists through the continuous reconstruction of its own nodes and relations[[80]](#footnote-80). The knowledge thus produced may be understood as a form of collective intelligence treated as a common good, aimed at strengthening social autonomy against the extractive logics of digital capitalism[[81]](#footnote-81). Some networks operate specifically as knowledge brokers, that is, as actors that select and adapt information in order to connect otherwise disconnected groups[[82]](#footnote-82). The circulation of knowledge may also assume a *glocalised form*, in which innovations developed within local or regional contexts are coordinated through decentralised networks capable of addressing cross-border problems[[83]](#footnote-83).

This dynamic is evident in technopolitical experiences in which interfaces, codes, and protocols are deliberately designed to reconfigure the power relations that structure participation, as shown by the case of Decidim[[84]](#footnote-84). In e-participation devices, ICTs are used to reduce the distance between citizens and government and to redefine modes of access and interaction within decision-making processes[[85]](#footnote-85). Online platforms for public participation further show how digital environments may sustain deliberative practices and the co-production of knowledge at the urban scale. Certain digital participatory architectures may be understood as environments for mobilising collective intelligence, capable of activating distributed competences and transforming them into resources useful for solving complex public problems[[86]](#footnote-86).

A ninth dimension concerns inequality, access, and the differentiated capacity to benefit from connection. Connection does not automatically translate into an equal capacity for influence, insofar as networks distribute opportunities unequally, and access to relevant ties does not guarantee that actors are equally able to shape agendas, obtain recognition, or transform participation into effective political presence. Differences in visibility, sponsorship, affiliation with central nodes, and the capacity to convert contacts into outcomes contribute to the stratification of networked fields[[87]](#footnote-87). Inequality depends not only upon a possible “capital deficit”, but also upon a “return deficit”, namely the inability to obtain comparable returns from formally accessible ties because of positional constraints, institutional discrimination, or lower recognition. In other words, what matters is not only whether actors are connected, but whether they are equally able to convert connection into recognition, agenda-setting capacity, and durable organisational advantage (Lin, 2001). This aspect is analytically central to democratic networks, since inclusion may remain formally open while becoming substantively unequal in practice. In these contexts, power often operates not through formal exclusion or direct repression, but through quieter forms of marginalisation or selective disconnection, which leave some actors formally present yet substantively switched off or irrelevant[[88]](#footnote-88).

The question is not only how connected a network is, but who is actually admitted to its decision-making arenas and with what resources, since network closure may restrict access and reduce the possibility of contestation by marginal or less organised actors[[89]](#footnote-89). In some arenas, such dynamics may take the form of configurations close to what have been defined as “velvet triangles”[[90]](#footnote-90), namely relatively restricted circles of institutional actors, experts, and organisational leaders who concentrate access, expertise, and brokerage capacity. These dynamics also point to a management of resources in which the unequal distribution of funding and professional expertise tends to reinforce internal hierarchies and managerial logics, producing forms of “organizational incivility” or “diminished democracy” that are difficult to reconcile with the public image of open and inclusive networks[[91]](#footnote-91).

A classic trade-off between efficiency and democracy emerges here: restricted and dense networks may prove faster and more effective in decision-making terms, but they also tend to raise more acute problems of legitimacy, inclusion, and public accountability than more open configurations[[92]](#footnote-92). This problem recalls the paradox of interest representation: the intensification of professional lobbying and the exchange of “access goods” may strengthen organised presence within institutional arenas without automatically translating into an expansion of civil society oriented towards the common good[[93]](#footnote-93).

The extension of networks into digital space does not automatically neutralise existing inequalities, since visibility and the capacity for information circulation often tend to reproduce hierarchies already present offline, including those connected to geographical position and location within the global system[[94]](#footnote-94). Network governance may also generate forms of “institutionalized domination”, in which arbitrary power derives not so much from explicit commands as from the capacity of already included participants to orient rules, access, and priorities in ways that are difficult for those outside or at the margins to contest. The risk is that some networks progressively close themselves off, reducing transparency and weakening the presence of critical voices from wider civil society[[95]](#footnote-95).

Several mechanisms and possible dynamics deserve attention: actors with limited resources may be present but exercise limited influence; peripheral members may remain connected while struggling to shape priorities; new entrants may depend heavily upon the sponsorship of already central organisations in order to obtain recognition, funding, or institutional access[[96]](#footnote-96). Inequalities—as noted above—may assume less visible forms, including linguistic asymmetries, barriers connected to mobility and participation costs, dependence upon larger members, the concentration of power within central hubs, and divisions between centre and periphery. The internal life of networks may be further shaped by excessive professionalisation, “invisible work”[[97]](#footnote-97), burnout, symbolic inclusion without effective influence, or the silent exit of members who no longer find meaningful space within the organisation. This stratification may be further aggravated by cognitive and evaluative biases that lead to the systematic underestimation of the contribution of peripheral or marginalised actors, even when they perform essential organisational functions or occupy structurally relevant positions[[98]](#footnote-98). These dynamics are significant because advantage tends to accumulate relationally: actors that are already visible, trusted, and well positioned are generally better able to attract further resources, attention, and opportunities for coordination[[99]](#footnote-99). This dimension therefore makes it possible to illuminate the relational mechanisms through which inequality is reproduced, normalised, or at times contested within formations that publicly present themselves as open, plural, and democratic.

Taken together, these dimensions show why reconnecting with network theory is necessary for constructing an analytical vocabulary adequate to the object of this volume. The literature offers a repertoire of tools for analysing relational configuration, embedded trust, diffusion, brokerage, identity formation, scale, institutional articulation, socio-technical mediation, unequal access, and so forth. At the same time, this volume focuses on a set of questions that remain only partially integrated within the existing literature and that become particularly visible when democratic networks are analysed as evolving organisational formations under pressure. Its contribution lies in bringing these strands into relation within a common analytical framework and in showing how democratic networks should indeed be understood as arenas of connection, but above all as unequal, adaptive, and politically significant organisational formations.

**3. A Minimal Analytical Grid**

The discussion developed thus far points towards a more structured analytical framework for the study of democratic networks. The proposed grid is organised around a limited number of principal dimensions—six in total, to which three cross-cutting dimensions are added—each of which brings together a more specific set of analytical questions. The dimensions identified here are not intended as a closed taxonomy, but rather as a heuristic structure that will make it possible to examine and compare empirical cases in a systematic, ordered, and synoptic manner, while at the same time preserving sensitivity to variation and internal tensions.

From a comparative perspective, it may also be useful to distinguish collaborative governance arrangements along institutional and geopolitical axes, in order to map the great variety of existing systems of authority. This taxonomy makes it possible to differentiate, along the institutional axis, between purely governmental configurations (Type 1), collaborative arrangements between authorities and non-state actors (Type 2), and alliances composed exclusively of non-state actors (Type 3). Along the geopolitical axis, it is possible to distinguish between domestic forms of cooperation (Type A), cross-border forms (Type B)—which focus on areas physically adjacent to borders—and interstate or transnational configurations (Type C), operating at a global or interregional scale. The intersection of these dimensions generates a matrix of nine types that makes it possible to analyse who participates in governance, how it is structured, and within which territorial sphere the actors operate [[100]](#footnote-100).

Organisational identity, trajectory, and positionality concern the ways in which a network defines itself, presents its public role, and reworks that self-understanding and self-definition over time. This area of analysis includes organisational form, public self-description, democratic mandate, prevailing function, reputation and perceived threats, branding, and the temporal trajectory of the network through phases of emergence, consolidation, reorientation, or expansion. It also leaves room for a possible gap between the way in which a network describes itself and the way in which it appears when observed through its actual relational structure. What emerges here is an understanding of identity as an organisational and political accomplishment, shaped through historical development, strategic positioning, and reflexive self-interpretation. It may be useful to distinguish between “serendipitous” networking trajectories, which emerge from spontaneous interactions and contingent opportunities without a predefined collective objective, and “goal-directed” trajectories, characterised by a teleological movement towards a desired state and constructed around explicit purposes of coordination or intervention[[101]](#footnote-101).

Membership, composition, and internal inclusion direct attention to the conditions under which actors enter, remain within, and participate meaningfully in the network. The analysis therefore extends beyond the simple question of who belongs to the organisation and considers the practical terms through which belonging is structured and sustained. This area includes the diversity of actor composition, the basis of membership, openness of access, the costs of participation, and the material or linguistic barriers that may limit effective inclusion. It also draws attention to the ways in which internal asymmetries are reproduced through differences in resources, geographical position, institutional capacity, proximity to the organisational centre, and the self-referential discourse or language of the leadership group. Under this heading, it becomes possible to examine symbolic inclusion, silent exit, dependence upon larger members, centre-periphery inequalities, structural asymmetries, and the unequal capacity of actors to exercise voice within formally open arrangements.

Governance, coordination, and democratic coherence direct attention towards the organisation of collective authority, the practical coordination of activities, and the relationship between declared principles and internal procedures. This area encompasses formal governance arrangements, decision-making procedures, internal coordination among activities and partners, conflict management, and the management of the tension between cooperation and competition. It also requires careful consideration of less visible dynamics, including informal opacity, the concentration of power within restricted circles, brokerage roles, wished-for ties, and the role of rotation in redistributing responsibilities over time. Democratic coherence is particularly relevant in this regard, since it makes it possible to ask whether the principles and methods promoted externally are reflected, contradicted, or only partially realised in the internal life of the network. Ethical charters, manifestos, and similar normative devices may therefore be treated as attempts to stabilise expectations, regulate conduct, and manage internal pluralism. This same area also includes geographical and thematic clustering, organisational fatigue, and the unequal distribution of invisible work, insofar as these aspects shape the practical conditions under which coordination is maintained, as well as the practical dilemmas of network management, including the tension between loyalty to one's own organisation and loyalty to the network, which may generate conflicts between individual commitments and collective purposes Sørensen and Torfing (2007).

Resources, formalisation, and organisational sustainability concern the material and legal conditions that enable collective action to endure over time. This area includes funding structure, dependence on grants, pressure from funders, dependence upon large members, degree of formalisation, level of institutionalisation, and legal status. It also concerns the ways in which these elements affect autonomy, flexibility, continuity, and exposure to risk. This field includes questions concerning whether legal form strengthens recognition or produces rigidity, whether institutionalisation expands influence or narrows room for manoeuvre, and whether merger with other networks appears as an opportunity or a threat. Organisational sustainability is therefore understood in a broad sense, as the capacity to preserve operational continuity without eroding mission or adaptive capacity.

External relations and legitimacy concern the way in which the network positions itself within a broader context inhabited by different types of actors. This dimension examines patterns of collaboration, strategic partnerships, relations with public institutions, international scope, and the capacity to maintain a recognisable position within fields that are often politically competitive and normatively contested. It also includes legitimacy in a stronger sense, namely the capacity to remain credible among different publics, including academic, civic, and political ones, as well as the risks and opportunities associated with so-called coopetition, multi-scalar articulation, and the tension between advocacy and service provision. Legitimacy cannot be measured only in terms of effectiveness or access, but also in terms of deliberative accountability, understood as the capacity of the network to render visible and contestable the reasons that guide decision-making processes. This approach implies that representatives must justify their actions in moral and rational terms before differentiated publics, ensuring that control is substantive and not merely symbolic[[102]](#footnote-102).

Knowledge, learning, and mediation concern what a network produces, how knowledge circulates within it, and the practices of mediation through which such knowledge becomes usable and politically relevant. This area includes the network's prevailing function as a knowledge broker, advocacy actor, service provider, agenda-setter, training environment, or hybrid configuration combining these functions. It also encompasses the role attributed to knowledge, the production of evidence, the network's operational language in its internal and external dimensions, the relationship between research, communication, and advocacy, and the capacity to learn through reflection on practice. This area also includes the management of tacit and off-record knowledge, the approach to best practices and bad practices, the possible organisation of awards or forms of recognition relating to practices regarded as robust, outreach capacity, intercultural translation, and the use of digital tools. Particular attention may be paid here to the forms through which knowledge is circulated, maintained, and made accessible, as well as to the risk that expertise may remain concentrated within restricted circles or that relevant forms of knowledge may remain only weakly formalised and institutionalised.

Three further dimensions cut across all the others and are therefore treated as transversal: adaptation, resilience, and transformative capacity; commoning; and impacts. The first directs attention to the ways in which networks respond to pressure and reconfigure themselves under changing and critical political and organisational conditions. The second concerns the extent to which networking is treated as a common good through relational practices, forms of shared ownership, processes of collective value creation, reciprocity, co-production, and inclusive infrastructures for collaboration. Networking may be understood as a common good not only when it brings different actors into relation, but when it produces and maintains shared resources—knowledge, protocols, infrastructures, and coordinating capacities—whose usefulness requires the adoption of rules of self-organisation and collective practices of care and use, so that the resource remains sustainable and accessible over time (Ostrom, 1990, in Sørensen and Torfing, 2007). The third concerns impacts, considering their scale, visibility, and possible unexpected or negative outcomes, together with the network's capacity to strengthen existing alliances. It also includes the cognitive and discursive dimension of the effects produced, such as agenda-setting, the introduction of new interpretative frames, the legitimation of actors, or the mainstreaming of concepts, methods, or investment orientations. To these are added institutional and policy effects, observable in changes to procedures, rules, policies, or formal decisions, as well as societal and practical effects, such as widespread learning, lessons learned, capacity-building, awareness raising, and the mobilisation of new publics.

Taken together, these dimensions provide the framework through which the remainder of the volume addresses democratic networks across different contexts and trajectories. Their purpose is certainly not to remove complexity, but to refine and organise it, so that the cases discussed in the following chapters may be more readily read through a common analytical language without flattening their differences.
