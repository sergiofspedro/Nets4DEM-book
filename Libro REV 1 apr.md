**Networks for Democratic Intensification. Cases, Pathways and an Analytical Framework for Europe**

**Networked democracy in Europe: pathways to democratic intensification**

**Cross-pollinating democracy: networks and democratic innovation in Europe**

**Strengthening democracy through networks: evidence-based pathways in Europe**

This book begins from a simple but urgent observation: networks working on democratic participation, deliberation, civic inclusion and democratic innovation are becoming more important at the very moment in which their environment is becoming more fragile. Across Europe, organisations committed to intensifying democracy operate under the pressure of shrinking civic space, declining funding — especially public funding — increasing legal and regulatory constraints in several countries, and a broader erosion of trust in collective intermediaries. In some contexts, civil society organisations and universities face growing restrictions, delegitimation, or even forms of criminalisation. In many others, they face a less visible but equally corrosive condition: people's declining confidence in organised actors that claim to speak, mediate or advocate in the public sphere.

In this context, networking is no longer simply an optional strategy of cooperation. It becomes a way to survive, to pool resources, to produce knowledge, to widen legitimacy, and to defend democratic practices in adverse conditions. Yet networks are not unambiguously democratic forms. They can generate solidarity, but also hierarchy. They can enable cooperation, but they also contain competition — especially in a field where actors often depend on grants, visibility, and institutional recognition. They may advocate democratic pluralism externally while reproducing internally narrow governance structures, unequal influence, or membership models that privilege larger, wealthier, urban and professionalised actors over smaller, poorer, rural or more fragile ones. The democratic value of networks, therefore, cannot be assumed. It has to be examined.

This volume argues that existing analytical frameworks are not yet strong enough for that task. The literature on democratic innovations is richer than the literature on the organisational networks that sustain, circulate, defend and scale them. Concepts such as networks, network of networks, alliance, hub, platform, ecosystem, or highly networked organisation are often used loosely, even though they refer to different forms of coordination, membership, governance and power. The field is rich in practice but still conceptually uneven.

The book therefore proposes an operational analytical framework built inductively from practice. It draws first on the empirical material already produced within Nets4Dem, especially the two Social Network Analyses[[1]](#footnote-1), which were explicitly conceived not only as descriptive maps but as reflective and maieutic tools for collective discussion. Those analyses already reveal asymmetries, thematic and geographical gaps, vulnerabilities in consolidation, tensions between internal democratic coherence and external action, and the need to clarify what kind of network is actually being built. They also show that most of the relevant actors are not, strictly speaking, networks of networks, but highly networked organisations or mixed forms whose role still needs to be better understood.

To strengthen this initial base, the book adds a limited number of dense interviews with five existing networks or highly networked organisations working on democratic participation. The cases were selected to maximise analytical variation across the dimensions most relevant to the book's argument: organisational maturity, geographic scale, funding model, degree of formalisation, and relationship to public institutions. The purpose is not to produce a census of the field, but to deepen the dimensions that matter most in a time of democratic stress: how networks adapt their membership, funding, legal forms and governance; how they navigate cooperation and competition; how they relate to institutions; and how they build resilience and influence across levels of governance.

At the centre of that framework lies a precise analytical concept — *democratic intensification* — understood as the process by which participatory practices become more embedded in institutional and civic life, more connected across local, national and European levels of governance, and more capable of producing durable influence on public decisions. This concept is distinct from democratic consolidation, which refers to the stabilisation of formal institutions; from democratic resilience, which refers to the capacity to resist erosion; and from democratic deepening, which refers to increases in the quality or inclusiveness of participation at a given scale. Intensification implies movement: across levels, across actors, and across time. It is the evaluative lens through which the book reads its cases, extracts its pathways, and formulates its final questions.

In that sense, the book is both analytical and practical. It is meant to clarify concepts, extract patterns from cases, and provide a usable tool for networks that are trying to understand what they are becoming — and how they might become more democratic, more resilient and more consequential in Europe.
