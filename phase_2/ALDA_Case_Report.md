# ALDA — European Association for Local Democracy
## Analytical Case Report — Phase 2 (Nets4DEM)

> **Sources:** IFP1 — Antonella Valmorbida (Secretary General, ALDA) and ITR6 — Marco Boaria (ALDA Director of Programmes, EPD Board member). Two interviews, June 2026.

---

## 1. Case Overview

**What ALDA is:** The European Association for Local Democracy (ALDA) is a membership-based organisation founded in 1999, headquartered in Strasbourg with offices across Europe. It operates at the intersection of local authorities and civil society, with a distinctive membership model that brings together municipalities, regions, associations, and individual citizens. ALDA is best known for its network of Local Democracy Agencies (LDAs) — locally rooted, multi-stakeholder organisations operating primarily in the Western Balkans, Eastern Partnership countries, and the broader European neighbourhood — that serve as instruments of decentralised cooperation, citizen participation, and democratic capacity-building.

**Central sources:** Antonella Valmorbida (IFP1), ALDA's Secretary General, provides the foundational narrative of ALDA's identity, evolution, and values. Marco Boaria (ITR6), ALDA's Director of Programmes and a Board member of the European Partnership for Democracy (EPD), provides complementary insight into ALDA's financial model, project-driven tensions, and multi-network positioning.

**What the case is mainly about:** ALDA represents a hybrid organisation that combines the characteristics of a membership association, a project-implementing NGO, and a network of local democracy agencies. This hybridity is simultaneously ALDA's strength — enabling it to bridge the civil society-local government divide — and its vulnerability, creating tensions between membership democracy and project-driven imperatives, between headquarter strategy and LDA autonomy, and between a European identity and a global ambition.

## 2. Dimension-by-Dimension Analysis

### Dimension 1: Organisational Identity — A Hybrid Between Association, Network, and NGO

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP1, Antonella Valmorbida; ITR6, Marco Boaria)

*Multi-stakeholder identity:* ALDA distinguishes itself from purely governmental networks (like OIDP) and from purely civil society networks (like People Powered) by bringing both sectors together as co-equal members. Antonella describes ALDA as "a membership-based organisation which brings together local authorities and civil society on an equal footing."

*The Local Democracy Agency model:* The LDA network — locally registered organisations with their own governance structures, typically involving local authorities, civil society organisations, and international partners — is ALDA's most distinctive organisational innovation. Each LDA is legally and operationally autonomous but networked through ALDA's coordinating structure. This creates a federated rather than centralised model.

*Evolution from association to broader network:* ALDA was founded in 1999 by the Council of Europe's Congress of Local and Regional Authorities. Over 25 years, it has expanded from a European-focused association into an organisation with a global reach and a diversified portfolio spanning citizen participation, local governance, youth engagement, and European integration support.

*Positioning between service provider and movement:* Marco describes the tension: ALDA is simultaneously a membership organisation accountable to its members, a project-implementing body accountable to donors (primarily the EU), and an advocacy network promoting local democracy. These three identities do not always align, and the project-driven identity has grown disproportionately due to funding structures.

### Dimension 2: Membership — Multi-Level, Multi-Sector, Multi-Geography

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP1, Antonella Valmorbida; ITR6, Marco Boaria)

*Three membership categories:* ALDA's membership comprises local authorities (municipalities, regions), civil society organisations (associations, NGOs, foundations), and individual members. This structure reflects ALDA's founding principle that local democracy requires both democratic institutions and an active civil society.

*Geographic diversity:* Members span across the European Union, the Western Balkans, Eastern Partnership countries, and increasingly the Southern Mediterranean and Sub-Saharan Africa. This geographic spread creates significant asymmetries in member capacity, resources, and institutional contexts.

*The membership-project tension:* Marco highlights a structural challenge: ALDA has grown substantially through EU-funded projects, bringing new partners into its orbit. But project partners are not necessarily members, and members are not necessarily project partners. This creates a "two-speed" organisation where the project-driven side and the membership-driven side coexist without full integration.

*LDA autonomy and ALDA membership:* LDAs are members of ALDA's network but operate with considerable local autonomy. Antonella describes them as "locally rooted organisations with their own governance and their own dynamics." The relationship between ALDA's central structure and individual LDAs varies — some are deeply integrated, others more loosely connected.

### Dimension 3: Governance — Democratic Structure in a Project-Driven Organisation

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP1, Antonella Valmorbida; ITR6, Marco Boaria)

*Formal governance:* ALDA has a General Assembly (all members), a Governing Board (elected from members), a President, and a Secretary General (Antonella). The governance structure follows standard association models with democratic elections and representation.

*Governance in practice — the project gravity:* Marco observes that despite the formal democratic structure, much of ALDA's actual strategic direction is shaped by its project portfolio rather than by its membership governance. EU-funded projects define activities, partnerships, and geographic priorities. The member-driven governance and the donor-driven programming exist in parallel.

*The central-LDA tension:* Each LDA has its own governance structure, creating a federated model where the centre coordinates but does not control. Antonella describes this as both a strength (local ownership) and a challenge (coordination complexity, variable quality).

*Internal democratic coherence:* Antonella emphasises that ALDA applies participatory methodologies not just in its external work but internally. However, Marco's account suggests that the project-driven operational model limits the space for member-driven strategic deliberation beyond the formal governance calendar.

### Dimension 4: Resources — EU Dependency and the Projectisation Challenge

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (ITR6, Marco Boaria; IFP1, Antonella Valmorbida)

*Funding structure:* ALDA's funding is predominantly project-based, with the European Union as the primary funder. Additional sources include the Council of Europe, national governments, and private foundations, but EU institutional funding dominates. Marco describes this as both ALDA's lifeline and its strategic constraint.

*Projectisation as structural force:* The project funding model shapes everything — geographic presence (where EU funding is available), thematic focus (what EU programmes prioritise), partnership composition (who can meet EU consortium requirements), and staff profile (project managers dominate over policy or advocacy staff).

*Membership dues as symbolic:* Member contributions form a relatively small portion of ALDA's budget. This financial asymmetry means that members do not exercise the leverage over organisational direction that formal governance suggests.

*Financial sustainability risks:* EU funding cycles and changing priorities create vulnerability. Marco identifies funding diversification as an ongoing strategic priority, but acknowledges that alternatives to EU institutional funding are limited for an organisation of ALDA's scale and scope.

*The paradox of growth:* ALDA has grown significantly through successful EU project acquisition. But this growth has been project-driven rather than strategy-driven, creating an organisation whose shape reflects donor opportunities more than a self-defined strategic plan.

### Dimension 5: External Relations — Between Council of Europe, EU, EPD, and Local Partners

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP1, Antonella Valmorbida; ITR6, Marco Boaria)

*Council of Europe origins:* ALDA was founded by the Council of Europe's Congress of Local and Regional Authorities and maintains a close institutional relationship. This origin shapes ALDA's identity around the Council of Europe's values of human rights, democracy, and the rule of law.

*European Union as primary funder and partner:* The EU is both ALDA's largest funder and a policy environment that ALDA seeks to influence. Antonella describes ALDA's advocacy work in Brussels and Strasbourg aimed at shaping EU programmes to better support local democracy and citizen participation.

*European Partnership for Democracy (EPD):* Marco serves on EPD's Board, and ALDA is a member of EPD. This positions ALDA within a broader ecosystem of democracy support organisations. Marco describes EPD as "a network of organisations working on democracy support," where ALDA brings the local governance and citizen participation dimension.

*The LDA network as a form of external relations:* The LDAs function simultaneously as ALDA's operational arms in specific territories and as independently networked organisations with their own local partnerships. This creates a distributed external relations profile — ALDA's presence in a country is mediated through the LDA, which has its own legitimacy and relationships.

*Relation with other democracy networks:* ALDA maintains relationships with the International Observatory of Participatory Democracy (OIDP), the Congress of Local and Regional Authorities, and various national associations of local authorities. However, Antonella notes that unlike some networks, ALDA's commitment of financial and human resources to its secretariat gives it stability that looser networks lack.

### Dimension 6: Knowledge — Participatory Methods as Methodology and Ethos

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP1, Antonella Valmorbida)

*Participatory methodologies as core practice:* ALDA applies participatory approaches in its project work — citizen assemblies, participatory budgeting, community-led local development — across the territories where it operates. Antonella frames participation as both ALDA's working method and its substantive mission.

*Knowledge transfer across geographies:* The LDA network enables cross-border knowledge sharing — experiences from participatory processes in one Western Balkan municipality can inform practice in another. This peer-learning function is informal but valued.

*The EU integration knowledge niche:* ALDA has developed specific expertise in supporting local governance reforms in EU candidate and neighbourhood countries, particularly around decentralisation, citizen participation, and local government capacity-building. This knowledge is embedded in practitioners rather than systematically codified.

*Limitation — knowledge production as secondary:* ALDA's primary output is project implementation, not research or knowledge production. While individual projects generate lessons and methodology, systematic knowledge codification, publication, and dissemination is relatively underdeveloped compared to organisations whose primary mandate is research or advocacy.

### Dimension 7: Adaptation — Growing Into and Beyond the EU

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP1, Antonella Valmorbida; ITR6, Marco Boaria)

*Geographic diversification:* ALDA has gradually expanded beyond its original Western Balkan focus to the Eastern Partnership, Southern Mediterranean, and Sub-Saharan Africa. This geographic diversification represents an adaptation to changing EU neighbourhood policies and an ambition for global relevance beyond the European continent.

*COVID-era adaptation:* Like most organisations, ALDA shifted to online operations during COVID. This disrupted the place-based, relationship-intensive work that characterises its LDA model but also accelerated digital capacity and demonstrated the possibility of remote coordination across the network.

*Strategic adaptation gap:* Marco's analysis suggests that ALDA's strategic direction has been shaped more by funding opportunities than by deliberate adaptation. The organisation has grown and diversified but without fully resolving the structural tensions between its membership-based identity and its project-driven operations, between its European roots and its global ambitions, and between the centre's coordination role and the LDAs' autonomy.

*EPD engagement as adaptive positioning:* ALDA's participation in EPD represents an adaptive move toward network-of-networks positioning — connecting ALDA's local democracy expertise with the broader European democracy support ecosystem.

### Dimension 8: Commoning

**Presence: MARGINALLY PRESENT** (IFP1, Antonella Valmorbida)

*The LDA as a commons space:* Each Local Democracy Agency can be understood as a local commons — a space where different stakeholders (municipalities, civil society, international partners) pool resources, share governance, and co-produce outcomes. The multi-stakeholder governance model mirrors a commons logic.

*Network as shared infrastructure:* ALDA provides coordination, funding access, methodology, and legitimacy that individual LDAs could not generate independently. This shared infrastructure has commons-like characteristics — it is collectively produced and collectively drawn upon.

*The membership-citizen connection:* Antonella emphasises that individual citizens are members alongside institutions, suggesting an aspiration toward a more distributed, participatory governance model than the traditional association structure.

*Limitation — the project-funding constraint on commoning:* The dominance of project funding, with its specific deliverables and timelines, limits the extent to which the network can function as a true commons where members collectively determine priorities and resource allocation.

### Dimension 9: Impacts

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP1, Antonella Valmorbida; ITR6, Marco Boaria)

*Scope — local governance:* ALDA's most tangible impacts are at the local level in specific territories. LDAs have supported municipalities in the Western Balkans to develop participatory budgeting processes, citizen engagement mechanisms, and decentralised service delivery. These are concrete, place-based outcomes.

*Scope — European integration:* ALDA has contributed to local governance reforms in EU candidate and neighbourhood countries, supporting the decentralisation and democratisation dimensions of the European integration process.

*Institutional — the LDA model:* The LDA model itself is an institutional innovation — a locally rooted, multi-stakeholder organisation that persists beyond individual projects. Several LDAs have been operating for 15-20 years, demonstrating institutional sustainability.

*Limitation — attribution and visibility:* Project-based funding makes it difficult to attribute long-term impacts to ALDA specifically, as outcomes are shared with multiple consortium partners. Additionally, ALDA's visibility in the broader democratic innovation field is lower than might be expected given its scale and longevity — a function of its operational rather than advocacy-oriented profile.

## 3. Cross-Cutting Analytical Themes

### Theme 1: The Hybridity Dilemma

ALDA's simultaneous identity as a membership association, a project-implementing body, and a network of LDAs creates an ongoing strategic tension. Each identity pulls in a different direction: the membership identity toward democratic accountability and member-driven strategy, the project identity toward donor responsiveness and operational efficiency, and the network identity toward decentralised autonomy and peer coordination. None of these identities is false, but they are not fully integrated, creating a permanent negotiation among competing logics.

### Theme 2: EU Dependency and Strategic Autonomy

ALDA's dependence on EU project funding is both its most reliable resource and its most significant structural constraint. The project-driven growth model has delivered scale, geographic reach, and operational capacity, but at the cost of member-driven strategic direction. The organisation has grown into a shape determined by donor priorities rather than by an internally generated strategy. Diversifying funding — a priority shared across the Nets4DEM corpus — is constrained by the limited availability of non-EU institutional funding for an organisation of ALDA's profile.

### Theme 3: The LDA as Federated Governance Model

The LDA network is ALDA's most distinctive organisational innovation — and its most complex governance challenge. Each LDA is autonomous, locally governed, and operationally independent, yet networked through ALDA's coordinating structure. This federated model combines local ownership with shared infrastructure, but creates coordination costs and variable quality. The model is particularly relevant for other transnational networks seeking to combine global coordination with local rootedness.
