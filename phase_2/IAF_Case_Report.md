# IAF — International Association of Facilitators
## Analytical Case Report — Phase 2 (Nets4DEM)

> **Sources:** IFP7, IFP17 — Gerardo de Lutzenberger (ex-Chair, IAF Global) and Tamara Zivadinovic (Chair, IAF Global). Joint interview, June 2026.

---

## 1. Case Overview

**What IAF is:** The International Association of Facilitators (IAF) is a global professional body for facilitators, registered in the USA with its administrative office in Canada. It has approximately 2,000 members across all world regions and operates through regional chapters, a certification programme, and a growing set of digital peer-learning spaces. It is entirely self-funded through membership dues and accreditation fees — it does not accept donor or government funding.

**Central sources:** Gerardo de Lutzenberger (ex-Chair, served until December 2025) and Tamara Zivadinovic (current Chair, 2026–2028). Tamara previously served as Regional Director for Europe and the Middle East and led a local chapter for Central/Eastern Europe, providing a multi-level perspective. The interview was conducted jointly, with both speakers complementing each other.

**What the case is mainly about:** IAF represents a distinctive model of a self-funded, volunteer-intensive network that deliberately separates its networking function from commercial activity. It illustrates the tension between professional credentialing and community-building, the challenges of maintaining a global safe space across radically different regional contexts, the transition from an "old friends club" to a professionalised organisation, and the strategic choice of self-funding as a form of independence from donor-driven agendas.

## 2. Dimension-by-Dimension Analysis

### Dimension 1: Organisational Identity — Professional Body as Network

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP7/17, Gerardo and Tamara)

*Professional body identity:* IAF identifies primarily as a professional body, not a social movement or advocacy network. Members join for standards, accreditation, certification, and peer recognition: "Some people join the IAF because they look for standards, an accreditation, a certification, or just finding and using the standards we have set" (IFP7/17, Gerardo).

*Network as secondary identity:* The community-of-practice dimension is equally important but expressed differently: "Being in contact and in touch with people around the world, connecting, learning from each other, or even just being involved in projects together" (IFP7/17, Gerardo). The organisation's official list of member benefits places "community of practice" first.

*The "old friends club" origin:* IAF was born approximately 50 years ago from a group of friends who had been working together for about 20 years before formalising. This origin as a peer network among friends has shaped its governance culture — informal, trust-based, volunteer-led — and created the tension that the organisation now faces as it professionalises: "We are at the point where: do you remain as the old friends club, or do you want to become a more professional association?" (IFP7/17, Gerardo).

*Strategic pillar shift:* A recent strategic reorientation moved from "offering value to members" to "helping our members to shine in the profession." This subtle shift reframes the organisation from a service provider to a platform for member visibility and professional growth: "For us, even just being the chair of the IAF means that you are seen as the best facilitator in the world. This means shining" (IFP7/17, Gerardo).

*Temporal trajectory — key phases:*
1. **Founding phase (~50 years ago):** A group of friends, largely British and Dutch, creating a certification programme
2. **European semi-independence (pre-2011):** Europe operated as an independent island — separate website, separate treasurer, largely autonomous from the global body
3. **Reintegration phase (post-2011):** Europe brought back into the global structure, standardising membership management across regions
4. **Professionalisation phase (current):** Hiring of an Association Management Organisation (AMO), transition from all-volunteer board to a mixed model with paid executive staff

### Dimension 2: Membership — Global Diversity, Asymmetric Needs

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP7/17, Gerardo and Tamara)

*Global reach:* IAF has members in all world regions — Africa, North America, Latin America, Central America, Europe, Iran, Lebanon, Ukraine, China, Japan, Oceania. This geographic breadth creates radically different member needs: "People in China is really crazy for accreditation. They really want accreditation. In Africa, they even cannot access our accreditation unless we offer a bursary for most of them. So it's a very different situation" (IFP7/17, Gerardo).

*Solo-preneur profile:* A significant portion of the membership are independent consultants — "solopreneurs" — who join primarily for community and peer connection: "Very often in our practice, we are kind of solopreneurs... they seek for a community, they seek for somebody to work with, to find their people to exchange" (IFP7/17, Tamara). This makes IAF's networking function existential for members who would otherwise work in isolation.

*Membership size and scaling ambition:* Approximately 2,000 members globally. The organisation aims to scale up membership and professionalise its operations — the AMO and executive director hiring are driven partly by growth ambition.

*Barriers to access:* The accreditation programme is effectively inaccessible to African members without bursaries — an acknowledged structural asymmetry. The organisation offers bursaries but cannot fully resolve the economic gap. Language is another barrier: the certification programme is primarily in English, though Mandarin and Dutch were offered historically, with Dutch now discontinued due to declining membership in the Netherlands.

*Silent exit uncertainty:* The organisation does not yet have a systematic exit survey: "We tried a couple of times to have some very limited surveys, but we never conducted the big survey" (IFP7/17, Tamara). A regular global membership survey is being planned for future years.

### Dimension 3: Governance — Volunteer Board with Professionalising Support

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP7/17, Gerardo and Tamara)

*Governance structure:* An elected global board, currently chaired by Tamara Zivadinovic (Serbia — the first chair from outside the traditional Western European/North American core). Regional directors and chapter leads form the intermediate governance layer. Chapter leads meet monthly, exchanging information about local activities and funding.

*Rotating leadership with fast turnover:* Board members are volunteers with term limits, creating a recurring cycle of onboarding and learning loss: "We have quite a fast turnover in the board, which also implies that because you are a volunteer, you need time to start understanding what you're expected to do, understanding the mechanics of the organisation, and then you step down from your role" (IFP7/17, Gerardo). This maintains democratic openness but limits institutional memory.

*Volunteer-to-professional transition:* The most significant governance shift has been hiring an Association Management Organisation (AMO) to run the office, manage membership systems, and maintain the website. This transition was traumatic when a previous AMO quit the contract, leaving IAF with "two bank accounts in America, a bit messy and complicated" (IFP7/17, Gerardo). A new AMO is now in place and an executive director has been hired.

*Ethical procedures:* Formal complaint and appeal processes exist. Complaints are written, reviewed by the board with regional director involvement, and resolved through dialogue. The organisation has never expelled a member through the complaint process in 30 years of history. An appeal process exists for certification disputes, and some appeals have been positively resolved.

*Systemic learning from incidents:* A recent case concerning a member misrepresenting their IAF status on social media led to the introduction of digital badges as a systemic fix — an example of organisational learning from a specific incident: "We learned from this case and did some improvements that will ensure protection of our reputation" (IFP7/17, Tamara).

*Regional chapter governance:* Chapters can apply for small annual grants from the global organisation, submitting a light financial report. This creates a decentralised but transparent subsidiary structure. External project funding obtained by chapters operates through separate legal entities, not controlled by the global organisation.

### Dimension 4: Resources — Self-Funding as Independence Strategy

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP7/17, Gerardo and Tamara)

*Funding model:* IAF is entirely self-funded through three sources: membership dues, accreditation/certification fees, and (occasionally) conference revenue. The organisation deliberately does not accept external donor or government funding: "We don't get funding from other bodies, donors or other kind of people. We leave the money we raise from members — membership and mostly accreditations, plus maybe just sometimes a little bit from conferences" (IFP7/17, Gerardo).

*Reasons for self-funding:* Independence is the primary motivation. The second, equally important reason is the deliberate separation of commercial activity from the network: "We keep quite intentionally the business out of the network. If you do a business — a project funded by an external donor where someone is getting paid — how you select these people? You enter into a completely different thing" (IFP7/17, Gerardo).

*Local chapter funding:* Regional chapters can and do apply for local funding (Erasmus+, national projects), but they must form separate legal entities for this purpose. These funds are not controlled by the global organisation: "We cannot have the ownership, full ownership of these projects, and we cannot benefit directly as organisation from them. But our members can" (IFP7/17, Tamara).

*Budget allocation:* The entire budget is allocated to networking activities — the paradoxical phenomenon observed in other networks of "more funding for networking than for operating in the territories" does not apply, because IAF's core function is networking itself. As Gerardo notes: "In this sense, we use our budget entirely for networking, and we haven't really the need to do something on the territory."

*Financial risk:* The self-funding model limits growth speed. Scaling membership requires professionalising the office, which requires more funding, which creates pressure to increase membership numbers and dues. The professionalisation transition is ongoing and fragile — the earlier AMO withdrawal demonstrated dependency on a single administrative partner.

### Dimension 5: External Relations — Professional Peers, Not Advocacy Partners

**Presence: MARGINALLY PRESENT** (IFP7/17, Gerardo and Tamara)

*Peer professional bodies:* IAF maintains connections with similar organisations — the International Coaching Federation (ICF), facilitation networks in Australia, Singapore, and Korea, and the Scrum network. These relationships are primarily peer exchange, not formal partnerships: "We are definitely connected to different networks... in some cases cooperation, in some cases just exchange" (IFP7/17, Tamara).

*Limited advocacy engagement:* As a professional body, IAF does not position itself in the advocacy space. It is acknowledged by the European Commission as a professional body but does not proactively seek this relationship: "We are not proactively doing that" (IFP7/17, Tamara).

*Relationships as individual, not institutional:* IAF's external connections are often personal — board members' individual networks rather than organisational MoUs. Gerardo met the ICF executive director through a board colleague's personal contact: "Networks are made by people, not by organisations" (IFP7/17, Gerardo).

*No substantive connection to the participatory democracy field:* Despite facilitation being a core skill for participatory processes, IAF has no formal connection with networks like OIDP, People Powered, or Decidim. This gap is striking for a study focused on democratic resilience — the facilitation profession and the participatory democracy movement are structurally disconnected despite functional overlap.

*Global-local tension in external relations:* The global identity makes regional engagement complicated: "We are really a global organisation which makes quite a difference for us. Europe and the European Commission is very important in Europe, but it's not so important if you look at it from a global perspective" (IFP7/17, Gerardo).

### Dimension 6: Knowledge, Learning, and Mediation

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP7/17, Gerardo and Tamara)

*Peer learning as core offering:* IAF does not provide training itself but creates spaces for members to learn from each other: "Our organisation is not providing training per se, but we are mostly relying on the learning between members" (IFP7/17, Tamara).

*Share-and-Learn Sessions:* A structured space where members offer their methods, trials, or experiences to the community for feedback: "There is a safe space for them to try and to offer something to the community receiving feedback, which is very important as the value that this organisation is providing" (IFP7/17, Tamara).

*Special Interest Groups (SIGs):* Self-organised, topic-based groups that form around specific facilitation themes. These are the primary vehicle for cross-cultural exchange — the same topic discussed from different cultural perspectives. SIGs operate with considerable autonomy, outside formal organisational structures.

*Mentoring programme:* Cross-regional mentoring where mentees seek mentors in different IAF regions: "We encourage people to look for a mentee or a mentor in another region of the IAF. So you can work with a Chinese mentor if you are Italian" (IFP7/17, Gerardo). This deliberately pairs people from different cultural and geographic backgrounds.

*Certification and appeal processes:* The certification programme operates through a formal assessment system with an independent appeals process. This is IAF's most formalised knowledge-validation mechanism. The programme has historically been conducted in Mandarin and Dutch as well as English.

*North-South knowledge exchange:* The organisation values cultural differences in facilitation style — African facilitators using music and song, Latin American facilitators connecting with indigenous communities, Western European facilitators focusing on business environments. The mentoring programme and cross-regional committee composition are explicit mechanisms to leverage these differences, though the approach is described as "creating spaces and inviting exchange" rather than formal affirmative action.

*Marketplace:* A dedicated space where external requests for facilitation services are posted. IAF does not directly engage in business transactions but provides the infrastructure for members to find work opportunities.

### Dimension 7: Adaptation — From Friends' Club to Professional Association

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP7/17, Gerardo and Tamara)

*COVID as accelerator:* IAF was already working online before the pandemic, but COVID dramatically increased the volume of online sessions: "After COVID, we really started to work even more online. The number of sessions we offer online has increased" (IFP7/17, Gerardo).

*Professionalisation challenge:* The most significant adaptation is the ongoing transition from an all-volunteer organisation to one with professional staff: "At some point we realised that we couldn't rely anymore on our volunteer work as a board to run the organisation" (IFP7/17, Gerardo). The AMO and executive director are responses to this recognition.

*Time-zone constraint:* As a truly global organisation, IAF finds time zones now more constraining than geography: "One of the challenges we always have is how to connect people from Japan and Vancouver in Canada, which is completely on the opposite side" (IFP7/17, Gerardo). A planned website feature for asynchronous member connection has been discussed with a supplier but not yet implemented.

*Demographic diversification of leadership:* The board has diversified significantly. Gerardo was the first Italian chair; Tamara is the first chair from Serbia. The current Director of Professional Development is Chinese — a significant shift from the early decades when such positions "could have been only a WASP or a Brit" (IFP7/17, Gerardo). The rapid turnover of board positions accelerates this diversification.

*AI as emerging challenge:* AI is recognised as transformative for every phase of facilitation — design, delivery, and reporting. The organisation's office likely uses AI extensively, but the board has not yet formally addressed it: "AI is not still on the table of the board and especially in the relationship with the office, because it's quite a sensitive topic. We don't want to go there" (IFP7/17, Gerardo). Members are nevertheless organising around the topic independently through SIGs and informal discussions, seeking both answers and community in the face of professional disruption.

### Dimension 8: Commoning

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP7/17, Gerardo and Tamara)

*Networking as the common good:* IAF's entire value proposition is the network itself. Members join not for services delivered to them but for access to a community. The "give-to-get" dynamic is explicit: "What we have experienced is that you get a benefit once you start doing something for the association. When you offer something to the others, you get something back" (IFP7/17, Gerardo).

*Volunteer-driven commons:* The organisation runs primarily on voluntary contribution. Board members, chapter leads, committee members, and SIG conveners all contribute their time and expertise. This creates a shared ownership dynamic — the network is literally what members make it.

*Safe space as relational practice:* The commitment to keeping "business out of the network" is a deliberate commoning practice — protecting the relational space from market dynamics. The designated Marketplace provides a bounded commercial space with transparent rules, while the core network remains a non-commercial commons. As Tamara explains, IAF does not control or intervene in business transactions that happen between members; it simply provides the infrastructure.

*The "shining" pillar as mutual elevation:* The strategic shift to "helping members shine in the profession" reframes the organisation as a platform for collective visibility rather than a top-down value provider: "When you do something for the association — you chair a committee, you are a chapter lead — you get visibility. And this visibility is in our network, but also with clients" (IFP7/17, Gerardo).

*Commoning constraint — the self-funding ceiling:* The model creates a tension: the network is a commons sustained by member contributions, but those contributions are regressive — the wealthiest members and regions subsidise the poorest. The self-funding model protects independence but limits the common's capacity to expand equitably.

### Dimension 9: Impacts

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP7/17, Gerardo and Tamara)

*Scope — individual level:* The most tangible impact is on individual facilitators who find community, professional recognition, work opportunities, and personal growth through IAF membership. The mentoring programme, certification, and cross-regional volunteering create lasting professional relationships and career development.

*Scope — sector level:* IAF has established facilitation as a recognised profession with standards, certification, and ethical frameworks. Its certification programme sets a global standard for facilitation competence. However, its impact on the broader democratic participation ecosystem is limited by the lack of formal connections with participatory democracy networks.

*Institutional/policy:* IAF is acknowledged by the European Commission as a professional body — a modest institutional recognition. Beyond this, the organisation's policy impact is negligible by design — it positions itself as a professional body, not an advocacy organisation.

*Cognitive/discursive:* IAF has contributed to the professionalisation of facilitation discourse — shifting it from an informal practice to a recognised discipline with standards, ethics, and career pathways. However, the organisation has not engaged substantively with the discourse on democratic innovation or participatory governance.

*Unexpected result — the digital badge innovation:* A member's misuse of IAF status on social media led not just to a single-case resolution but to the systemic introduction of digital badges tied to real-time membership status — an example of organisational learning from a specific incident that produced a portable innovation.

## 3. Cross-Cutting Analytical Themes

### Theme 1: Self-Funding as Independence — The Price of Autonomy

IAF's refusal to accept external funding is the single most distinctive feature of its organisational model. The decision protects the network from donor-driven agenda distortion and preserves the peer-to-peer character of the community. But it also limits growth, professionalisation speed, and the organisation's ability to subsidise membership for Global South practitioners. The self-funding model functions well for a professional body whose members derive direct economic value from certification and networking, but it creates a structural barrier to geographic equity: African members cannot access accreditation without bursaries, and those bursaries must be funded from the membership-dues pool.

### Theme 2: The Safe Space as Deliberate Architecture

IAF has developed a layered architecture of spaces — the safe zone of Share-and-Learn sessions (where members can present unfinished work for feedback), the Marketplace (where commercial transactions happen transparently), Special Interest Groups (topic-based self-organisation), and the ethical complaints process (where conflicts are resolved through dialogue). This explicit zoning of different types of interaction — peer learning, business, community organising, dispute resolution — is more sophisticated than many networks that leave these dynamics to informal negotiation or conflate them in a single space.

### Theme 3: The Transition from Volunteer to Professional — Generational Governance Challenge

IAF's trajectory from an "old friends club" to a professional association mirrors the challenge faced by many membership-based networks. The volunteer model that sustained the organisation for decades has reached its limit — board members cycle through too quickly to accumulate institutional memory, and the administrative load exceeds what volunteers can carry. The AMO and executive director represent necessary professionalisation, but introduces a new tension: paid staff now handle functions that volunteers previously owned, shifting the organisation's centre of gravity from distributed volunteer ownership to centralised professional management.

### Theme 4: Regional Diversity Without Formal Affirmative Action

IAF manages its regional diversity through what Tamara calls "creating spaces and inviting exchange" rather than through formal quotas or affirmative action mechanisms. Cross-regional mentoring, geographically balanced committees, and the encouragement of inter-regional volunteer work all promote exchange without imposing top-down diversity requirements. The result is organic but uneven — the organisation acknowledges that it does not systematically track whether its diversity mechanisms are working, and the "Southern" facilitation tradition (community-based, indigenous-connected) and the "Northern" tradition (business-focused) coexist without formal integration.
