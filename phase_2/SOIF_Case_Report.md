# SOIF / SUAF — School of International Futures
## Analytical Case Report — Phase 2 (Nets4DEM)

> **Sources:** IFP3 — Cat Tully (Founder, SOIF) and IFP12 — Maria Lorente Perez (Spanish Hub contact, SOIF/SUAF). Two interviews, June 2026.

---

## 1. Case Overview

**What SOIF is:** The School of International Futures (SOIF, also referred to as SUAF in its French iteration) is a UK-based networked organisation approaching its 15th year. It operates as a "network of networks" — approximately 20 networks encompassing around 5,000 people across 60+ countries — united around the mission of bringing futures thinking and long-term governance into policymaking, civil society, and democratic practice. SOIF is not a traditional foresight consultancy; it works from the demand side, positioning itself as a translation bridge between futures practice and policy action. Its founder and strategic lead is Cat Tully, a former member of the UK Prime Minister's Strategy Unit.

**Central sources:** Cat Tully (IFP3) provides the strategic, organisational, and historical overview of SOIF. Maria Lorente Perez (IFP12) provides a granular, on-the-ground account of the Spanish Hub — a country-level network being incubated with SOIF's support — offering a complementary perspective on how SOIF's hub strategy operates in practice.

**What the case is mainly about:** SOIF represents a distinctive organisational model that sits between consultancy and movement-building. It generates surplus through advisory services and reinvests it into network infrastructure, community-building, and advocacy. It illustrates the strategic use of "network weaving" as a deliberate practice, the tension between founder-driven governance and democratic distribution, the challenge of centering the Global South from a UK-based organisation, and the deliberate cultivation of country-level hubs as semi-autonomous partners. SOIF also exemplifies how an organisation can deliberately navigate its own succession — Cat Tully is on a six-year exit journey — forcing structural questions about governance, business model, and the distribution of power.

## 2. Dimension-by-Dimension Analysis

### Dimension 1: Organisational Identity — From Consultancy to Network of Networks

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP3, Cat Tully; IFP12, Maria Lorente)

*Three-phase evolution:* SOIF's identity has evolved through three distinct phases. **Phase 1 (first 5 years):** "How do you actually institutionalise futures in a way that means it continues to be transformative?" Tully explains that the fundamental contradiction is "bureaucratising and institutionalising something that is actually challenging power." **Phase 2 (next 5 years):** Building networks at scale — the Next Generation Foresight Practitioners (NGFP) network, the Long-Term Governance Network. A community emerged around SOIF of practitioners who "also don't care about whether we call it Futures or Foresight. What we care about is transforming our community." **Phase 3 (last 3-4 years):** Transition to "field and movement-building organisation." SOIF now explicitly defines itself as a "network of networks."

*Not a foresight supplier:* SOIF's identity is distinct from the foresight field. "We're not really a foresight supplier. We've always been on the demand side. So working with policymakers, social movements." This positions SOIF as a translator — bridging the language of futures into the language of governance.

*Unit of analysis — people, not ideas:* "Ideas have never changed the world, it's people using ideas that change the world. So our unit of analysis is how people use ideas about the future to change the world." This people-centred orientation makes networks a structural necessity, not a strategic choice.

*The hub-network distinction:* The Spanish Hub deliberately avoids calling itself a "network." Maria explains: "When we speak of network, people seem to understand something that is much more established already, with a clear structure. We were not yet at that stage. Retaining the word 'hub' was, at this moment, freer and more open. It's a place where action can happen... where you can come in and explore." The hub is "more of a convergent place — not just a meeting place. It's not like a very clear forum network that is already having this structure."

### Dimension 2: Membership — Networks Within Networks, Centering the Global South

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP3, Cat Tully; IFP12, Maria Lorente)

*Scale:* Approximately 5,000 people across 20 networks, including the Long-Term Governance Network (700 people in 60 countries), the NGFP (with ~400 in Africa alone), country hubs (~15-16 countries), and specialised hubs (Police and Security Hub, Futures Methods from Around the World Hub, Rural Hub).

*Pioneers and practitioners:* The Long-Term Governance Network includes a "Pioneers Group" of senior politicians and senior civil servants — a deliberate tiered membership that recognises different levels of engagement and influence.

*Spanish Hub composition:* 17 organisations and 31 individuals. The composition emerged organically from existing connections: "It was created more on who do we know that might be doing something around this" (IFP12, Maria). Maria acknowledges the hub "is not a fractal of society" — diversity, especially around gender and regional representation, has not yet been systematically addressed: "We haven't thought about it yet. We don't have any feminist organisation."

*Global South centering:* Tully is explicit: "If you're talking about future generations and the future of the 21st century, you cannot have that without centring the Global South and in particular Africa, where most of the young people and future generations are going to be living." SOIF has invested in building networks with universities and communities across Asia, Latin America, and Africa. However, Tully candidly notes: "Our SMT, we've got four people and we're all middle-aged white Brits. That's a big problem."

*Individual-institution dynamic:* The Spanish Hub operates "31 individuals of 17 organisations — so sometimes you have more individuals per organisation." This suggests organisations are not yet fully institutionally committed; they have internal champions participating who may or may not carry their organisations with them. Maria emphasises building relationships before institutional commitments: "We are trying to meet or to establish the relationships as human beings and individuals before we establish relationships with organisations."

*Language barriers:* English is the working language, but SOIF is developing the "Futures Methods from Around the World Hub" where "young foresight practitioners from the Global South say, these are how we're innovating practices and approaches that suit the community and the mythology and the language of how we operate."

### Dimension 3: Governance — Founder-Driven with Deliberate Transition

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP3, Cat Tully)

*No traditional board:* SOIF operates without a formal governance board — an explicit choice that Tully acknowledges as "quite a non-democratic element to the way that we operate." The centripetal force required to hold a virtual organisation together and maintain value alignment against financial incentives "requires a really powerful kind of commitment." An advisory board composed of NGFP representatives serves as the primary governance mechanism, supplemented by bilateral advice from senior pioneers.

*Six-year exit journey:* Tully is deliberately engineering her own succession: "I'm stepping back. I'm on a six-year exit journey and I'm halfway through it." This process is framed as reorienting the organisation toward being "in service" to the wider community: "What can we do to be in service to you, driving change in your countries, wherever you are?" Possible future forms under discussion: cooperative, social franchise, federation, DAO.

*Hub governance varies by context:* In Finland, "three natural leaders — SITRA (government), Demos (think tank), FINGO (grassroots) — have taken the lead and are bringing the coalition together." In Portugal and Spain, "it's been really difficult to get anybody to lean into that role because people really struggle with the value of network weaving." SOIF has had to fund a network weaver role in some contexts.

*Spanish Hub governance — the working group model:* Governance emerged through a self-selecting working group of six (from the original 17 organisations), created at the first collective meeting: "Whoever wants belongs to the smaller working group. The working group will craft proposals, bring them back to the hub for enrichment and vetting, and once approved, put them in action" (IFP12, Maria). The working group operates dialogically with sociocratic consent: "We always bring the offer of agreeing by consent. If you can live with it, even if you don't like it 100%, that's great. But if you have an objection, you need to say what the objection is and propose a way forward." Maria, a professional facilitator, provides the facilitative coordination.

*Rotation and legitimacy:* The hub is currently grappling with legitimacy: "We're not a legal entity. But when we want to be visible, we're going to have to have some kind of legitimacy. And in order to build that legitimacy, we need to be hyper-transparent in everything" (IFP12, Maria).

### Dimension 4: Resources — Surplus Reinvestment and the Founder's Rolodex

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP3, Cat Tully; IFP12, Maria Lorente)

*Business model:* SOIF's advisory and services function generates surplus that is reinvested into network infrastructure, community-building, and advocacy. Tully describes this as "10-year bets that we've been investing in over time." The organisation has a ~£2.5 million turnover with 40 people, all contractors with the same terms regardless of role or location.

*Founder dependency:* The model depends heavily on Tully's personal access and relationships: "My Rolodex and my access basically makes this business model work. If we move away from that, we need a re-envisioning of what that business model looks like." This dependency is the driver of the exit journey.

*Spanish Hub funding — SOIF as enabler:* SOIF provided the Spanish Hub with: Sophie Howe's physical presence for inspiration, small financial support for events (room hire, translation, invitations), a dedicated staff person for four months to help structure and shape the hub, a shared Google Drive hosted by SOIF, and web hosting. Maria: "They have given us a shared drive, hosted in SWAF. They decided they will finance the web and we will still have the funds available. We still have that pot of financing."

*Pragmatic non-formalisation:* The Spanish Hub channels funding through existing member organisations rather than incorporating: "We've said, these are the two organisations that will be willing to channel the funding. One was decided. We remain doing the worst — we've decided no more of the legal part." Maria closed a previous organisation due to Spanish bureaucratic burden and is reluctant to repeat the experience. The ambition, however, is "distributed leadership. The one who knows best, leads. Period. That seems to be very difficult with human beings."

*DXF merger as resource transfer:* The Foundation for Democracy and Sustainable Development (DXF) — a board-led initiative without staff that had pioneered the democracy-sustainable development nexus — agreed to transfer its remaining £35,000 in reserves to SOIF upon dissolution. This funds two fellowships per year for five years, with former board members becoming mentors. Tully calls it "a beautiful intergenerational story."

### Dimension 5: External Relations — From the UN to Country Hubs

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP3, Cat Tully)

*Multilateral engagement:* SOIF supported a UN dialogue between Jamaica and the Netherlands and is activating Kenya to lead on implementing the Declaration on Future Generations. A "Friends of Future Generations" group is being organised.

*European Union:* Tully references the European citizens panel on intergenerational fairness as "such a brilliant experience." She credits citizens with intuitively grasping intergenerational fairness more quickly than policymakers and academics.

*Country hub ecosystem:* About 15-16 country hubs exist at varying levels of maturity. Finland is "really easy to engage" with self-organising leadership. Spain and Portugal need more structured support. Italy is "not an official hub."

*Bipartisan positioning as condition for engagement:* The Spanish Hub explicitly frames its work as bipartisan: "This movement needs to be totally bipartisan. It cannot be hijacked by any ideology. Everyone needs to feel they can do what they can do with their approach — it's legitimate" (IFP12, Maria). Tully reinforces this: "It cannot be politicised. You need to engage with all parties. Otherwise, this whole agenda becomes co-opted by the left." This creates a strategic tension — as Maria notes, "just by saying you're working to protect future generations, you're already placed in the progressive part." The hub must work with "targeted relationship building" to reach conservative actors.

*Relationship with existing institutions:* SOIF advised the Spanish government's youth ministry and advocates for impact evaluation of public policy. Tully is notably critical of how citizens' assemblies are currently designed, arguing that "current power dynamics are fungible into citizens' assemblies unless you do proper power brokering" — a position that positions SOIF as a critical friend to the deliberative democracy field.

### Dimension 6: Knowledge — Codifying the Invisible

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP3, Cat Tully)

*The POL Prism framework:* SOIF's core analytical framework has three components: (1) technical capacity — the "Long-Term Governance Prism" that helps institutions embed foresight, (2) intergenerational dialogue — bringing older and younger generations together around shared futures, and (3) citizens who see around the corner — the recognition that "it's people on the periphery who are much better at understanding how the new paradigm is going to look."

*Not "best practice" but "good practice":* Tully explicitly rejects best-practice language: "There is no right or wrong. It's just sharing experiences and often around questions." This epistemic modesty is operationally embedded — the NGFP award criteria include "potential of the ideas as well as track record."

*Codifying invisible work:* Tully emphasises that the most important futures work happens invisibly — "a lot of the work about this stuff is invisible, and it's not codified, because the people who are doing it are not funded, not recognized, putting everything into the creation of change." SOIF is now writing this up, and Tully's interview itself is happening during an active codification period.

*The Spanish Hub's observatory concept:* One of three agreed strategic pillars is "an observatory where we can bring evidence. It won't be our evidence. We will need to take it from different places. And we will make sense of it. But we are beginning to structure that space literally" (IFP12, Maria). The observatory is intended to serve a translation function — "making evidence accessible, understandable" for civil society and policymakers. Maria: "If it will be done by somebody else with more resources, let them do it. Just make sure it is available. Sometimes things for CSOs are not accessible or understandable. We can play a role there. That's the sense-making."

*Futures Methods from Around the World Hub:* A deliberate knowledge-diversification initiative where practitioners from the Global South articulate methods rooted in their own communities, mythologies, and languages — an explicit challenge to the Western, English-language dominance of futures methodology.

### Dimension 7: Adaptation — The Award-to-Fellowship Evolution

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP3, Cat Tully)

*The NGFP trajectory — award to fellowship:* The Next Generation Foresight Practitioners Award evolved through iterative adaptation driven by community feedback. It began with a $25,000 prize to "cut through" low awareness. Over time, it became a fellowship programme with $10,000 awarded at the end of a year-long process. Regional awards were introduced to target underrepresented continents. A "Young Voices Award" partnered with Teach the Future for 12-17 year olds.

*Judging as collaborative practice:* SOIF moved judging from individual assessment to collective deliberation: "It's the collaboration and the discussion between judges that is both insightful and motivating for the judges, but also powerful for the applicants." The interviewer suggests opening this to wider public readership — an idea Tully finds "really interesting."

*The exit transition as organisational adaptation:* The six-year exit journey is SOIF's most significant adaptation. It forces the organisation to articulate what of its value is structural (the frameworks, the networks, the brand) versus what is personal (Tully's access, Rolodex, and relentless work ethic: "I work 80-hour weeks and I get paid the least of the organisation.").

*COVID as external catalyst — limited internal impact:* SOIF was virtual from the beginning: "I work full-time with colleagues since 2012. I've met them in person three times." COVID's primary impact was external — it shifted the demand for futures work as institutions realised the limits of short-term planning. Internally, SOIF was already adapted to the disruption COVID created for others.

### Dimension 8: Commoning

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP3, Cat Tully; IFP12, Maria Lorente)

*Network weaving as commoning practice:* SOIF explicitly uses the framework of "network weaving" — the "five Cs" (common purpose, community mapping, cultivating trust, coordination mechanisms, collaboration mechanisms) — as a deliberate practice for building shared infrastructure. The system steward role "holds those things on behalf of the whole community."

*Surplus reinvestment as commoning:* The reinvestment of advisory services surplus into network infrastructure is a commons-building mechanism — wealth generated at the centre is redistributed to the periphery through hubs, fellowships, and convenings.

*Relationships before institutions:* The Spanish Hub's emphasis on building relationships before formal commitments — "we're trying to meet as human beings and individuals before we establish relationships with organisations" — enacts a commoning logic. Trust is the substrate on which the collective work is built.

*Tension in commoning — the gatekeeping problem:* The hub's current composition emerged from personal networks: "It was created more on who do we know." This creates a practical commoning challenge — the network is accessible to those who are already known, creating a structural barrier to those outside existing social networks. Maria recognises this: "Going forward, it won't be for the working group to decide which level of diversity. Our recommendation will be: the more diverse, the better."

### Dimension 9: Impacts

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP3, Cat Tully; IFP12, Maria Lorente)

*Scope — field-level:* SOIF has contributed to making futures thinking and long-term governance a recognised field of practice. The NGFP community has created a global cohort of futures practitioners who did not previously identify with the label. The Long-Term Governance Network connects practitioners across 60 countries.

*Scope — policy:* Spain's youth minister was engaged through the hub's advocacy; the Welsh Commissioner for Future Generations (Sophie Howe) served as an inspirational model replicated through SOIF's convening power. The UN Declaration on Future Generations implementation is being actively supported through Kenya.

*Institutional — the prism framework:* SOIF's POL prism has been adopted as a structuring framework in several country hubs. It provides a common language for diverse actors working on long-term governance.

*Societal — reimagining participation:* Tully positions futures work as essential to democracy renewal: "The reimagined form of democracy has to have long-term intergenerational anticipatory features built into its institutions. Otherwise, it's a suboptimal equilibrium that's going to collapse into authoritarianism."

*DXF legacy as institutional impact:* The DXF merger demonstrates how a small, board-led initiative can pass its legacy to a larger organisation, seeding fellowship programmes that continue its mission — a model of deliberate institutional succession that may be relevant to other networks reaching end-of-life.

*Limitation:* Most impacts are early-stage. The country hubs are nascent, the exit transition is incomplete, and the codified frameworks are still being documented. SOIF's ambition — "to disseminate futures so all communities around the world can use the power of playing with time" — is a generational mission still in its early phases.

## 3. Cross-Cutting Analytical Themes

### Theme 1: The Founder's Paradox — Centripetal Force and Democratic Distribution

SOIF's success depends on a central contradiction: the founder's vision, Rolodex, and relentless work ethic have built a "network of networks" with global reach, but the organisation's next phase requires distributing that power in ways that may not be compatible with the current business model. Tully's six-year exit journey is an explicit attempt to resolve this paradox — but the path (cooperative? social franchise? DAO?) remains uncertain. The Spanish Hub's struggle to identify distributed leadership ("the one who knows best leads — that seems very difficult with human beings") reflects the same tension at a smaller scale.

### Theme 2: The Hub as Aspirational Form — Between Network and Event

The Spanish Hub is a deliberate non-network — or, more precisely, a pre-network — that uses the language of "hub" to signal openness, freedom, and convergence without the structural expectations that "network" carries. This naming choice is not semantic; it shapes how members understand their obligations, how the hub presents itself to potential members, and how it navigates the path toward (or away from) formalisation. The hub sits at an interesting organisational threshold: developed enough to have impact (meeting with a minister, crafting a manifesto) but nascent enough that core governance questions (legal form, legitimacy, diversity) remain unresolved.

### Theme 3: Bipartisanship as Strategic Necessity and Structural Constraint

SOIF's commitment to non-partisan engagement is both a strategic necessity (the futures agenda loses its power if captured by one political side) and a structural constraint (the very language of "future generations" and "sustainability" already positions the work in the progressive field). The Spanish Hub navigates this through "targeted relationship building" — finding the common ground that unites without alienating. But the tension is real: as democratic backsliding accelerates, the space between "bipartisan" and "complicit" may narrow.
