# EPD — European Partnership for Democracy
## Analytical Case Report — Phase 2 (Nets4DEM)

> **Sources:** IFP1 — Antonella Valmorbida (ALDA, EPD member), IFP20 — Thijs Berman (EPD Executive Director), ITR2 — Anu Juvonen (Demo Finland, EPD member), ITR6 — Marco Boaria (ALDA, EPD Board member). Four interviews, June 2026.

## 1. Case Overview

**What EPD is:** The European Partnership for Democracy (EPD) is a Brussels-based network of democracy support organisations, founded to coordinate and strengthen European civil society's role in supporting democracy worldwide. It operates at the intersection of the European Union institutions, its member organisations, and the broader democracy support ecosystem. EPD advocates for EU democracy support policy, facilitates joint programming among members, and serves as a collective voice for European democracy assistance.

**Central sources:** Thijs Berman (IFP20), EPD's Executive Director, provides the strategic overview. Antonella Valmorbida (IFP1) and Marco Boaria (ITR6) provide complementary perspectives from ALDA, one of EPD's larger members. Anu Juvonen (ITR2) provides the perspective of a smaller, nationally-focused member (Demo Finland).

**What the case is mainly about:** EPD represents a network of organisations that each deliver democracy support in different ways — from ALDA's local governance work to Demo Finland's political party support — united through a Brussels-based coordination body. Its challenges centre on the tension between serving as a collective advocacy voice and delivering joint programming, the asymmetric relationship between larger and smaller member organisations, and the dependence on the EU institutional funding ecosystem.

## 2. Dimension-by-Dimension Analysis

### Dimension 1: Organisational Identity — Between Network and Implementing Body

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP20, Thijs Berman; IFP1, Antonella Valmorbida; ITR6, Marco Boaria)

*Coordination and advocacy mandate:* EPD was created to give European democracy support organisations a collective voice in Brussels and to facilitate joint action. Thijs Berman describes EPD's role as bridging the gap between the EU's democracy support ambitions and the operational capacity of its member organisations.

*The member-implementer tension:* EPD has evolved from a pure coordination network toward a more operational role — implementing EU-funded democracy support programmes through consortia of member organisations. This creates tension: when EPD competes for and manages EU funding, it can be perceived as competing with its own members rather than coordinating them.

*Complementary but not overlapping:* Member organisations maintain distinct identities and operational niches. ALDA focuses on local governance and citizen participation; Demo Finland on political party capacity-building; NIMD on multiparty dialogue. EPD's value proposition is that these complementary specialisations can be combined into integrated democracy support programmes.

### Dimension 2: Membership — Asymmetric Capacity, Shared Brussels Presence

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP1, Antonella Valmorbida; ITR2, Anu Juvonen; ITR6, Marco Boaria)

*Size asymmetry:* Members range from large, multi-country organisations (ALDA, NIMD) to small, nationally-focused ones (Demo Finland). This creates structural asymmetry in members' capacity to engage with EPD — larger members can dedicate staff to Brussels advocacy and consortium-building, while smaller members struggle to participate beyond the formal governance calendar.

*The Brussels advantage:* For smaller members like Demo Finland, EPD provides access to the Brussels policy environment that they could not access independently. Anu Juvonen describes EPD as a window into EU democracy support policy and funding that a small, Helsinki-based organisation would otherwise lack.

*Marco Boaria's Board perspective:* As an EPD Board member from ALDA, Marco provides insight into the internal dynamics. He sees EPD's value in its convening power and policy influence, but acknowledges the tension between members' individual funding needs and EPD's collective advocacy role.

*Antonella's dual identity:* As ALDA's Secretary General and an EPD member, Antonella navigates between ALDA's operational identity (a project-implementing organisation) and EPD's coordinating identity (a network advocate). The relationship is mutually reinforcing — ALDA benefits from EPD's Brussels access, EPD benefits from ALDA's operational experience on the ground.

### Dimension 3: Governance — Board-Governed with Member Participation

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (ITR6, Marco Boaria; IFP20, Thijs Berman)

*Board governance:* EPD is governed by a Board composed of member organisation representatives. Marco Boaria serves on this Board, providing ALDA's perspective in strategic decisions.

*The Brussels centrality:* EPD's governance is centred in Brussels, where most strategic discussions and EU institutional engagement occur. This geographic centrality advantages members with a Brussels presence or the capacity to travel regularly.

*Democratic coherence:* The Board structure provides formal democratic governance, but the asymmetric capacity of members to engage means that larger, Brussels-based members exercise disproportionate influence on EPD's direction. This mirrors the governance challenge observed across the Nets4DEM corpus — formal equality in governance structures does not guarantee substantive equality in influence.

### Dimension 4: Resources — EU Funding as Enabler and Constraint

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP20, Thijs Berman; ITR2, Anu Juvonen; ITR6, Marco Boaria)

*EU institutional funding:* EPD's primary funding source is the European Union, through framework contracts and programme grants. This positions EPD at the centre of the EU democracy support funding ecosystem — simultaneously dependent on it and able to shape it.

*The consortium model:* EPD manages funding through consortia — bringing together member organisations to deliver integrated programmes. This creates opportunities for members to access funding they could not secure independently, but it also creates dependency: members need EPD's consortium leadership to access certain funding streams.

*The small member perspective:* Anu Juvonen describes how Demo Finland benefits from EPD's funding access while maintaining operational independence in its own bilateral programmes. EPD's consortium model provides supplementary resources without replacing Demo Finland's core funding.

*The project-funding trap — Marco's analysis:* Marco observes that EU project funding shapes the agendas of all organisations in the EPD ecosystem. The priorities of EU programmes become the priorities of the network, whether or not they match member organisations' strategic priorities. This donor-driven agenda-setting is a structural feature of the model, not a temporary condition.

### Dimension 5: External Relations — The EU Interface as Primary Function

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP20, Thijs Berman; IFP1, Antonella Valmorbida)

*EU institutions as primary interlocutor:* EPD's external relations are focused overwhelmingly on the European Union — the European Commission, the European External Action Service, the European Parliament, and member state development agencies. This concentration is both a strength (deep expertise in a specific institutional environment) and a limitation (vulnerability to shifts in EU policy priorities).

*Relationship with the broader democracy ecosystem:* EPD connects with the wider European democracy support infrastructure — the European Endowment for Democracy, the International Institute for Democracy and Electoral Assistance, and national democracy foundations. These relationships are collaborative rather than competitive, given the complementary mandates.

*Advocacy influence:* Thijs Berman emphasises EPD's role in shaping EU democracy support policy — advocating for sustained funding, methodological innovation, and a holistic approach that connects democracy support with human rights, governance, and civil society strengthening. This advocacy function is the primary benefit that members identify, particularly those who cannot do Brussels advocacy independently.

### Dimension 6: Knowledge — Aggregating Practice, Not Producing Research

**Presence: MARGINALLY PRESENT** (IFP20, Thijs Berman)

*Practice aggregation:* EPD's primary knowledge function is aggregating the practical experience of its member organisations — bringing together lessons from local governance work (ALDA), political party support (Demo Finland, NIMD), media development, and electoral assistance — into integrated programme designs and policy positions.

*Limitation — knowledge as operational input:* EPD does not have a dedicated research function. Knowledge production serves its advocacy and programming needs rather than constituting a standalone output. This distinguishes EPD from research-oriented organisations in the corpus like SOIF or KNOCA.

### Dimension 7: Adaptation — Navigating EU Policy Shifts

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP20, Thijs Berman)

*EU policy cycles as external driver:* EPD's strategic direction is heavily influenced by EU policy cycles — the Multi-Annual Financial Framework, the thematic programmes, and the political priorities of each Commission. This creates a reactive adaptation pattern where EPD adjusts to EU priorities rather than shaping them proactively.

*Democracy support in a closing space:* The global democratic backsliding and the shrinking of civic space create both challenges and opportunities for EPD. The challenge: democracy support becomes politically contested. The opportunity: the EU's stated commitment to democracy as a foreign policy priority creates openings for EPD's advocacy.

*The ALDA-EPD synergy as adaptation:* The relationship between ALDA and EPD represents an adaptive pattern where a larger operational member and a coordinating network reinforce each other. ALDA provides on-the-ground credibility and programme delivery capacity; EPD provides Brussels access and policy influence. Neither could achieve alone what they achieve together.

### Dimension 8: Commoning — Insufficiently Addressed

**Presence: MARGINALLY PRESENT** (IFP20, Thijs Berman)

*Coordination as shared infrastructure:* EPD provides shared infrastructure — Brussels presence, policy intelligence, consortium management — that individual members draw upon. This functions as a commons of coordination, where members contribute and benefit according to their capacity.

*Limitation — the competitive tension:* When EPD competes for EU funding and manages consortia, it can be perceived as a competitor rather than a coordinator. This tension between commoning (shared infrastructure) and competition (competing for the same funding pool) is unresolved in the EPD model and in the broader EU democracy support ecosystem.

### Dimension 9: Impacts

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP20, Thijs Berman)

*Policy influence:* EPD's most significant impact is at the policy level — shaping EU democracy support programmes, advocating for sustained funding, and positioning democracy support within the broader EU external action framework.

*Programmatic — consortium delivery:* Through consortium-managed programmes, EPD enables member organisations to deliver integrated democracy support that no single organisation could deliver alone.

*Limitation — attribution:* EPD's impacts are difficult to attribute, as they are mediated through member organisations' work and through the EU policy processes that EPD influences but does not control.

## 3. Cross-Cutting Analytical Themes

### Theme 1: The Coordinator-Competitor Tension

EPD's dual identity as a coordinating network and a programme-implementing body creates an unresolved tension. When EPD competes for and manages EU funding, it enters the same space as its member organisations. This tension is structural — it arises from a funding ecosystem where both coordination bodies and implementing organisations compete for the same limited resources.

### Theme 2: Asymmetric Membership in Practice

Despite formal equality in governance, EPD's membership is deeply asymmetric. Larger organisations with Brussels presence and dedicated EU policy staff exercise disproportionate influence. Smaller organisations benefit from EPD's access but cannot shape its direction to the same degree. This asymmetry mirrors patterns observed in People Powered, OIDP, and other networks in the corpus.
