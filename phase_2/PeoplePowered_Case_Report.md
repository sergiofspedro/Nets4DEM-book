## 1. Case Overview

**What People Powered is:** People Powered is a global hub for participatory democracy, founded as the Participatory Budgeting (PB) Hub before expanding its mandate to encompass the full spectrum of participatory and deliberative democracy. It is headquartered in the United States with a recently established European legal entity. It operates through a distinctive member-driven governance model — including participatory budgeting for allocating internal resources — and serves as a convener, capacity-builder, advocate, and funding intermediary for over 300 member organisations and individuals worldwide.

**Central sources:** The People Powered case draws from the richest set of interviews in the Nets4DEM corpus. Josh Lerner (IFP10), the founder and Executive Director, provides the strategic and historical overview. Four Board members — Diana Dajer (IPO7), Carla Bezerra (IPO4), Tarson Nunez (IPO16), and Andrés Falck (IPO1) — offer governance-level perspectives from Latin America. Clara and Nikhil (IFP5, ITR4) contribute staff-level insights. Stefano Stortone (IPO15) and Charlie Ngounou (IPO6) provide critical member perspectives.

**What the case is mainly about:** People Powered is the most polarising organization in the corpus — deeply loved by its core members for its human-centred culture and participatory governance, and sharply criticised by some former members for the gap between its democratic aspirations and the structural power asymmetries embedded in its American-influenced funding and governance model. It represents an ambitious experiment in running a global network according to participatory principles, and the tensions that experiment generates — between democratic governance and organisational efficiency, between American funding traditions and Global South realities, and between member-driven programming and donor-driven imperatives.

## 2. Dimension-by-Dimension Analysis

### Dimension 1: Organisational Identity — From PB Hub to Global Participatory Democracy Platform

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP10, Josh Lerner; IPO4, Carla Bezerra; IPO7, Diana Dajer)

*Origin in the PB Hub:* People Powered was born as the Participatory Budgeting Hub, bringing together researchers and practitioners around PB. Diana Dajer entered through the research board — a space where scholars she had been reading could meet face-to-face. The shift from PB Hub to People Powered was "a great idea" (IPO7, Dajer), expanding the mandate from a single methodology to the broader participatory democracy field.

*"Hub" not "network":* Like SOIF's country hubs and the Spanish Hub, People Powered uses "hub" to signal convergence and openness rather than the structural expectations of "network." Josh Lerner describes People Powered as intentionally different from OIDP ("they already have a network of governments, we want to strengthen civil society").

*Non-partisan positioning:* People Powered deliberately avoids partisan affiliation: "They are really non-partisan" (IPO4, Bezerra). This distinguishes it from politically-defined organisations like Decidim. However, Carla Bezerra critiques that "not having a clear position also may limit political action" — the broad tent creates limitations when members want clear leadership on urgent issues.

*American DNA:* The organisation's funding model, governance culture, and operational style reflect its American origins. Stefano Stortone critically describes "something that I felt was very American — people created projects that became common goods, and then someone approved them because they had the money." This American DNA creates cultural friction with members from other funding traditions.

### Dimension 2: Membership — Individual and Organisational, Global and Asymmetric

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP10, Lerner; IPO7, Dajer; IPO4, Bezerra; IPO1, Falck; IPO6, Ngounou)

*Dual membership:* People Powered offers both individual and organisational membership, accommodating practitioners who move between institutional roles. Diana Dajer exemplifies this: "Now I'm in a double role because four years ago I was elected as part of the board... I'm more like an individual that has a role." Carla Bezerra similarly joined the board as an individual with expertise, not as a representative of the Brazilian government.

*Global scale with North-South asymmetry:* The membership spans continents, but structural asymmetries persist. Diana Dajer is precise: "Within the global South, there are many global Norths and global Souths. I'm from the global South and I'm a woman, but I'm also from Bogotá that had a lot of opportunities." Language barriers, awareness of funding calls, and the volunteer culture of the field all reproduce global inequalities within the network.

*The membership-commitment gradient:* Members range from deeply engaged (Board members, programme participants) to loosely connected (newsletter subscribers, occasional event attendees). The participatory budgeting process for internal resource allocation creates high engagement among those who participate, but only a fraction of the membership engages at this level.

*Stefano Stortone's exit:* Stortone left People Powered due to a combination of sustainability pressures ("I no longer have the time or resources to go networking") and disillusionment with governance dynamics. His departure illustrates the structural challenge of retaining small practitioners who cannot afford the time investment that meaningful participation requires.

### Dimension 3: Governance — Participatory Budgeting as Internal Democracy Experiment

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP10, Lerner; IPO7, Dajer; IPO15, Stortone; IPO4, Bezerra)

*Member-driven governance:* People Powered's most distinctive governance feature is its participatory budgeting process for internal resource allocation — members collectively decide how a portion of the organisation's budget is spent. Josh Lerner frames this as walking the talk: an organisation promoting participatory democracy should itself be governed participatorily.

*Board structure:* A Board provides formal governance oversight. Board members serve as individuals, though some also represent member organisations. Diana Dajer describes representing Fundación Corona while maintaining her individual contribution. The Board includes diverse geographic representation but has been criticised for predominantly Northern composition.

*The participation-implementation gap:* Stefano Stortone identifies a structural governance problem: "I found myself making proposals, seeing those proposals voted on by the majority, and then being a candidate to implement the proposals — a conflict of interest as big as a house was created." The participatory process generates proposals; those proposals are then implemented by the same people who proposed them, creating a circular dynamic where participation and benefit are linked in ways that advantage insiders.

*The mentorship controversy:* Stortone describes clashing with Josh Lerner over a mentorship programme that "had been voted on democratically, but the design of the proposal wasn't democratic, because it was headed by the People Power organization, which isn't democratic, except in traditional forms." This critique — that the organisation's formal governance is representative rather than genuinely participatory — is a recurring theme.

*The "loved" network:* Despite these tensions, multiple interviewees describe People Powered's relational culture in unusually positive terms. The interviewer notes: "People love it, and why? Because People Powered shows it cares about people. Its events begin with icebreakers which always focus on people's health, mental health." Diana Dajer confirms the in-person connection was transformative. This emotional attachment — what the interviewer calls a "filial, fraternal relationship" — creates strong loyalty but also, as Stortone argues, a potentially "toxic" dependency when governance is not sufficiently horizontal.

### Dimension 4: Resources — American Funding, European Expansion, Donor-Shaped Programmes

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP10, Lerner; IPO4, Bezerra; IPO1, Falck)

*Funding shock and diversification:* People Powered was heavily dependent on USAID and other American sources. The first Trump administration's cuts eliminated "half or one third of the budget" (IPO4, Bezerra). The organisation successfully pivoted to European funding by opening a European legal entity — a move driven by practical necessity: some EU calls require a local presence.

*Donor-driven programmes:* Carla Bezerra critiques that "many of the programmes are done because they are a condition of the funder, not because they are the goals of the organisation." Examples include the Climate Democracy Accelerator and the Narratives Alliance — "interesting projects, very much aligned with the goals of People Powered, but also very much formatted by the donors."

*The American extractivist concern:* Stefano Stortone and the interviewer recall co-authoring a letter with Diana Dajer to People Powered, raising the question: "What antibodies do those who win a project built collectively but financed by an individual have, and what do they give back to the community to reward them for having written a project that they then built and immediately received a salary for?" This question — how collective intellectual labour is compensated when individuals capture the funding — remains unresolved.

*Andrés Falck's experience — the accidental opportunity:* Falck was able to represent People Powered in the Nets4DEM project because People Powered lacked a European entity at the time. This created an accidental opportunity that revived Coglobal at a moment of financial difficulty — an example of how network positioning, even when unplanned, can determine organisational survival.

### Dimension 5: External Relations — Between Civil Society and Government Networks

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP10, Lerner; IPO4, Bezerra)

*Deliberately non-governmental:* People Powered explicitly positions itself as a civil society and practitioner network, not a government network. Josh Lerner reportedly said the organisation "was created to do what OIDP was not doing" (IPO4, Bezerra). This distinguishes People Powered from government-focused networks and creates a clear institutional niche.

*Peace-building connection — a recent development:* Diana Dajer describes the recent convergence of her two professional worlds — participatory democracy and peace-building. A workshop in Kenya in 2026 marked a concrete step. Greta from People Powered contacted Dajer to explore this connection, and Dajer's existing peace-building network (Build Peace/Build Up) independently reached out to People Powered. This thematic expansion is recent (the last six months) and positions People Powered in a new field.

*Relationship with Decidim:* Carla Bezerra provides a comparative analysis: People Powered is "much more diverse in political views" and has "a very broad, open political view with minimum definition." Decidim has "a very defined political view" that attracts committed participants but can exclude others. Each approach creates distinct opportunities and limitations.

*OPG and the broader ecosystem:* People Powered intersects with OGP, OECD, and UN processes through its members' participation, but does not lead these relationships institutionally. Its external relations are more member-mediated than centrally managed.

### Dimension 6: Knowledge — Peer Learning, Not Training

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP10, Lerner; IPO7, Dajer)

*Peer learning infrastructure:* People Powered facilitates knowledge exchange through its programmes, events, and online platforms. The emphasis is on practitioner-to-practitioner learning rather than formal training.

*The research board legacy:* Diana Dajer's entry through the PB research board reflects People Powered's origin in scholar-practitioner collaboration. The research board brought together "people like Stephanie McNulty, Brian Wampler" — an intellectual community that predated and shaped the organisation.

*Knowledge as commons — the extraction question:* The letter by Stortone, Dajer, and the interviewer raised the question of knowledge extraction: when collective knowledge creates a project that an individual then funds and implements, what returns to the community? This question — central to the "commoning" dimension — has not been institutionally resolved.

### Dimension 7: Adaptation — European Expansion and Thematic Diversification

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IPO4, Bezerra; IFP10, Lerner)

*European legal entity:* The creation of a European legal entity was an adaptive response to funding diversification needs. It enables access to EU funding calls and reduces dependency on American sources. This is a structural adaptation with long-term implications for the organisation's geographic centre of gravity.

*Thematic expansion:* From PB to the full spectrum of participatory and deliberative democracy. The peace-building connection is the most recent expansion. The Climate Democracy Accelerator and Narratives Alliance represent donor-responsive thematic development.

*Generational transition:* Diana Dajer describes the transition from the original research/practice boards as "awkward" but necessary — "that generational change has allowed more and more to bring voices that are not mainstream." She does not see it as a crisis but as natural evolution: "I wouldn't call it a crisis."

### Dimension 8: Commoning

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IPO15, Stortone; IPO4, Bezerra; IPO7, Dajer)

*Participatory budgeting as commoning mechanism:* The internal PB process is People Powered's most explicit commoning practice — members collectively decide resource allocation, creating shared ownership of organisational direction.

*The unaddressed question — from commoning to extraction:* Stortone's critique identifies an unresolved tension: "The very concept of giving back must become structural. There is still no answer from my point of view on this." When collective labour produces value that individuals capture through funding, the commons logic breaks down. People Powered has not yet institutionalised a mechanism for ensuring that those who benefit from the collective do so in ways that return value to the collective.

*The culture of care as relational commons:* The "loved" network phenomenon — the icebreakers, the attention to personal well-being, the filial relationship — represents a relational commons that many members value deeply. This emotional infrastructure sustains commitment but, as Stortone argues, can become a dependency relationship if governance power is not genuinely distributed.

### Dimension 9: Impacts

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP10, Lerner; IPO7, Dajer)

*Field-building:* People Powered has contributed significantly to building the field of participatory democracy as a recognisable community of practice. Diana Dajer: "The opportunity to build the field together... that gave a lot of value to the world."

*Capacity building:* Through its programmes, People Powered has supported practitioners in multiple countries to develop and implement participatory processes. The impact is at the practitioner level — building skills, networks, and confidence.

*Policy influence — indirect:* Unlike OIDP or OGP, People Powered does not engage governments directly. Its policy influence is indirect — through the practitioners and organisations it supports.

*Limitation — the measurement gap:* Like most networks in the corpus, People Powered's impacts are difficult to attribute and measure. The "Rising Stars" programme, mentorship, and peer exchanges create diffuse impacts that resist quantification.

## 3. Cross-Cutting Analytical Themes

### Theme 1: The Love-Critique Paradox

People Powered generates stronger emotional attachment than any other organisation in the corpus — members describe it in familial terms, value its care for their well-being, and maintain loyalty through career transitions. Simultaneously, it generates sharp critique from former members who see its participatory governance as more performative than substantive. This paradox — the network that is most loved is also most criticised — may be inherent in an organisation that aspires to practice what it preaches. The higher the democratic aspiration, the more visible the gap between aspiration and practice.

### Theme 2: The Participation-Extraction Cycle

Stortone identifies a structural cycle: members participate in proposal creation, proposals are voted on by those who participated, proposals are then implemented — and funded — by the same people who proposed them. Those outside this cycle (small organisations, Global South practitioners without the time to participate intensively) are structurally disadvantaged. The network that is built on participation can reproduce the inequalities it claims to challenge.

### Theme 3: The European Pivot as Organisational Transformation

The creation of a European legal entity is not merely an administrative adjustment — it represents a potential shift in the organisation's centre of gravity from its American origins toward a European institutional logic. EU funding brings different requirements, different timelines, and different political sensibilities than American philanthropy. Over time, this may reshape People Powered's governance, programming, and culture in ways that are not yet visible.
