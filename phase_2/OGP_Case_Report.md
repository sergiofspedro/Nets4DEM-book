# OGP — Open Government Partnership
## Analytical Case Report — Phase 2 (Nets4DEM)

> **Sources:** IFP16 — Tim Hughes (Director, OGP) and IPO15 — Stefano Stortone (BIPART, OGP project participant). Two complementary interviews, June 2026.

---

## 1. Case Overview

**What OGP is:** The Open Government Partnership (OGP) is a multilateral initiative launched in 2011 that brings together national and local governments with civil society organisations around commitments to transparency, citizen participation, accountability, and technology-enabled open government. It operates through a distinctive mechanism: participating governments co-create National Action Plans with civil society, committing to concrete reforms that are independently monitored. OGP now includes over 75 national governments and 150+ local governments, supported by a small secretariat and an independent reporting mechanism.

**Central sources:** Tim Hughes (IFP16), OGP's Director, provides the strategic, institutional perspective on OGP's model, evolution, and challenges. Stefano Stortone (IPO15), founder of the Italian social enterprise BIPART, provides a grassroots perspective — as a small practitioner who found an alignment of interests with OGP around a specific platform project. Stortone's interview is notably critical of network governance dynamics more broadly, offering an outsider-insider perspective on participation, power, and sustainability in the democracy support ecosystem.

**What the case is mainly about:** OGP represents a distinctive institutional innovation — a multilateral mechanism that creates binding commitments from governments through a co-creation process with civil society. It is not a membership network in the conventional sense but a structured partnership between state and non-state actors. Its challenges centre on the quality and ambition of commitments, the power asymmetry between governments and civil society in the co-creation process, and the question of whether OGP is a reform accelerator or a legitimacy platform for governments that have no intention of genuine openness.

## 2. Dimension-by-Dimension Analysis

### Dimension 1: Organisational Identity — Between Multilateral Institution and Reform Movement

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP16, Tim Hughes; IPO15, Stefano Stortone)

*Distinctive form — the action plan mechanism:* OGP is neither a traditional membership network nor an international organisation. Its core mechanism — the National Action Plan (NAP) co-created between government and civil society — is a governance innovation that creates formal commitments with monitoring and reporting obligations. Tim Hughes describes this as OGP's unique contribution: a structured space where governments commit publicly and are held accountable.

*Identity tension — platform vs. community:* OGP is simultaneously a government-to-government platform (where states share reforms and learn from each other), a government-civil society partnership (where civil society co-creates and monitors commitments), and an advocacy community (where open government reformers coordinate across borders). These three identities coexist but create different expectations.

*Scale and selectivity:* With 75+ national and 150+ local governments, OGP has significant scale. However, participation is not automatic — governments must meet eligibility criteria and demonstrate commitment. This selective membership distinguishes OGP from universal-membership multilateral bodies.

*Stortone's perspective — OGP as outlier:* For Stortone, OGP was the network where he "found an overlap of interests in good faith" — a space where his platform project could be developed without the extractive dynamics he perceived in other networks. Unlike People Powered or OIDP, OGP "opened its arms to that project hypothesis." This suggests OGP may function differently for practitioners — as an enabling platform rather than a gatekeeping network.

### Dimension 2: Membership — States and Civil Society in Asymmetric Partnership

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP16, Tim Hughes)

*Dual membership:* Governments and civil society organisations are co-equal participants in OGP's formal structure. Both must sign on to the Open Government Declaration, and both participate in the co-creation process. This institutionalised dualism is distinctive — most networks include either governments or civil society, not both as formal partners.

*Power asymmetry in practice:* Despite formal equality, governments hold significantly more power in the NAP process. They control the resources, the implementation capacity, and ultimately the decision on which commitments to include. Civil society's power is primarily in monitoring, advocacy, and naming-and-shaming — but this depends on a functioning civil society space that is itself under threat in many OGP countries.

*The local programme expansion:* OGP's expansion to local governments (OGP Local) represents a membership diversification that responds to the reality that many open government reforms are implemented at the municipal level. This creates a multi-level governance structure — national and local governments operating within the same framework.

*Stortone's perspective — the survival threshold:* Stortone identifies a structural barrier: small civil society organisations cannot afford to participate in OGP's process meaningfully because "networking is essential for being part of projects, but if you don't network, people forget what you do." The OGP model assumes civil society capacity that may not exist in contexts where organisations struggle to survive — a "dog chasing its tail where networking is essential to get resources, but you need resources to do networking."

### Dimension 3: Governance — The Co-Creation Model as Democratic Innovation

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP16, Tim Hughes; IPO15, Stefano Stortone)

*The co-creation mechanism:* OGP's central governance innovation is the requirement that National Action Plans be co-created — not just consulted on — with civil society. This goes beyond typical consultation models in multilateral processes. Tim Hughes frames co-creation as OGP's core value proposition and its most difficult practical challenge.

*Independent reporting mechanism:* Commitments are independently assessed through the Independent Reporting Mechanism (IRM), which publishes progress reports on each country's action plan. This creates transparency and accountability that governments cannot control — a structural check on the natural tendency to make weak or unambitious commitments.

*The secretariat's role:* A small secretariat supports the process but does not drive it — the action plans are nationally owned. This limited secretariat role distinguishes OGP from more top-down multilateral processes and from networks where a central body sets the agenda.

*Stortone's critique — representative democracy within networks:* Stortone argues that all organisations, "even civil associations, have this DNA problem — they are all representative democracy and fight representative democracy." The co-creation model, while innovative, still operates within a representative governance framework where some actors (governments, large CSOs, the secretariat) have structural advantages over others (small CSOs, individual practitioners).

*The inclusion paradox:* "The bigger we are, the more visibility we have, but the bigger we are, the more difficult it is to include everyone." Stortone identifies a structural trade-off between scale and democratic depth — as OGP grows, the meaningful participation of smaller actors becomes harder to maintain.

### Dimension 4: Resources — Government Contributions and Foundation Support

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP16, Tim Hughes)

*Funding structure:* OGP is funded through a combination of government contributions from participating countries, foundation grants, and multilateral support. This mixed funding model provides more stability than purely grant-dependent networks but also creates dependency on the governments that OGP is supposed to hold accountable.

*The resource access question — Stortone's perspective:* For small practitioners, the resources that flow through OGP and similar networks create a structural tension. Stortone found himself "making proposals and then seeing those proposals voted on, and then even being a candidate to implement the proposals — so a conflict of interest as big as a house was created." The network creates the proposals, the network's governance allocates the resources, and the same people who proposed may benefit — a circular dynamic that advantages insiders.

*Survival economics:* Stortone's withdrawal from most networks was driven by economic necessity: "Since Bipart was born, which still struggles with sustainability, I have essentially cut all ties because I no longer have the time or resources to go networking." This is not critique of networks per se but a structural observation — network participation requires resources that small organisations do not have, creating a participation gap that favours established, well-funded actors.

### Dimension 5: External Relations — Between Multilateralism and Grassroots Reform

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP16, Tim Hughes)

*Multilateral positioning:* OGP operates at the intersection of multilateral governance and grassroots reform. It has relationships with the UN system, the World Bank, and bilateral development agencies. These relationships provide legitimacy and resources but also create expectations about OGP's role in the broader governance ecosystem.

*Relationship with the democracy support field:* OGP intersects with the participatory democracy networks studied in Nets4DEM — particularly OIDP at the local government level and People Powered at the civil society level — but its primary identity is government-focused. This positions OGP differently from civil-society-led networks: its leverage comes from governments' willingness to participate, not from civil society's mobilising power.

*Stortone's perspective — OGP as enabler vs. extractor:* For Stortone, OGP functioned as a space where he could contribute "something procedurally, model-wise, or vision-wise" without the extractive dynamics he experienced elsewhere. This suggests OGP's platform model — where contributions are structured around specific projects with clear boundaries — may avoid some of the ambiguity that characterises looser networks where "the time dedicated to doing other things is greater than the time spent building my vision."

### Dimension 6: Knowledge — Reform Commitments as Evidence

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP16, Tim Hughes)

*The commitment bank as knowledge infrastructure:* OGP's database of thousands of reform commitments — with implementation status, civil society commentary, and independent assessments — constitutes a unique knowledge resource on open government reforms. This is evidence-based policy learning infrastructure at global scale.

*Peer learning as methodology:* OGP facilitates peer exchange between governments and between civil society organisations across countries. This peer learning is less structured than formal research or evaluation but is valued by participants as a mechanism for spreading reform ideas and practices.

*Limitation — commitment quality vs. quantity:* The knowledge infrastructure reveals what governments have committed to, but the quality and ambition of commitments varies dramatically. The IRM provides assessment but does not guarantee that commitments are transformative rather than cosmetic.

### Dimension 7: Adaptation — Maintaining Relevance in a Closing Space

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP16, Tim Hughes)

*Democratic backsliding as context:* OGP was launched in a period of democratic optimism (2011, post-Arab Spring). It now operates in a context of democratic backsliding, shrinking civic space, and the rise of authoritarian governments — including within some OGP member states. This creates a fundamental challenge: how does a reform partnership function when one partner (the government) is actively undermining the conditions for the other partner (civil society) to participate?

*Local expansion as adaptation:* The expansion to OGP Local can be read as adaptation — shifting the locus of reform to the municipal level where governments may be more responsive and civil society more engaged than at the national level.

*Stortone's perspective — the accidental opportunity:* Stortone's engagement with OGP was serendipitous — "when this opportunity arose, I said if there's an opportunity where I can contribute something procedurally, model-wise, or vision-wise, I'll do it." This suggests OGP's adaptive capacity lies partly in its ability to capture practitioners at moments when their interests and OGP's agenda align — an opportunistic rather than strategic adaptation mechanism.

### Dimension 8: Commoning

**Presence: MARGINALLY PRESENT** (IFP16, Tim Hughes; IPO15, Stefano Stortone)

*Commitments as commons of reform ideas:* OGP's National Action Plan commitments represent a form of commons — reform ideas that are publicly available, monitored, and shared across countries. A commitment made in one country can be adapted by another, creating a shared pool of reform practices.

*The accountability commons:* The IRM and civil society monitoring create a shared accountability infrastructure — a commons of transparency that no single government can control.

*Limitation — the extraction risk:* Stortone identifies a structural risk in network governance: "those who govern the networks have an interest in having people, and those who are not part of the networks have an interest in being within the networks." When participation in the commons becomes necessary for survival, the commons can become a mechanism of exclusion rather than inclusion — those outside cannot access the resources that those inside control.

*The proposal-ownership gap:* Stortone describes creating proposals that were then implemented by others differently from how he conceived them: "after having participated for so many hours, my contribution did not have accountability criteria." This is a commoning failure — the individual contributes to the collective pool, but the collective process does not return value to the contributor in a way that respects their contribution.

### Dimension 9: Impacts

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP16, Tim Hughes)

*Scope — reform commitments:* OGP's most measurable impact is the thousands of reform commitments that have been made, implemented, and independently assessed. These span transparency, participation, accountability, and technology across 75+ countries.

*Scope — institutional change:* The co-creation mechanism has institutionalised civil society participation in government reform processes in some countries, creating permanent structures (multi-stakeholder forums, open government units) that persist beyond individual action plans.

*Cognitive — the open government norm:* OGP has contributed to establishing "open government" as a global norm. The concept — that governments should be transparent, participatory, and accountable — is now embedded in international policy discourse.

*Limitation — ambition gap:* Not all commitments are transformative. Some are administrative reforms with limited impact on power relations. The distinction between genuine openness and performative openness remains a central tension.

## 3. Cross-Cutting Analytical Themes

### Theme 1: The Participation-Survival Paradox

Stortone's experience crystallises a structural paradox: networks require participation, participation requires resources, and resources are accessed through networks. Small organisations and individual practitioners are caught in a cycle where they need to network to survive but lack the resources to network effectively. This creates a structural advantage for larger, better-funded organisations — a Darwinian selection that networks rarely acknowledge or address.

### Theme 2: Co-Creation Between Unequal Partners

OGP's co-creation model is a genuine governance innovation, but it operates within fundamental power asymmetries. Governments control the resources, the implementation, and ultimately the decision. Civil society's tools — monitoring, advocacy, exposure — depend on a functioning civic space that is shrinking in many contexts. The model works best where governments are already committed to openness; it struggles where governments participate for legitimacy without intention of genuine reform.
