# GPR2C — Global Platform for the Right to the City
## Analytical Case Report — Phase 2 (Nets4DEM)

> **Sources:** IFP11 — Lorena Zarate (co-coordinator, GPR2C; ex-President, HIC) and IFP14 — Nelson Saule Jr. (co-coordinator, GPR2C; Polis Institute, Brazil). Two complementary interviews, June 2026.

---

## 1. Case Overview

**What GPR2C is:** The Global Platform for the Right to the City (GPR2C) is a "network of networks" — a coordination space where existing international organisations, networks, and social movements converge around the shared framework of the right to the city. It was formally constituted in 2014 following preparatory discussions dating to 2012. It has no legal personality, no independent budget, and no dedicated staff. Instead, it functions as a voluntary political coalition supported by a rotating coordination group and a support team hosted by HIC (Habitat International Coalition) and the Polis Institute (Brazil).

**Central sources:** Lorena Zarate (IFP11) and Nelson Saule Jr. (IFP14) are the GPR2C's two co-coordinators. Zarate was previously President of HIC; Saule is a Brazilian researcher and activist from the Polis Institute. Together they provide founder-level, strategic perspectives on the GPR2C's origins, governance, and challenges.

**What the case is mainly about:** The GPR2C is the purest expression of the "network of networks" form in the Nets4DEM corpus. It was created explicitly to overcome competition and fragmentation among habitat and housing networks, using the right-to-the-city framework as a unifying concept without requiring member organisations to abandon their own agendas. It represents an extreme case of voluntary, non-formalised coordination — its strength (flexibility, low cost, ideological alignment) and its weakness (precarious sustainability, dependence on individual commitment, uncertainty about the future) are two sides of the same coin.

## 2. Dimension-by-Dimension Analysis

### Dimension 1: Organisational Identity — A Platform, Not an Organisation

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP11, Zarate; IFP14, Saule)

*"Platform" as deliberate choice:* The term was carefully selected to signal non-competition. Zarate explains: "Choosing the term platform was intentional — not to create a new organisation, not to compete with what was already there, but to create a space for coordination among existing actors." Each member network "has your agenda — habitat international coalition has a long-time agenda about human rights… international alliance of inhabitants also a long-time perspective." The platform "is not necessary to modify your agenda, but you consider your agenda when they are working with some specific issues, they are working with the right to the city" (IFP14, Saule).

*Historical roots — a 20-year trajectory:* The GPR2C did not emerge from nowhere. Saule traces a legacy through: the 1992 Rio Earth Summit (where the "World Treaty on Just and Sustainable Cities and Villages" was drafted by civil society), the creation of Brazil's National Urban Reform Forum, the World Social Forum (Porto Alegre, 2000 onwards), and the World Urban Forum. Zarate points to the 15M movement, Occupy, and the municipalist wave as contextual drivers.

*Constitutional moment — São Paulo 2014:* The platform was formally constituted at an international meeting in São Paulo involving key networks: HIC, the International Alliance of Inhabitants, the Habitat International Coalition, and others. Each brought its own agenda; the platform was designed to hold them together without requiring any to subordinate its work.

*No legal personality — by design:* "We don't have legal status. It's informal. It's a political coalition, not an institutional or legal coalition" (IFP14, Saule). Zarate affirms: "It's not something that is going to compete with HIC or any of those networks." The funding flows through existing member organisations, not through the platform itself.

*The right to the city as unifying framework:* While member organisations focus on specific dimensions — housing rights, evictions, economic rights, social production of habitat — the platform provides the holistic, rights-based framework that connects these fragments.

### Dimension 2: Membership — A Voluntary Coalition of Existing Networks

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP11, Zarate; IFP14, Saule)

*Network-of-networks composition:* The GPR2C brings together international networks (HIC, International Alliance of Inhabitants, Habitat for Humanity), national coalitions (Brazil's Urban Reform Forum), social movements (homeless workers' movements, housing cooperatives), research institutes (Polis Institute), academic partners, and — distinctively — local government networks (UCLG). Saule mentions "professional associations for architect or engineer, geographers, some academics, class unions."

*Inclusion of local governments:* UCLG is a formal partner, and several progressive local governments (Barcelona, Mexico City, Montevideo, cities in Ecuador) have aligned with the platform's agenda. However, UCLG's membership is heterogeneous: "It's not a consensus for different local governments, different cities. Some are very conservative" (IFP14, Saule). A Commission of Human Rights within UCLG provides the most natural interlocutor.

*Voluntary commitment as membership cost:* There are no membership dues. The "cost" of membership is political alignment and voluntary contribution of time, expertise, and advocacy capacity. Saule: "They consider your agenda when they are working with some specific issues — they are working with the right to the city."

*Tensions — internal to organisations, not between them:* Saule's analysis suggests a distinctive pattern: conflicts are more likely to occur within member organisations than between them. "There's some tensions inside the coalitions, inside internal groups, more than tensions between one organisation with another. I don't think we have this kind of problems until this moment." The example: some African housing rights organisations are "skeptical of the right to the city" because they fear it dilutes the housing rights focus. This tension plays out within HIC's membership rather than at platform level.

### Dimension 3: Governance — Minimal Structure, Maximum Voluntarism

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP11, Zarate; IFP14, Saule)

*The coordination group:* The organisations present at the founding São Paulo meeting became the initial coordination group. "We invite exactly these organisations we joined this international meeting in São Paulo to create the global platform to be the coordination" (IFP14, Saule). This founding-moment governance structure has continued, with the coordination group serving as the primary decision-making body.

*The support team:* HIC and the Polis Institute provide the administrative backbone — a "support team" of individuals from both organisations who handle website management, material production, campaign coordination, and meeting organisation. This is not a secretariat in the conventional sense; it is an arrangement where existing organisations lend staff capacity to the platform.

*Working groups:* Three working groups — advocacy, communication, and (described as a third group in development) — operationalise specific platform functions. These are the most tangible governance structures, bringing together representatives from different member organisations around shared tasks.

*No formal governance documents or procedures:* The governance operates through informal consensus, trust, and shared political commitment. Zarate describes the co-coordination role as based on "commitment and conviction. Sometimes we have funding, sometimes we don't have funding and we just do it."

*The individual-institution tension:* Both co-coordinators represent institutions (HIC and Polis Institute) while also serving in personal capacity. Zarate's account suggests that the platform's continuity depends on individual commitment that exceeds institutional mandates. Saule similarly describes the work as flowing from political conviction rather than organisational delegation.

### Dimension 4: Resources — Precarious, Project-Based, Personally Sustained

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP11, Zarate; IFP14, Saule)

*Funding trajectory:* The Ford Foundation provided substantial support for the first six years ("for the beginning, for I think six years, good support from the Foundation was very important"). After this initial period, funding shifted to project-based support from Misereor (a German cooperation agency) and specific UCLG contracts.

*Current funding:* "In this moment, we are discussing again with Misereor a new project for continuing the work, especially advocacy and capacity building. And also we have a project with UCLG for to collaborate with this space they create between civil society and local governments" (IFP14, Saule). These are time-limited project grants, not core institutional funding.

*Personal subsidies as funding model:* Zarate is explicit that the co-coordinators' work is personally subsidised: "We're doing this because of commitment and conviction. Sometimes we have funding, sometimes we don't have funding." She sustains herself through "small things with other organisations and small writing commitments." Saule similarly combines platform coordination with his role at the Polis Institute and academic work.

*No independent budget:* The platform has no bank account. All funds flow through member organisations — primarily HIC and Polis Institute — who then commit staff time and resources to platform activities. "So it's more like a line of work, a line of action that is embedded in different organisations" (IFP11, Zarate).

*Funding paradox:* The deliberate avoidance of legal status and independent funding protects the platform from competition with member organisations and from donor-driven agenda distortion. But it leaves the platform dependent on the financial health and strategic priorities of its host organisations — and, crucially, on the personal commitment of individuals who work beyond what they are paid to do.

### Dimension 5: External Relations — Multilateral Advocacy with Shrinking Access

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP14, Saule; IFP11, Zarate)

*UN engagement — shifting terrain:* The platform has a long history of multilateral advocacy, particularly around the New Urban Agenda (Habitat III, 2016) and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 11 on sustainable cities). However, Saule describes a deteriorating environment: "Until this moment, we don't know if civil society can join this meeting, can provide some statement. It became very, very difficult for us to have a dialogue, to have a space for advocacy."

*Exclusion from high-level forums:* At the recent high-level meeting of the New Urban Agenda, "only the government, the members of the organisation of the United Nations could join." Similarly, in COP processes, "the government decides 100 indicators for the prevention of climate. We don't have a lot of difficulty to provide proposals or view in this process." The shrinking of civic space is quantifiable and directly affecting the platform's most important advocacy channel.

*UCLG partnership — selective alignment:* The UCLG partnership provides access to local government networks and the "townhouse" dialogues — structured spaces at UCLG congresses for civil society-local government exchange. However, the alignment is partial: UCLG is "working more now with the social service" and "working with the perspective of the commons," while the platform wants deeper engagement with democracy, political participation, and urban policy implementation.

*National government engagement — issue-specific:* Dialogue with national governments occurs around specific processes: the New Urban Agenda review (coordinated by Poland and Malawi), the Pact for the Future, and COP agreements. This is transactional rather than institutionalised.

*Trans-local dialogues as niche methodology:* The platform has developed a distinctive practice of "inter-regional dialogues and trans-local dialogues" — bringing together actors from different regions around shared urban challenges. This methodology is "the core of what we do" (IFP11, Zarate) and differentiates the platform from networks that operate at a single scale.

### Dimension 6: Knowledge — The Right-to-the-City Framework as Intellectual Infrastructure

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP11, Zarate; IFP14, Saule)

*The World Charter for the Right to the City:* The platform has produced and disseminated core documents: the World Charter for the Right to the City and the Global Charter-Agenda for Human Rights in the City. These serve as both advocacy tools and intellectual frameworks, providing a common language across diverse member organisations.

*Counter-data and normative production:* Zarate emphasises the platform's role in connecting the right to the city to emerging agendas — "the care agenda and the caring cities, climate justice, the commons and public-community partnerships." This conceptual expansion is a form of knowledge production, creating new discursive connections that enable coalition-building across previously separate movements.

*Glossary as codification:* Saule mentions a "glossary of the right to the city and climate justice" — a specific knowledge product developed through webinars, task forces, and capacity-building activities, then deployed in workshops in Angola and Indonesia.

*Limitation — knowledge as advocacy support, not primary output:* Like several other operational networks in the corpus, GPR2C's knowledge production serves advocacy and capacity-building rather than functioning as an independent research programme. Systematic codification is secondary to political action.

### Dimension 7: Adaptation — Uncertainty as the Defining Condition

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP11, Zarate; IFP14, Saule)

*Political crisis as existential challenge:* Both co-coordinators express uncertainty about the platform's future. Saule is candid: "I don't know if I have an answer about the future of the global platform. The planet is very difficult. When you created the global platform 10 years ago, we have a lot of conflicts now — war in different regions."

*The UN model in question:* The very framework that the platform has used for multilateral advocacy is eroding: "The model we work for a long time — the United Nations — was in crisis, a serious crisis. It's very difficult for us to have a concrete strategy in this moment."

*Continuity through individual commitment:* The platform's primary adaptation mechanism is the personal commitment of its co-coordinators and member organisations. Zarate frames this explicitly: "We're trying to maintain the platform alive." This voluntarist adaptation model works in the short term but creates vulnerability — it depends on individuals who cannot be replaced institutionally.

*From global moments to trans-local spaces:* An adaptive shift is detectable in the platform's evolving methodology. While global advocacy (UN, Habitat, COP) remains important, there is increasing emphasis on trans-local dialogues — bringing together specific cities, regions, and communities around shared challenges. This may represent a strategic adaptation to the shrinking of global civic space: if you cannot change the UN, connect the cities directly.

### Dimension 8: Commoning

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP11, Zarate; IFP14, Saule)

*Platform as commons of networks:* The GPR2C is perhaps the purest commons form in the corpus — a shared space created by existing organisations, sustained by voluntary contribution, governed by political alignment rather than legal structure, and producing shared goods (advocacy, knowledge, campaigns) that no single member could produce alone.

*Non-competition as commoning principle:* The explicit anti-competitive design — no legal status, no independent budget, funding flowing through members — is a commoning design choice. By refusing to become an organisation, the platform protects the commons from enclosure by any single member.

*The commitment subsidy:* The co-coordinators' unpaid and underpaid work represents a form of commoning subsidy — individuals contributing more than they extract, sustaining the shared space through personal sacrifice. This is both the platform's strength (it functions without significant resources) and its vulnerability (it cannot function without these specific individuals).

*Knowledge as commons infrastructure:* The World Charter, the glossary, the campaign materials — these are collectively produced and collectively owned knowledge commons, available to all members for deployment in their own contexts.

### Dimension 9: Impacts

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IFP11, Zarate; IFP14, Saule)

*Scope — discursive:* The GPR2C's most significant impact is discursive — it has contributed to mainstreaming the right-to-the-city framework in international policy discourse, urban development agendas, and local government programmes. The framework now appears in UN documents, local charters, and municipal policies in cities across multiple continents.

*Scope — normative:* The World Charter for the Right to the City and the Global Charter-Agenda serve as normative reference points for urban social movements and progressive local governments. These documents provide intellectual and political infrastructure that member organisations deploy in their own advocacy.

*Institutional — the UCLG interface:* The platform has helped establish structured dialogue spaces between civil society and local governments through the UCLG "townhouse" mechanism. This institutional innovation creates a channel for movements to engage with the institutions of local governance.

*Limitation — attribution challenge:* The platform's deliberately non-institutional form makes attribution difficult. When a city adopts a right-to-the-city charter or a movement successfully advocates for housing rights, it is difficult to determine how much of this outcome should be attributed to the platform versus its member organisations. This is an inherent feature of the network-of-networks form.

*Limitation — existential uncertainty:* Unlike ALDA or IAF, the GPR2C cannot guarantee its own survival. It depends on the continued commitment of its co-coordinators, the financial health of its host organisations, and a global political environment that currently trends toward civic space closure. The platform's future is genuinely uncertain in a way that more institutionalised organisations' futures are not.

## 3. Cross-Cutting Analytical Themes

### Theme 1: The Anti-Organisation as Organisational Form

The GPR2C represents a deliberate institutional innovation: the creation of a collective political actor that refuses to become an organisation. No legal status, no independent budget, no dedicated staff, no membership dues — and yet it produces campaigns, glossaries, charters, advocacy interventions, and trans-local dialogues that would be impossible for any single member. The anti-organisation form protects against competition and co-optation, but at the cost of sustainability, scalability, and institutional memory.

### Theme 2: The Commitment Subsidy

Both co-coordinators sustain the platform through personal commitment that exceeds their institutional mandates and, in Zarate's case, their paid work. This "commitment subsidy" — the gap between what the platform costs to run and what it is funded to run — is filled by individuals who believe in the mission. This is simultaneously inspiring (people working for conviction, not salary) and precarious (the platform's continuity depends on specific individuals being willing and able to subsidise it). The commitment subsidy is a fragile foundation for long-term institutional resilience.

### Theme 3: From Global to Trans-Local — Adaptation in a Closing Space

The platform's trajectory from primarily global advocacy (UN processes, Habitat conferences, COP negotiations) toward trans-local dialogues (connecting specific cities and communities across regions) represents an adaptive response to the closing of global civic space. If multilateral institutions no longer provide meaningful access, connecting cities directly — bypassing the national and global levels — becomes an alternative pathway. This adaptation mirrors the "sandwich strategy" described in the HIC case report, but with an emphasis on horizontal exchange rather than vertical pressure.
