# FIDE — Federation for Innovation in Democracy Europe
## Analytical Case Report — Phase 2 (Nets4DEM)

> **Source:** FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere (Director). Single interview, June 2026.

---

## 1. Case Overview

**What FIDE is:** The Federation for Innovation in Democracy Europe is a Brussels-based international NGO that serves as a European-level convenor and advisor on deliberative democracy. Founded in the aftermath of the Belgian G1000 project, FIDE was created to fill a structural gap: the European Commission, Council of Europe, and national governments needed a reliable interlocutor for deliberative democracy expertise, but the field was fragmented across independent actors, for-profit consultants, and single-issue initiatives. FIDE operates with an annual budget of approximately €350,000, no structural EU funding, and a minimal staff under Director Yves Dejaeghere.

**Distinctive model:** FIDE represents a deliberately **non-membership** organisation that calls itself a "federation" — a paradox Yves explains through the ethical imperative of avoiding conflicts of interest when an advisory organisation also convenes the actors who bid on government contracts. Its governance is light: an executive board meets quarterly to approve budget and strategy; an advisory board of respected figures plays a largely symbolic role. FIDE applies a rigorous three-criteria framework to project selection (policy impact, lighthouse potential, genuine need) and maintains a deliberate posture of strategic abstinence from most networks, including Nets4Dem.

**Current state:** Operating at a maintenance level with a selective project portfolio. Core activities include: (a) a by-invitation professional network for civil servants working on deliberative democracy at regional/national level (~40–50 members, funded by Bertelsmann); (b) direct advisory engagement with governments in Ukraine, Bosnia, and Moldova; (c) ad hoc expert convening when European institutions request advice. The organisation is navigating a contracting funding environment with the withdrawal of OSF and USAID.

## 2. Dimension-by-Dimension Analysis

### Dimension 1: Organisational Identity

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere)

*Name and origin:* The name "FIDE" was originally an acronym for "Foundation for Innovation in Democracy in Europe." Belgian law prohibited "foundation" for the organisation's legal form, so the last word was changed to "federation" to preserve the acronym. The Latin meaning of "fide" (trust) was discovered after the fact — a happy accident Yves treats with characteristic candour: *"I could now give it a beautiful backstory… but the piece is the first song, so I'm just being honest"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere).

*Identity as convenor:* FIDE positions itself not as a repository of expertise but as a convener of leading actors. Its founding pitch proposed *"bring together the leading actors in Europe. So if somebody wanted advice, we would bring Marcin Gerwin from Poland, the guy from Ireland, and et cetera, and have them work together rather than have one person be a sort of oracle in this space"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere). This anti-oracle identity is central: FIDE does not claim a monopoly on deliberative wisdom.

*Quality-before-quantity motto:* The organisation's core philosophical commitment: *"Increase the quality and quantity, but always in that order"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere). This distinguishes FIDE from actors who prioritise volume — *"let's do as many as possible and everywhere, at some point in what way, whatever, as long as we can get them done"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere).

*Not a membership organisation:* Despite the name "federation," FIDE is explicitly not a membership organisation. Yves insists on this distinction: *"The idea of federation, if you still want to have a play on words, is that we federate people a bit in the sense of convening, but we are not a membership organization"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere).

### Dimension 2: Membership — Rejected by Design

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere)

*Conscious non-membership:* FIDE's most distinctive structural feature is the deliberate rejection of a membership model. The reasoning is ethical. The deliberative democracy field contains actors across a vast spectrum — from anarcho-syndicalist movements to Deloitte consultants — many of whom are for-profit practitioners doing excellent work. If FIDE advised governments while counting such actors as members, a structural conflict of interest would arise.

Yves makes the argument with a vivid analogy: *"Replace us with the car industry association and then Volkswagen… the car association giving advice on car standards and then one of the members of the car industry association being Volkswagen, getting money from the commission. You'd be a bit like, eh, this is a bit weird"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere). The risk was especially acute during the Conference on the Future of Europe period, when several million euros were available and multiple potential members would tender for contracts.

*Consequence:* FIDE operates as a standard NGO with an executive board and an advisory board — not a membership organisation. The advisory board functions as a network of high-profile volunteers: *"Call on me if you need me, but I'm really fine if you don't"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere).

*Separate civil servant network:* FIDE also runs a separate, by-invitation professional network for civil servants (~40–50 people). This is not a FIDE membership structure but a targeted peer-exchange platform funded by Bertelsmann. *"This network has about 40 people… It's Ireland, Belgium, the Bundestag, the Polish parliament"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere).

### Dimension 3: Governance — Light, with Interchangeability as Principle

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere)

*Two-tier structure:* FIDE's governance consists of an executive board that meets quarterly — Yves proposes the budget, presents projects, and seeks approval on strategic choices — and an advisory board of respected figures whose role is more symbolic than operational. *"So I'm not just an individual, you know, doing whatever I want"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere).

*Interchangeability as governance criterion:* Yves articulates a distinctive design principle: governance is healthy when individuals are substitutable. *"A good ship means at some point you could change a bit and the course of the ship wouldn't change dramatically. That would be actually a good definition for me of good governance"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere). He applies this to himself: *"Fide would also have worked if it wasn't me, but somebody else… Camille Doublé could have done my job and it would probably have been the same"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere).

*Lightness as strategy:* Beyond the formal board structure, FIDE prefers ad hoc convening over heavy governance: *"We try to more see things on an ad hoc basis than having a large oil tanker over governance system"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere). The "oil tanker" metaphor contrasts with FIDE's self-image as a small, agile organisation.

*Advisory board as symbolic:* The advisory board met twice. Yves is candid about its limited operational role: *"I would say 90% of [its value] is showing who supports your organization, what is reflected in your organization, rather than that you engage them constantly"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere).

### Dimension 4: Resources — €350,000 and No Structural Funding

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere)

*Budget and staff:* FIDE operates on approximately €350,000/year with minimal staff. Yves describes the constraint with a striking metaphor: *"I have six bullets in my gun"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere). Every choice matters.

*No Horizon Europe projects:* This is a deliberate choice. *"FIDE is a no horizon project"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere). The rationale: Horizon projects would pull FIDE toward funder-driven agendas rather than its own strategic priorities.

*Below-cost delivery:* FIDE regularly delivers projects at a financial loss. The Council of Europe contributed less than €10,000 for nine weekends of guidance across three Ukrainian cities. *"If I was a for profit, you know, I would be losing money on this. So basically we invest in those projects"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere). Colleague Kyle Redman has been the main practitioner on these engagements.

*Contracting funding environment:* Yves situates FIDE's resource strategy within a broader contraction: *"Especially now that OSF is gone and USAID is gone… the cake is not getting bigger"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere). This scarcity conditions every decision.

*Litmus test:* The resource constraint is operationalised through a simple question: *"Would I have done this if this project didn't exist?"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere). When a funder proposed a border areas project, Yves and the board concluded that border areas were not something FIDE would have pursued organically: *"We would be putting our little speed boat and going towards that bow just because there's money in there"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere).

### Dimension 5: External Relations — Strategic Abstinence

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere)

*Network selection:* Yves explicitly opts out of most networks, including Nets4Dem. *"I'm the only partner of Nets4Dem that is not coming back in the next project"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere). He frames this not as criticism of the networks but as strategic discipline: *"Nets4Dem works fine without me"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere).

*Key relationships:* Council of Europe (Ukraine project, <€10,000); Bertelsmann (civil servant network); European Commission (ad hoc advice, mainly during Conference on the Future of Europe); individual partners in Ukraine, Bosnia, Moldova.

*Selective engagement:* The three-criteria framework governs external engagement: (1) policy impact, not experimentation; (2) lighthouse potential; (3) genuine need for FIDE's involvement. Declined engagements include the Democratic Odyssey (too experimental) and the border areas project (funder-driven). *"I wasn't involved in the Odyssey. I was like, this is great, but you have Kami, even at your Talipso, you are… great addition. And I could travel to Athens, but you're fine without me"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere).

*The social dimension of refusal:* Yves acknowledges that saying no carries interpersonal costs. *"Half of my job is saying no to people and justifying myself"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere). He describes being asked repeatedly to join a European project: at a coffee, someone told him he *"looked edgy"* — *"it was like I had to justify for the fifth time. Why are you not a part?… It's almost like saying to a friend, why are you not coming to my party?"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere).

### Dimension 6: Knowledge and Learning — Peer Connection Over Research

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere)

*Knowledge as connection, not production:* FIDE's knowledge model focuses on connecting isolated practitioners rather than producing original research. Yves explains: *"Somebody working in the Polish parliament, on participation, being the single senior civil servant, there's no participation service. It's one person… will have a surplus value of being connected with the person who does this in the Bundestag"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere).

*Webinars and peer exchange:* When civil servants identify common challenges (e.g., communication with media), FIDE organises targeted webinars: *"Could we learn a bit from others? How they have connected with the media? We will set up a webinar around this"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere).

*Experimentation vs. practice:* Yves draws a sharp boundary between academic experimentation and applied practice. Experimentation belongs to the 700 academics at ECPR's Democratic Innovation Group: *"You experiment. If something works, I'll read about it. Then we'll see how we can bring this into an assembly"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere). FIDE focuses on the applied end.

*Learning through practice:* The civil servant network's design embeds peer learning: the goal is that members develop direct relationships so FIDE can step back. *"This person can then contact their peers and we could even be pulled out of the equation"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere).

### Dimension 7: Adaptation — Strategic Discipline Under Funding Contraction

**Presence: PRESENT** (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere)

*Adaptation to scarcity:* FIDE's project selection criteria function as adaptation mechanisms under resource constraint. The €350,000 budget forces clarity about what FIDE will and will not do. The litmus test — "would I do this without funding?" — prevents the slow mission drift that Yves sees as endemic in the field.

*Adaptation to mainstreaming:* The deliberative wave that FIDE was created to serve has largely arrived. Deliberative democracy is increasingly mainstreamed — but this creates new challenges: more actors, more networks, more noise. FIDE's response is not to expand but to sharpen its selectivity.

*Adaptation to funding contraction:* The departure of major funders (OSF, USAID) has shrunk the available resource pool. Yves argues the field as a whole must adapt: *"The cake is not getting bigger"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere). FIDE's adaptation is to deepen selectivity rather than broaden fundraising.

*The "speed boat" discipline:* Yves uses the metaphor of being a "speed boat" rather than following "that bow" of available money. The discipline is maintained through the interchangeability principle: FIDE is designed so that no individual's judgement — including Yves's — is irreplaceable.

### Dimension 8: Commoning

**Presence: MARGINALLY PRESENT** (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere)

*Knowledge sharing through peer network:* The civil servant network functions as a commons — practitioners share expertise on a peer-to-peer basis. FIDE provides the infrastructure but the value is collectively created.

*Below-cost investment:* FIDE's decision to deliver projects below cost (e.g., Ukraine, <€10,000 for nine weekends) represents a form of investment in the field's common infrastructure. *"If I was a for profit… I would be losing money on this. So basically we invest in those projects"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere). This is a deliberate commoning strategy: FIDE accepts financial loss to build deliberative capacity in contexts that cannot pay market rates.

*Collective expertise as commons:* FIDE's anti-oracle model means expertise is not proprietary. When advice is needed, a group of experts is convened rather than a single methodology promoted. This prevents knowledge from being captured by any single actor or approach.

### Dimension 9: Impacts

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere)

*Country-level impacts:* Ukraine (three small cities supported with deliberative processes); Bosnia (Mustar — first city-level assembly in an inter-ethnic context, working with Kyle Redman); Moldova (first assembly support). These are chosen for their "lighthouse" potential — their success or failure would significantly affect how deliberative democracy is perceived in those regions.

*Systemic impact:* The civil servant network (~40–50 members across Ireland, Belgium, Germany, Poland) aims to build enduring institutional capacity within governments. Members are strategically selected: only those in contexts where political will exists. The ultimate impact measure is FIDE's own withdrawal: *"The goal is that this person can then contact their peers and we could even be pulled out of the equation"* (FIDE, Yves Dejaeghere).

*Quality baseline:* FIDE's founding ambition — to raise the quality baseline by preventing unqualified advice from shaping national practice — is inherently difficult to measure. Yves does not claim measurable success but points to the logic: rather than a single oracle shaping a country's approach, FIDE convenes multiple leading practitioners.

*Concrete advisory:* The Paris city government reached out for process design advice; Yves pulled together experts ad hoc. This model — responsive, peer-based, light — is FIDE's primary impact mechanism.

## 3. Cross-Cutting Analytical Themes

### Theme 1: Strategic Discipline Through Scarcity

FIDE's €350,000 budget — "six bullets in my gun" — is not a constraint to be overcome but a discipline to be embraced. Every FIDE structural choice (non-membership, three-criteria triage, the litmus test, network abstinence) can be traced to the scarcity of resources relative to opportunity. Yves argues the field as a whole suffers from insufficient discipline: networking consumes more funding than practice, and the same actors appear in every project. FIDE's response is to do less, better, and to measure every engagement against the question: "Would I do this if the money weren't here?" This stands in sharp contrast to networks that measure success by growth in membership, events, or budget.

### Theme 2: The Anti-Oracle Model

Across multiple dimensions, FIDE's design rejects the concentration of expertise in a single person or methodology. The founding pitch was to convene leading actors rather than appoint a single oracle. The interchangeability principle applies to Yves himself: "Fide would also have worked if it wasn't me." The non-membership model prevents any single organisational type from dominating. The advisory board is designed for substitutability. Even the civil servant network is designed for FIDE's own obsolescence. This anti-oracle stance is a deliberate institutional immune system against the charismatic-leader model that pervades the deliberative democracy field.

### Theme 3: Ethical Boundaries as Organisational Architecture

FIDE's most distinctive feature is that its organisational form (non-membership NGO) was chosen explicitly to solve an ethical problem (conflict of interest in advisory-organisations-that-also-convene). The Volkswagen analogy is the analytical core of the interview. Most organisations design governance for efficiency, representation, or control. FIDE designed its governance for integrity — ensuring that its advice to governments could never be compromised by the commercial interests of its members. This makes FIDE a rare case of ethical considerations driving structural design rather than being bolted on afterward.

### Theme 4: The Social Economy of Refusal

Yves's revelation that "half of my job is saying no to people and justifying myself" opens a rarely examined dimension of network governance: the interpersonal cost of strategic selectivity. The deliberative democracy field runs on relationships, invitations, and collegiality. Refusing an invitation is not a neutral act — it requires repeated justification, risks reputational damage, and creates social friction. Yves acknowledges that his seniority enables him to absorb these costs where smaller organisations might not. This suggests an underexamined structural advantage in the network field: established actors can afford to be selective; emerging actors face pressure to accept everything, further straining their resources.

### Theme 5: The Interchangeability Principle

The principle runs as a thread through every dimension of FIDE's design. Governance: "you should be able to change a bit and the course of the ship wouldn't change dramatically." Leadership: "Fide would also have worked if it wasn't me." Network participation: "Nets4Dem works fine without me." Capacity building: the civil servant network aims for FIDE's own withdrawal. This is not humility — it is the most analytically developed statement in the NETS4DEM corpus of a deliberately designed principle for institutional resilience. FIDE is designed to survive its founder. Whether the principle will be tested (and whether it will hold) remains an open question.
