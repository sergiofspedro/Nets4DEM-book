## 1. Case Overview

**What OIDP is:** The International Observatory of Participatory Democracy (OIDP/IOPD) is a global network of cities, local governments, universities, NGOs, and other institutions focused on citizen participation and local democracy. Originally created as an EU-funded project in the early 2000s with leadership from the City of Barcelona, it evolved from a planned constellation of local observatories into a durable international network that organizes annual conferences and runs an annual Best Practice Award (now in its 20th edition).

**Interview source:** ITR1 — Adria Duarte Grino, Coordinator of OIDP since approximately 2013. Interview conducted June 2026 as part of the Nets4DEM book project on networking and democratic resilience.

**What the interview mainly covers:**
- The origin story: from EU project to network, including why the name "Observatory" persisted despite the abandonment of the local observatories model
- The Best Practice Award as the primary observation/monitoring mechanism ("You are an observatory thanks to the Best Practice Award")
- The individual-institution paradox: formal institutional membership vs. de facto individual-driven participation
- The relationship with UCLG (United Cities and Local Governments) — administrative integration, political benefits, EU funding limitations
- OIDP's political openness, including toward non-democratic countries (the Tehran case)
- Financial sustainability: small budget as resilience strategy; exploration and rejection of membership fees
- Key partners: People Powered, Global Democracy Coalition, SALAR, OIDP Africa
- The defensive turn: from optimism about participation to protecting democracy against authoritarian threats
- Organizational evolution: three phases (EU project → internationalization → defensive/adaptive)

**Evidence concentration:** Evidence comes from a SINGLE interview conducted by two interviewers (Giovanni and Vittoria). The interviewee was not present for the first 12-13 years of OIDP's history but has researched organizational documents and spoken with founders.

## 2. Dimension-by-Dimension Analysis

### Dimension 1: Organisational Identity, Trajectory, and Positionality

**Presence in the case: STRONGLY PRESENT**

**Detailed explanation:**

*Organisational form and self-description:* When asked whether OIDP feels more like an advocacy organization or a community of practice, Adrià answers both, noting a divergence between external communication and member experience: "We probably mentioned more the advocacy part, but we keep being of course a community of practice. And probably the people who participate, they value more the part of community of practice."

*The name paradox:* The name "Observatory" persisted despite the original model of local observatories being abandoned: "The idea of the local observatories... finished... I don't know why but they decided to keep [the name]." This represents path-dependence in organizational naming — the label outlasted the function it originally described, later acquiring new meaning through the Best Practice Award as a substitute observation mechanism.

*Temporal trajectory (three phases):*
1. **EU Project Era** (early 2000s, ~5-7 years): Funded project, focused on creating local observatories. Small community of mainly European and Latin American cities.
2. **Internationalization/Consolidation** (circa 2011 onward): OIDP Africa created. Going beyond Europe and Latin America. The award matures as a core activity. More political in orientation.
3. **Defensive/Adaptive Phase** (last ~10 years, intensified post-COVID): Responding to democratic backsliding and rising authoritarian narratives. Shift from "let's discuss what we're doing" to "how we can use participation to face authoritarian threats."

*Democratic mandate:* OIDP's political agenda is carried through conference declarations rather than a formal membership charter: "Our political agenda is the declarations. In its declarations we are for democracy, we are for human rights."

**Sub-dimensions addressed:** Organisational form, self-description, democratic mandate, reputation and perceived threats, temporal trajectory, turning points.

**Absent/marginally present:** Branding, gap between self-perception and structure.

**Interview sources:** Adrià Duarte Grino (Speaker 1), ITR1.

### Dimension 2: Membership, Composition, and Internal Inclusion

**Presence in the case: PARTIALLY PRESENT**

**Detailed explanation:**

*Membership base and openness:* Membership is exclusively institutional — municipalities, governments, universities, foundations, NGOs. Individuals cannot register as members. However, Adrià acknowledges the reality that many institutional members are effectively single individuals: "We know that many of these NGOs or associations, it's basically one person behind that."

*Inclusion of non-democratic countries:* OIDP maintains an explicitly open posture even toward cities from authoritarian regimes. The philosophy: "It's better to have one city of a non-democratic country being part of our neighborhood because we understand that... with us they're gonna be listening about our narrative, our values, and our principles." This was tested with Tehran's award nomination, which some jurors declined to evaluate on ethical grounds.

*Geographic equity — conference rotation:* "The first rule is always to change region each year" — a deliberate strategy to mitigate Eurocentrism. However, practical barriers persist: visa difficulties and travel costs disproportionately affect Global South participants. The Granollers 2022 conference created a specific fund (supported by French cities and embassies) for participants from low-income countries.

*The individual-institution tension:* A central paradox emerges. While membership is formally institutional, Adrià emphasizes that individuals are the network's lifeblood: "This network has keep working because there's persons around... it's really the people who are behind." He describes individuals as "pollinating bees" who carry institutional memory across different affiliations over time (e.g., Antonio Hernando moved from Bogotá city government to an NGO, Fernando Pindado from expert to Barcelona city official).

*Mortality and institutional memory:* When key individuals die, the institution faces a loss of continuity that formal structures don't address. The death of Ife Cabana prompted memorial publications and conference tributes, but Adrià acknowledges an asymmetry: "The institution do not die, but they often escape from the network. Persons are more loyal, more faithful, but they die."

*The award and inclusion:* The award was originally limited to local governments but now accepts nominations from civil society organizations — a change made explicitly for inclusivity, particularly for countries without institutionalized local governance: "It's a matter of inclusiveness."

**Sub-dimensions addressed:** Diversity of actor composition, membership base, openness of access, participation costs, centre-periphery inequalities, symbolic inclusion, exit/mortality.

**Absent/marginally present:** Linguistic and material barriers, dependence on large members, silent exit, self-referential discourse, structural asymmetries.

**Interview sources:** Adrià Duarte Grino (Speaker 1), with analytical framing by Giovanni (Speaker 2), ITR1.

### Dimension 3: Governance, Coordination, and Democratic Coherence

**Presence in the case: PARTIALLY PRESENT**

**Detailed explanation:**

*Governance arrangements:* OIDP operates administratively under UCLG's umbrella. Adrià coordinates both OIDP and the UCLG Committee on Participatory Democracy — a convergence that creates efficiencies ("being more efficient with the resources") but also redundancies: "Still we're doing some redundancies, but the idea is to keep both identities and both personalities."

*Decision-making on sensitive issues:* When faced with ethically contentious cases (like Tehran), decision-making is distributed rather than centralized: "Some juror... has a like ethical question about evaluating the one on Tehran, so they decide not to evaluate that." The interviewer frames this as governance through pluralism: "You solve through the pluralism... you don't have to take the decision as the secret of the institution. You let the larger community decide."

*Internal coordination across the dual structure:* The Committee and OIDP have overlapping but not identical membership: "From Barcelona, the ones that are with the committee it's the part of human rights; the observatory it's the citizen participation part. It happens in many cities."

*Concentration of power:* Barcelona's role as founding city, host, and primary funder creates a de facto concentration of influence. Adrià frames this positively as continuity: "The city of Barcelona even with a lot of political changes... has kept the political will."

**Sub-dimensions addressed:** Governance arrangements, decision-making, internal coordination, concentration of power.

**Absent/marginally present:** Conflict/competition management, informal opacity, rotation of roles, ethical charters, internal democratic coherence, coherence between internal and external methods, geographical/thematic clustering, organisational fatigue, invisible labour, brokerage, wished-for ties.

**Interview sources:** Adrià Duarte Grino (Speaker 1), Giovanni (Speaker 2), ITR1.

### Dimension 4: Resources, Formalisation, and Organisational Sustainability

**Presence in the case: STRONGLY PRESENT**

**Detailed explanation:**

*Funding structure and the resilience paradox:* OIDP operates on a small budget with a small team. Adrià frames this as a strategic advantage: "Also the good thing is that it's so small amount of money that it's probably more easy to survive." Key funders: the City of Barcelona and the EU (indirectly, via UCLG's framework agreement).

*Grant dependency and EU limitations:* The EU funding channel creates specific constraints: being under UCLG's framework agreement means OIDP "cannot participate through them to any bid... cannot sign letters in support of a project." This is a trade-off for administrative support and political visibility.

*Membership fees — explored and rejected:* OIDP explicitly considered introducing membership fees but decided against it: "We made some research and conversation with other people and we decided it was really not the good way to go."

*Alternative funding explorations:* The organization explored crowdfunding and micro-donations: "This idea of micro donations and things like that... some exploration on that." Neither was pursued.

*Formalisation through UCLG integration:* The UCLG relationship provides back-office functions (administration, HR, financial management) that a small network couldn't sustain independently: "When you are a really small network with not many resources, you need someone to take care of [administration]."

*Merger strategies:* The dual coordination role (OIDP + UCLG Committee) represents an operational merger without institutional absorption: "Reaching more with less... being more efficient."

*Survival moments:* Adrià has not faced an existential crisis during his tenure. He identifies the end of the original EU project funding as the critical historical juncture: "When the EU fund ended... at that moment it was a decision: we're gonna continue?" Barcelona's leadership made that choice.

**Sub-dimensions addressed:** Funding structure, grant dependency, membership fees, legal form (via UCLG), formalisation, organisational continuity, merging strategies.

**Absent/marginally present:** Funder pressure, dependence on large members.

**Interview sources:** Adrià Duarte Grino (Speaker 1), ITR1.

### Dimension 5: External Relations and Legitimacy

**Presence in the case: STRONGLY PRESENT**

**Detailed explanation:**

*Real (current) partnerships:*
1. **People Powered:** "The more clear one that works on the same path and doing a great job." People Powered members are always present at OIDP conferences and in the award process, though the relationship is not highly institutionalized.
2. **Global Democracy Coalition** (facilitated by International IDEA): A group focused on facing democratic backlash, where OIDP is a participant.
3. **SALAR (Swedish Association of Local Authorities and Regions):** Described as the model partner — "Probably the more clear one that we would love to replicate in many countries." SALAR has a dedicated democracy section, making collaboration easier.
4. **OIDP Africa:** Described as almost an equal rather than a subsidiary: "They have more a larger budget than we have... more resources and they do a lot of activities." Operates autonomously while sharing the OIDP brand.

*Wished-for partnerships:* Adrià expresses interest in working with large American foundations (mentioning Open Society specifically), primarily for funding but also for influence capacity: "Any of these ones... that really have this capacity to influence and have funds."

*Relation with public institutions — the UCLG value proposition:* The UCLG relationship provides OIDP with access to global institutional spaces: "Being linked to UCLG it's really a great opportunity to scaling up... to have this voice on the table at the UN, in many UN agendas, in many UN forums."

*Legitimacy and the Tehran case:* This reveals OIDP's approach to legitimacy — openness is prioritized over gatekeeping. When a non-democratic country seeks to engage, OIDP's stance is inclusionary: "We keep being open... We understand that it's better to have them... so they can be influenced." The tension is managed through distributed rather than centralized decision-making.

*International reach through the award:* The award functions as a recruitment channel for regions where OIDP has no other presence: "The ones from Philippines, from Nepal — it's gonna be impossible to get them from another world."

*Multi-scalar articulation:* OIDP operates at global (via UCLG/UN), continental (OIDP Africa), and local (member cities) scales. The integration with the UCLG Committee adds the human rights dimension, broadening OIDP's remit beyond participation.

**Sub-dimensions addressed:** Strategic partnerships, relation with public institutions, field positioning (global municipal movement), legitimacy across publics, international reach, multi-scalar articulation.

**Absent/marginally present:** Coopetition, advocacy/service provision tension.

**Interview sources:** Adrià Duarte Grino (Speaker 1), Giovanni (Speaker 2), ITR1.

*Knowledge role — the Award as intelligence tool:* The Best Practice Award is described as OIDP's primary knowledge-gathering mechanism, effectively functioning as its observation capacity. *Digital tools:* COVID forced digital transformation (webinars, online events), but this has decreased post-pandemic: "Maybe now we're not doing so much."

**Sub-dimensions addressed:** Knowledge role, evidence production, learning capacity, use of digital tools.

**Absent/marginally present:** Operating language, relation between research/communication/advocacy, tacit knowledge, approach to best practice, bad practices, intercultural translation, outreach capacity (beyond the award).

### Dimension 7: Adaptation, Resilience, and Transformative Capacity

**Presence in the case: STRONGLY PRESENT**

**Detailed explanation:**

*Structural transformation:* OIDP has undergone major structural changes: from EU project to independent network, from observatories model to award/conference model, from autonomous to UCLG-integrated, from single coordinator to dual-role coordinator (OIDP + UCLG Committee).

*The defensive turn:* The organization has pivoted from an optimistic framing ("let's discuss what we've been doing, what we can improve") to a defensive one: "How we can use participation to face the rising of authoritarian threats, how we can regain and build trust again between communities." This is contextual, driven by global political shifts rather than internal strategy.

*Strategic reorientation through convergence:* The integration with the UCLG Committee represents a broadening of OIDP's agenda to include human rights: "The opportunity to keep the agenda of human rights present in the observatory."

*Alliance reconfiguration:* OIDP's partnership web creates resilience through diversification — People Powered, Global Democracy Coalition, national local government associations, and OIDP Africa as autonomous continental node.

*Financial resilience as paradox:* "We're small, so we can easily survive." Resource scarcity is reframed as strategic advantage.

*COVID adaptation:* The pandemic forced online modalities; the year without an in-person conference was "a bit of a challenge" but also "a consolidation."

**Sub-dimensions addressed:** Changes across major dimensions, responses to backsliding, strategic reorientation, alliance reconfiguration, future pathways.

**Absent/marginally present:** None — well-covered.

## 3. Cross-Cutting Analytical Themes

### Theme 1: The Individual-Institution Paradox

**Where it appears:** Dimensions 2 (Membership), 3 (Governance), 8 (Commoning)

**Dimensions connected:** Membership & Inclusion, Governance & Coordination, Commoning.

**Analytical significance:** OIDP is formally an institution-of-institutions, yet operates as a network-of-individuals. This paradox runs through membership (only institutions, but effectively individuals), governance (individuals "pollinate" across cities), and institutional memory (individuals die, institutions survive but lose continuity). The network persists through personal relationships while simultaneously maintaining institutional form. This may be a defining feature of "second-order networks" — networks whose members are themselves institutional, yet whose vitality depends on embodied personal relationships that the formal structure does not recognize.

### Theme 2: Resilience Through Smallness

**Where it appears:** Dimensions 4 (Resources), 7 (Adaptation)

**Dimensions connected:** Resources & Sustainability, Adaptation & Resilience.

**Analytical significance:** OIDP turns resource constraints into a strategic narrative — small budget equals easier survival. This "lean resilience" discourse may be a coping mechanism or a genuine competitive advantage. It shapes organizational choices: membership fees are rejected (barrier to entry), administrative functions are outsourced to UCLG (economics of scale), and growth ambitions are tempered. This pattern warrants comparison with larger, better-funded networks that may face different survival pressures.

### Theme 3: The Award as Substitute Observatory

**Where it appears:** Dimensions 1 (Identity), 6 (Knowledge)

**Dimensions connected:** Organisational Identity, Knowledge & Learning.

**Analytical significance:** The persistence of the "Observatory" name, despite the abandonment of actual observatory structures, is justified by the award functioning as a de facto observation mechanism. This suggests organizational naming can become decoupled from function while remaining symbolically coherent through substitute practices. The award accomplishes what surveys and direct outreach could not.

### Theme 4: Openness as Strategy and Vulnerability

**Where it appears:** Dimensions 2 (Membership), 5 (External Relations)

**Dimensions connected:** Membership & Inclusion, External Relations & Legitimacy.

**Analytical significance:** OIDP's policy of including non-democratic regimes reflects a calculated bet: engagement is better than exclusion. But this creates ethical tensions (Tehran) that are resolved through distributed decision-making (juror discretion) rather than institutional policy. This "managed openness" avoids the rigidity of a formal values charter but relies on the judgment of individual community members.

### Theme 5: The Defensive Turn

**Where it appears:** Dimensions 1 (Identity), 5 (External), 7 (Adaptation)

**Dimensions connected:** Organisational Identity, External Relations, Adaptation.

**Analytical significance:** The shift from an optimistic "let's improve participation" to a defensive "let's protect democracy" represents a reorientation driven by external political context. This mirrors broader trends in the democracy promotion field and may be a shared pattern across all networks in the Nets4DEM corpus.
