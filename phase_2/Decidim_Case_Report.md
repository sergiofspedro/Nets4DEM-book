## 1. Case Overview

**What Decidim is:** Decidim (from the Catalan "we decide") is both a free, open-source digital participation platform and the community ecosystem surrounding it. The platform was born from the 15M movement and Barcelona en Comú's municipalist project, first deployed by Barcelona City Council before scaling globally. Today it has approximately 3 million users across hundreds of installations — the largest being Brasil Participativo (1.5 million users). MetaDecidim is the community layer: a space where developers, governments, and enterprises collaborate on the platform's roadmap, governance, and development.

**Central sources:** Seven interviews provide a multi-perspective view. Joel Berenguer (IPO11) offers the OSP/coordination perspective. Carla Bezerra (IPO4) provides the perspective of the largest installation (Brazil's federal government). Giulia Bertone (IPO8) offers the view of a small Italian enterprise implementing Decidim for municipalities. Additional interviews contribute specialist perspectives on Decidim's use in France (IPO9), Spain (IPO10), and Catalonia (IPO17).

**What the case is mainly about:** Decidim represents a distinctive case — a political-technical network built around a shared software infrastructure. Unlike the other organisations in the corpus, its centre of gravity is technological: the platform itself. The community that forms around it (MetaDecidim) is predominantly a developer community, creating a structural gap between those who shape the tool (developers) and those who use it (governments, citizens). This gap — between the democratic values the platform enables and the governance of the platform itself — is the central tension of the case.

## 2. Dimension-by-Dimension Analysis

### Dimension 1: Organisational Identity — Politically Forged, Technologically Mediated

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IPO11, Berenguer; IPO4, Bezerra; IPO8, Bertone; IPO9, Saunier)

*Political origins:* Decidim was "politically forged" by the 15M movement in Spain and the municipalist project of Barcelona en Comú. Unlike neutral software tools, Decidim embodies political values: "the way they see technology as a non-neutral, not as neutral tools — the way you build the technology, it influences the politics" (IPO4, Bezerra). This political DNA differentiates Decidim from commercial participation platforms and shapes everything from its open-source licensing to its community governance model.

*Platform + community = identity:* Decidim is simultaneously a technological artefact and a human community. Joel Berenguer describes the dual nature: the platform as infrastructure and MetaDecidim as the governance layer. This duality creates the central organisational challenge: the most active participants in MetaDecidim are developers and enterprises, not the governments and citizens who use the platform.

*Catalan-centric reality:* Multiple interviewees describe Decidim as inherently Catalan-centric. Giulia Bertone agrees: "I do agree that Decidim is very much Catalan-centric — they're still based there, most of their financing are there, most of the stronger processes are there." The political and technical leadership remains in Barcelona, creating a structural centre-periphery dynamic despite the platform's global spread.

*The Decidim Fest as community anchor:* The annual Decidim Fest in Barcelona (300 participants) serves as the most tangible expression of community. Giulia Bertone, who attended with initial anxiety about entering an insular group, found "great openness, even to new people — a great willingness and welcome." The Fest combines inspiration, networking, and capacity-building — but remains Barcelona-centred.

### Dimension 2: Membership — A Developer Community with a Government Gap

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IPO4, Bezerra; IPO11, Berenguer; IPO8, Bertone)

*MetaDecidim's composition:* Carla Bezerra is blunt: "To be part of MetaDecidim, you can be both as an individual or as an enterprise that is a developer. People who participate actively in Decidim forums — they are developers. It's not a community of practitioners. It's not a community of governments. It's not a community of NGOs. Or it's a community of developers" (IPO4, Bezerra). The most active members are "enterprises that are specialised into doing services for government."

*The Government Council — consultative only:* A council of governments using Decidim was created at Brazil's proposal. It includes Barcelona, Generalitat de Catalunya, the Brazilian government, and a few others. But Bezerra notes: "It's still something like more consultative, more like a political space, very general. It doesn't influence directly into the MetaDecidim discussions or communities."

*The enterprises as de facto members:* The network of enterprises implementing Decidim for governments — PARES (Italy, IPO8), PokeCode (Spain, IPO10), OCTREE (France, IPO9), OSP (international, IPO11) — constitute a parallel membership structure. These enterprises depend on Decidim for their business model and contribute to its development, but their participation is primarily technical rather than political.

*Individual practitioners and the time poverty barrier:* Giulia Bertone, who follows MetaDecidim but cannot actively participate, represents a common profile: "I follow, I see. But unfortunately I haven't had time to actively participate yet." The barrier is not complexity ("very little was needed") but bandwidth. Enterprises implementing Decidim for clients are precisely the organisations with the least time to contribute to the community's governance.

*Bezerra's post-government relationship:* After leaving the Brazilian government, Bezerra describes her relationship as: "I do have my sympathy for Decidim and I will meet people and do have some personal connections. But I'm not participating actively in the forums." The institutional tie (government) was severed, and the personal tie (individual membership) is weaker than in People Powered.

### Dimension 3: Governance — The Developer-Governed Commons

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IPO4, Bezerra; IPO11, Berenguer)

*MetaDecidim governance:* The community governs the platform's development roadmap through a participatory process on the MetaDecidim platform — proposals are submitted, debated, and prioritised. This is an internal application of participatory methods to the platform's own development.

*Who governs:* "The more active ones are the enterprises that are specialised into doing services for government" (IPO4, Bezerra). Developers and enterprises shape the roadmap; governments and citizens who use the platform do not. This creates a governance gap: the voices that determine what the platform becomes are not the voices that experience its limitations in practice.

*The platform as commons, governed by developers:* Decidim is a commons — freely available, collectively developed, shared by all. But the governance of this commons is concentrated among those with technical expertise. The commoning of the software has outpaced the commoning of its governance.

*The Catalan centre and global periphery:* Governance reflects the organisation's geography: the decisions that shape the platform for users in Brazil, Africa, and Asia are made primarily in Barcelona. Brazil's massive installation (half of all Decidim users) developed its own adaptations (mobile-first, simplified interface, WhatsApp API) because the Barcelona-centred development process did not prioritise those needs.

### Dimension 4: Resources — Barcelona Dependency and Diversification Attempts

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IPO4, Bezerra; IPO11, Berenguer; IPO8, Bertone)

*Barcelona as primary funder:* Decidim "still had more than 50 percent dependent on the Ayuntamiento de Barcelona" (IPO4, Bezerra). The platform survived the government change from Barcelona en Comú to PSOE — a significant resilience test. But the dependency on a single city government creates structural vulnerability.

*Diversification efforts — and limitations:* "They have been trying very much to diversify. But they were not experts into applying to different funding in different sources. They still relied very much in government" (IPO4, Bezerra). Unlike People Powered, which aggressively diversified from American to European funding, Decidim's diversification is slower and more constrained by its political identity.

*The enterprise ecosystem as distributed resource model:* A distinctive feature of the Decidim ecosystem: enterprises implement and maintain installations for governments, creating a distributed funding model where the platform itself is free but implementation is paid. This creates a self-sustaining ecosystem — enterprises have an economic incentive to maintain and develop the platform. But it also means that the enterprises, not the users, have the greatest stake in the platform's health.

*Brazil's self-funded development:* Brazil developed its adaptations independently: "We had the money to do it by ourselves" (IPO4, Bezerra). This autonomy was necessary because Barcelona-centred development did not prioritise Brazil's needs, but it also created technical debt — the WhatsApp integration overrode core code rather than following proper plugin architecture. The adaptations still need rewriting to be contributed back to the community.

*The multi-tenant opportunity:* In Catalonia, the AOC (Open Consortium of Catalonia) provides multi-tenant Decidim to municipalities. In Emilia-Romagna, Italy, this has not happened despite "pushing for it several times" (IPO8, Bertone). The multi-tenant model — shared infrastructure, local autonomy — represents an untapped scaling mechanism. A municipality called PARES asking "When is it coming?" because they needed the functionality.

### Dimension 5: External Relations — Political Positioning and Partnership Risks

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IPO4, Bezerra; IPO11, Berenguer)

*Political positioning as identity:* Decidim took a public position on Palestine and "lost some partners" as a result (the interviewer's framing, confirmed by Bezerra). This is structurally consistent with Decidim's identity — a politically defined organisation cannot remain neutral on issues that its values demand it address. The contrast with People Powered (which refused any position) illustrates how political DNA shapes external relations.

*Relationship with other networks:* Decidim has informal connections with OIDP, OGP, and People Powered, but no formal partnerships. The "Decidim Directors Group" facilitates coordination among governments using the platform. The platform's relationship with these networks is mediated by individual practitioners (Bezerra in Brazil, Bertone in Italy) rather than through institutional agreements.

*The Swiss chapter — national autonomy as funding requirement:* Oktri, the Swiss Decidim implementer working in Tanzania, Mozambique, and Mali, founded the first national Decidim chapter "after many years of rejection" (interviewer). The rationale was practical: Swiss funding cannot flow to a Catalan organisation, so legal autonomy was necessary. This required an agreement reassuring Decidim that the Swiss chapter was not "fleeing." National chapters represent a governance adaptation driven by funding necessity rather than strategic design.

### Dimension 6: Knowledge — The Tool Embodies Knowledge, Not the Community

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IPO4, Bezerra; IPO8, Bertone; IPO11, Berenguer)

*The platform as knowledge infrastructure:* Decidim's most significant knowledge contribution is the platform itself — a codified expression of participatory democracy principles in technological form. The platform embodies knowledge about how participation should work: transparent, traceable, modular, and open.

*Documentation and methodology:* The platform includes methodologies for participatory processes (assemblies, consultations, participatory budgets) embedded in its modules. This is prescriptive knowledge — the tool suggests how participation should be done.

*Limitation — knowledge as developer expertise:* The knowledge that matters most in the Decidim ecosystem is technical knowledge — how to install, configure, develop, and maintain the platform. The broader knowledge about what makes participation effective is secondary. This reverses People Powered's knowledge orientation (participatory methodology expertise first, tools second).

*The observatory gap:* Giulia Bertone describes the Emilia-Romagna region's use of Decidim as primarily "a transparency operation" rather than a participatory one. The platform's knowledge — what participation looks like — is not being used. The knowledge is in the tool; the participatory culture is absent.

*Cross-cultural knowledge exchange:* The Brasil Participativo experience generated knowledge about adapting a European-built, desktop-oriented platform for a mobile-first, high-inequality context. This knowledge — about simplifying interfaces, reducing information density, designing for low-bandwidth users — is relevant for Africa and other Global South contexts but has not been systematically fed back into the community.

### Dimension 7: Adaptation — From Barcelona to Brazil and Beyond

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IPO4, Bezerra; IPO11, Berenguer; IPO8, Bertone)

*Brazil as adaptation engine:* Brasil Participativo — the largest Decidim installation — forced adaptations that the Barcelona-centred development process had not prioritised: mobile-first design, simplified interface, WhatsApp integration, and multi-tenant support for municipalities. These adaptations were developed independently and need to be contributed back as proper plugins.

*The Swiss chapter as governance adaptation:* The creation of a national chapter in Switzerland represents a governance adaptation — accommodating the reality that funding must flow through national legal entities. But it required negotiation and reassurance, revealing the tension between centralised control and the practical necessities of global expansion.

*The Italian multi-tenant gap:* Giulia Bertone's frustration — the Emilia-Romagna region has Decidim, has the need, has the multi-tenant capability, but lacks the political will — illustrates a different adaptation challenge. The technological adaptation exists; the political and cultural adaptation does not.

*AI as emerging challenge:* Decidim, like IAF, has not formally addressed AI. But AI tools integrated into the platform could transform both how participation is designed and how the platform is maintained. The Open Source community's relationship with AI — particularly around code generation and moderation — will shape Decidim's next adaptation.

### Dimension 8: Commoning

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IPO4, Bezerra; IPO11, Berenguer; IPO8, Bertone)

*The platform as commons:* Decidim represents the purest commons in the corpus — a shared digital infrastructure, freely available, collectively developed, and governed (in principle) by its community. The Open Source license operationalises the commons: anyone can use, modify, and distribute the platform.

*The commons-governance gap:* The commons of the platform is more developed than the commons of its governance. The developers who contribute to the platform's code shape its direction; the governments and citizens who use it do not. "The idea of a commons, when you use them, you have to give back to the community. There is some sort of flow of interactions" (interviewer). Brazil's adaptations — developed independently, not yet contributed back as proper plugins — are a commoning debt: the largest user has benefited from the commons but has not fully reciprocated.

*The enterprise commons:* The ecosystem of enterprises implementing Decidim constitutes an economic commons — a shared market created by the platform's existence. These enterprises benefit from the platform's reputation, community, and continuous development. Their economic stake aligns them with the platform's health, but their profit motive can conflict with the platform's political values.

*Parma as commoning in practice:* Decidim transformed Parma's neighbourhood workshops from informal, ad-hoc communication between citizens and councillors into a structured, transparent, and accountable process. Giulia Bertone: "I never expected it to work this way. It has contributed not only to transparency, but also to ordering communication flows." The platform created a public space where proposals, comments, and outcomes are visible — a commons of civic communication that did not exist before.

### Dimension 9: Impacts

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IPO4, Bezerra; IPO8, Bertone; IPO11, Berenguer)

*Scale:* 3 million users globally. Brasil Participativo alone: 1.5 million users. The platform has enabled participatory processes at a scale that would be impossible without digital infrastructure.

*Democratic practice:* Beyond the numbers, Decidim has enabled specific democratic innovations: participatory budgeting at national scale in Brazil, neighbourhood workshop transformation in Parma, multi-channel participation in Catalonia through AOC's multi-tenant model.

*Field-level — the Open Source alternative:* Decidim has established an ethical alternative to commercial participation platforms. Governments that use Decidim are asserting a political choice — that democratic infrastructure should be publicly owned, transparent, and collectively governed — rather than a purely technical one.

*Limitation — the participatory culture gap:* The platform enables participation but does not create it. Giulia Bertone: "If you imagine and understand participation only as a questionnaire about a policy you're developing, you don't even feel the need to look for tools." The platform's sophistication exceeds the participatory culture of many of the institutions that adopt it. The result is underutilisation — "we have these thousand functions and they use thirty of them" (interviewer's frustration).

*Limitation — the Global South adaptation gap:* The platform was built by "welfare state Europeans that do not understand the big inequalities and the big challenges of accessing and using the internet" (IPO4, Bezerra). Brazil adapted it, but the adaptations have not been fully contributed back. Africa faces even greater adaptation challenges — WhatsApp integration, low bandwidth, expensive internet — that the current development roadmap does not prioritise.

## 3. Cross-Cutting Analytical Themes

### Theme 1: The Tool-Community Gap

Decidim's most fundamental organisational tension is the gap between the tool and the community. The tool is globally distributed, democratically enabling, and technologically sophisticated. The community that governs the tool is Barcelona-centred, developer-dominated, and technically specialised. Governments and citizens who use the platform have limited voice in what it becomes. This gap — between democratic values embedded in the tool and the democratic governance of the tool itself — is the unresolved tension at Decidim's core.

### Theme 2: The Catalan Centre and the Global Periphery

Decidim's Catalan-centricity is both its strength (political coherence, institutional stability, the Barcelona City Council's sustained financial commitment) and its limitation (reduced responsiveness to Global South needs, governance concentration, cultural distance from non-Catalan users). The Swiss chapter, Brazil's independent development, and the Italian multi-tenant gap all reflect the same structural dynamic: global users working around the Catalan centre rather than being served by it.

### Theme 3: The Platform as Political Choice

Decidim is not just a tool — it is a political statement. Governments that adopt it are choosing open-source, collectively-governed, transparent infrastructure over commercial alternatives. This political dimension attracts committed users but also constrains: Decidim's political identity makes neutrality impossible, and taking positions (like on Palestine) can cost partnerships. The platform's political DNA is inseparable from its technical DNA — a lesson for the broader democratic technology field.
