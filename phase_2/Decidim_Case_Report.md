# DECIDIM and METADECIDIM — Digital Platform and Developer Community
## Analytical Case Report — Phase 2 (Nets4DEM)

> **Sources:** IFP2 — Arnau (Barcelona City Council, co-founder), IPO11 — Joel Berenguer (Decidim/OSP), IPO4 — Carla Bezerra (Brasil Participativo), IPO8 — Giulia Bertone (PARES, Italy), IPO9 — Guillaume Saunier (OCTREE, France), IPO10 — Ivan Verges (PokeCode), IPO3 — Aram Lopez (Populate), IPO17 (Ruben Jerico — AOC/Catalonia; ITR5 Roque & IPO17 Vinicius — FGV/Brazil). Eight interviews, June–July 2026. Arnau (IFP2) provides the co-founder/insider perspective, the foundational three-layer framework, and the governance detail from within the Barcelona-centred political project.

---

## 1. Case Overview

**What Decidim is:** Decidim (from the Catalan "we decide") is both a free, open-source digital participation platform and the community ecosystem surrounding it. The platform was born from the confluence of three genealogical streams: the 15M/indignados movements of 2011, the free software movement in Spain (particularly Barcelona), and a "collective need of democracies" (IFP2, Arnau). First deployed by Barcelona City Council in 2016, it spread through an "oil slick effect" — without any international extension department, cities worldwide adopted it organically, Helsinki "almost automatically without us knowing" (IFP2, Arnau). Today it has approximately 3 million users across hundreds of installations, the largest being Brasil Participativo (1.5 million users).

**Foundational architecture:** Unlike the other cases in the corpus, Decidim is analytically structured around a consciously designed **three-layer model**: the political layer (articulating democratic processes), the technical layer (free/open-source, transparent, auditable code rooted in hacker ethics), and the technopolitical layer (democratic governance of the community itself). Arnau frames this as a foundational principle: "a platform for democracy, its governance obviously had to be democratic — if not, it was a contradiction or an oxymoron" (IFP2, Arnau). The three layers operate simultaneously as an analytical framework, a design principle, and a permanent arena of productive tension.

**Central sources:** Eight interviews provide multi-perspective coverage. Arnau (IFP2), a co-founder working at Barcelona City Council, provides the insider perspective on the project's architecture, governance evolution, political positioning, and internal tensions. Joel Berenguer (IPO11) offers the OSP/coordination perspective. Carla Bezerra (IPO4) provides the perspective of the largest installation (Brazil's federal government). Giulia Bertone (IPO8) offers the view of a small Italian enterprise implementing Decidim. Additional interviews contribute specialist perspectives on Decidim's use in France (IPO9), Spain (IPO10), Catalonia (IPO17), and Brazil's FGV (ITR5).

**What the case is mainly about:** Decidim represents a distinctive case — a political-technical network built around a shared software infrastructure. Unlike the other organisations in the corpus, its centre of gravity is technological: the platform itself. The community that forms around it (MetaDecidim) is predominantly a developer community, creating a structural gap between those who shape the tool (developers) and those who use it (governments, citizens). This gap — between the democratic values the platform enables and the governance of the platform itself — is the central tension of the case. Arnau's insider account reveals how this tension is managed through explicit governance design: the three-layer model, the social contract as internal constitution, the "rule of 3" for feature inclusion, and a deliberate anti-compete principle that prevents the association from becoming a service provider.

## 2. Dimension-by-Dimension Analysis

### Dimension 1: Organisational Identity — The Three-Layer Model as DNA

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP2, Arnau; IPO11, Berenguer; IPO4, Bezerra; IPO8, Bertone; IPO9, Saunier)

*Political origins and genealogical DNA:* Decidim's identity draws on three genealogical streams: the 15M/indignados/Occupy mobilizations, the free software movement (particularly in Barcelona), and a broader "collective need of democracies" (IFP2, Arnau). These are "inserted almost in the DNA of the community" (IFP2, Arnau). The platform was "politically forged" (IPO4, Bezerra) — "the way you build the technology, it influences the politics" (IPO4, Bezerra). Unlike neutral software tools, Decidim embodies political values from its first line of code.

*The three-layer framework:* Arnau provides the foundational analytical framework that no other interview articulates so clearly. Decidim's identity is structured around three interdependent layers. **The political layer** concerns the platform's object — articulating democratic processes. **The technical layer** governs the technological conditions: free/open-source code, transparent and auditable, copyable and modifiable, rooted in hacker ethics. **The technopolitical layer** concerns the democratic governance of the community itself. This was a deliberate foundational decision: "a platform for democracy, its governance obviously had to be democratic — if not, it was a contradiction or an oxymoron" (IFP2, Arnau).

The layers are operational, not merely descriptive. Open code enables trust; trust enables adoption; adoption extends the community; the community improves the code. The technopolitical layer prevents capture by any single actor. But the layers also conflict — when the political layer is "very excited" about federation protocols while the technical layer is "afraid" of the technological challenge (IFP2, Arnau) — and Arnau frames this conflict as productive rather than pathological.

*Platform + community = dual identity:* Decidim is simultaneously a technological artefact and a human community. Joel Berenguer describes the dual nature: the platform as infrastructure and MetaDecidim as the governance layer. This duality creates the central organisational challenge: the most active participants in MetaDecidim are developers and enterprises, not the governments and citizens who use the platform.

*Catalan-centric reality:* Multiple interviewees describe Decidim as inherently Catalan-centric. Giulia Bertone: "I do agree that Decidim is very much Catalan-centric — they're still based there, most of their financing are there, most of the stronger processes are there." The political and technical leadership remains in Barcelona, creating a structural centre-periphery dynamic despite the platform's global spread. Arnau himself, as a Barcelona-based co-founder, embodies this reality — though he is conscious of it and designed the Metadecidim association precisely to reduce dependency on Barcelona.

*Oil slick diffusion without strategy:* Decidim spread through an "oil slick effect" (IFP2, Arnau) — organic, demand-driven growth without any international extension department. Helsinki adopted the platform "almost automatically without us knowing" (IFP2, Arnau). The Barcelona Provincial Council, French cities, and Italian municipalities all approached Decidim. This semi-invisible virality was enabled by the open code itself: "the code is there and anyone can copy and replicate it" (IFP2, Arnau). Code adoption becomes "the excuse to make the link for where to start weaving a community" (IFP2, Arnau).

*Decidim Fest as community anchor:* The annual Decidim Fest in Barcelona (now in its sixth edition, 2026) serves as the most tangible expression of community. Giulia Bertone, who attended with initial anxiety about entering an insular group, found "great openness, even to new people — a great willingness and welcome." The Fest combines inspiration, networking, and capacity-building — but remains Barcelona-centred.

### Dimension 2: Membership — Developer Community, Government Gap, Diversity Challenges

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP2, Arnau; IPO4, Bezerra; IPO11, Berenguer; IPO8, Bertone)

*Developer dominance:* Carla Bezerra is blunt: "To be part of MetaDecidim, you can be both as an individual or as an enterprise that is a developer. People who participate actively in Decidim forums — they are developers. It's not a community of practitioners. It's not a community of governments. It's not a community of NGOs. Or it's a community of developers" (IPO4, Bezerra). The most active members are "enterprises that are specialised into doing services for government."

*The concentric circles of community:* Arnau provides a more formal governance picture. The Metadecidim association has structured the community into concentric circles: at the centre, a technical secretariat (product, communication, strategic management); alongside it, a coordination committee of eight elected by community vote; then partners (fee-paying, higher involvement); then the broader community (anyone who contributes); then service providers (companies with official partnership agreements financing the association in exchange for visibility); and finally governments, with collaboration agreements and a "committee of cities and institutions" (IFP2, Arnau).

*The Government Council — consultative only:* A council of governments using Decidim was created at Brazil's proposal. It includes Barcelona, Generalitat de Catalunya, the Brazilian government, and a few others. But Bezerra notes: "It's still something like more consultative, more like a political space, very general. It doesn't influence directly into the MetaDecidim discussions or communities" (IPO4, Bezerra).

*Enterprises as de facto members:* The network of enterprises implementing Decidim — PARES (Italy), PokeCode (Spain), OCTREE (France), OSP (international), Populate (Catalonia) — constitute a parallel membership structure. They depend on Decidim for their business model but their participation is primarily technical. This creates a structural tension that Arnau explicitly names: companies become "defenders of the interests of the customer in front of the community" (IFP2, Arnau), and the community must distinguish between "my need" and "the commons' need" through mechanisms like the "rule of 3" (see Dimension 3).

*Time poverty barrier for practitioners:* Giulia Bertone: "I follow, I see. But unfortunately I haven't had time to actively participate yet" (IPO8, Bertone). Enterprises implementing Decidim for clients are precisely the organisations with the least time for community governance.

*Gender and Global South inclusion — an unfinished project:* Arnau provides the first detailed account of Decidim's diversity efforts, entirely absent from the other interviews. Two editions of a Ruby training program for women developers were run, with some graduates incorporated into the project. The technical office prioritizes hiring women and avoiding "European, white, middle-class" homogeneity (IFP2, Arnau). The coordination committee has gender parity.

But the deeper challenge — framed as "design justice" — is structural: "how you incorporate into the community in a proactive way this look, if you want, of colonial, of the subglobal in the design process" (IFP2, Arnau). This is done "in a very artisan way" (IFP2, Arnau) — when working with Brazilian or African partners — not through systemic mechanisms. European companies "end up having a very important role" in development discussions. Arnau identifies an additional tension: "when the technopoliticians come, the community sometimes generates a certain rejection because the technical does not always fit with the political" (IFP2, Arnau). The meritocratic culture and the political-inclusion culture sometimes clash.

*Post-government relationship:* After leaving the Brazilian government, Bezerra describes her relationship as: "I do have my sympathy for Decidim and I will meet people and do have some personal connections. But I'm not participating actively in the forums" (IPO4, Bezerra). The institutional tie was severed, and the personal tie is weaker than in People Powered.

### Dimension 3: Governance — The Social Contract and the Architecture of Decision

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP2, Arnau; IPO4, Bezerra; IPO11, Berenguer)

*MetaDecidim governance:* The community governs the platform's development roadmap through a participatory process on the MetaDecidim platform — proposals are submitted, debated, and prioritised. This is an internal application of participatory methods to the platform's own development.

*Who governs — the developer-meritocracy tension:* "The more active ones are the enterprises that are specialised into doing services for government" (IPO4, Bezerra). Arnau adds nuance: the open source world operates on a meritocratic ethic — "whoever contributes and does it with greater quality has greater karma and has a greater capacity to have recognition" (IFP2, Arnau). But Decidim bends this dynamic. "The voice of the community that thinks about how to improve democratic processes has had more weight" (IFP2, Arnau). This is a distinctive adaptation of the standard open source governance model.

*The "Rule of 3" — distinguishing shared from particular needs:* Arnau introduces a specific governance mechanism absent from all other interviews. When companies propose new features driven by customer demands, the community applies the "rule of 3": a need must be shared by at least three platforms to warrant core inclusion. Otherwise, Decidim's modular architecture allows anyone to build modules outside the core. Even Barcelona, the founding city, has features kept outside the main repository: "Barcelona understood perfectly well that this was not a shared need" (IFP2, Arnau). This creates a "productive tension" — "between my need, as a company, as an individual, as a person, and the need of the commons" (IFP2, Arnau) — that is "permanently negotiated" across dimensions of sustainability, architecture, and shared need.

Arnau is critical of the core's defenders as "somewhat conservative" — sometimes "strictly too technical, issues of architecture and sustainability, and not so much political" (IFP2, Arnau). Technical conservatism can impede political innovation.

*The Social Contract as internal constitution:* Beyond the licensing and the formal governance, Decidim has a "social contract" — an instrument validated by the community that functions as an internal constitution. Arnau describes two cases of the social contract in action. The Chile case: Piñera's right-wing government contacted Decidim to use the platform for a constitutional debate process. "A part of the community said no to our name" (IFP2, Arnau). After intense debate, the community issued a statement: "Decidim is not good for anything if there is no real participation — we reject the use of Decidim" (IFP2, Arnau). The social contract "serves us to tell any government or institution that uses Decidim that not only the license has to be complied with, but the social contract has to be fulfilled" (IFP2, Arnau).

A parallel capacity was exercised on Palestine — Decidim took a collective position, and "lost some partners" as a result (interviewer, confirmed by Bezerra). Giovanni notes Decidim is "one of the few networks where the network has taken a stand" — refusing to let individuals use the community space for partisan positions while asserting collective political voice.

The social contract is now under pressure to evolve, particularly concerning AI: "a statement has to be prepared on how AI is going to be used... not just any AI is worth" (IFP2, Arnau).

*The Metadecidim association as institutional safeguard:* The 2019 constitution of the Metadecidim association was driven by a structural insight: "the project could not have the exclusive anchorage of Barcelona. A change of government could threaten everything" (IFP2, Arnau). The association was modelled on Wikipedia, Mozilla, and Drupal. Its governance architecture is consciously layered: a technical secretariat, an elected coordination committee of eight, a transparent participation process on Metadecidim (monthly community meetings, working groups on usability, development, and AI research), and a "committee of cities and institutions."

A critical design choice distinguishes Decidim from many other networks: "the association itself is not a service provider, it does not compete with the companies that provide services" (IFP2, Arnau). Instead, it does "code curatorship, research, consulting — things that only the community can do" (IFP2, Arnau). This anti-compete principle preserves the ecosystem's health.

*Founder interchangeability:* When asked about "primogeniture" — whether the founding group's weight endures — Arnau notes that Xavier and Antonio were elected to the coordination committee (not there by right), and that Carol (product) and Andrés (architecture) have more visibility because they do the daily technical work. The founders do not have automatic authority: "in the community we do not have this authority that founders of a project can have" (IFP2, Arnau). Technical contribution, not founding status, determines influence.

### Dimension 4: Resources — Barcelona Dependency and Diversification

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IPO4, Bezerra; IPO11, Berenguer; IPO8, Bertone; IFP2, Arnau)

*Barcelona as primary funder:* Decidim "still had more than 50 percent dependent on the Ayuntamiento de Barcelona" (IPO4, Bezerra). The platform survived the government change from Barcelona en Comú to PSOE — a significant resilience test. But dependency on a single city creates structural vulnerability.

*Diversification efforts — and limitations:* "They have been trying very much to diversify. But they were not experts into applying to different funding in different sources. They still relied very much in government" (IPO4, Bezerra). Unlike People Powered, which aggressively diversified from American to European funding, Decidim's diversification is slower and more constrained by its political identity.

*Enterprise ecosystem as distributed resource model:* A distinctive feature: enterprises implement and maintain installations for governments, creating a distributed funding model where the platform is free but implementation is paid. This creates a self-sustaining ecosystem where enterprises have an economic incentive to maintain and develop the platform. Arnau's framing of the association as a non-compete entity clarifies the boundary: the association does not seek the implementation revenue that sustains the enterprises.

*Partner fees and collaboration agreements:* Arnau adds detail on the association's revenue model: symbolic partner fees from individuals, official partnership agreements with companies that finance the association in exchange for visibility, and government collaboration agreements (e.g., New York City for participatory budgets). These are all elements of a deliberate strategy to diversify beyond Barcelona while maintaining political coherence.

*Brazil's self-funded development:* Brazil developed its adaptations independently: "We had the money to do it by ourselves" (IPO4, Bezerra). This autonomy was necessary because Barcelona-centred development did not prioritise Brazil's needs, but it created technical debt — the WhatsApp integration overrode core code rather than following proper plugin architecture.

*The multi-tenant opportunity:* In Catalonia, the AOC provides multi-tenant Decidim to municipalities. In Emilia-Romagna, this has not happened despite "pushing for it several times" (IPO8, Bertone).

### Dimension 5: External Relations — Political Positioning, Consul, Big Tech, Local Chapters

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP2, Arnau; IPO4, Bezerra; IPO11, Berenguer)

*Political positioning as identity:* Decidim's political DNA makes neutrality impossible. The social contract functions as both an internal governance instrument and an external positioning tool. Decidim lost partners over its Palestine position. Arnau's detailed account of the Chile case (Piñera rejection) shows the social contract in operation: the community collectively decided that instrumental use by a hostile government violated the project's principles, and issued a public statement.

*Participatory washing:* Arnau introduces the concept of "participatory washing" — the attempt to use technology as a "magic solution to a problem that is not real" (IFP2, Arnau). This is the first warning Decidim gives any organization: "if there is no political commitment, it doesn't work" (IFP2, Arnau). This positions Decidim's external relations as ethically conditional: not every government or institution is an appropriate partner.

*Consul as "first cousin":* Arnau views Consul (the other major open source participation platform, from which Decidim originally forked) with notable generosity: "it is a first cousin because it follows many of our principles, it is open source — so we cannot reproach it for anything" (IFP2, Arnau). Initial collaboration attempts failed for "more technical than political reasons" (IFP2, Arnau), but he sees shared DNA and potential for future collaboration. This contrasts with the competitive framing of for-profit platforms like GoVocal/Osprey (see Theme 6 below).

*Local chapters — governance as non-negotiable:* The Swiss chapter (Oktri, working in Tanzania, Mozambique, Mali) required "many years of rejection" before negotiation produced an agreement — Swiss funding cannot flow to a Catalan organisation, so legal autonomy was necessary. Arnau welcomes local chapters as territorial extension but with a non-negotiable condition: "the form of governance has to be untouchable. If we end up reproducing local chapters that subvert the democratic logic of the project, this is the risk" (IFP2, Arnau). The vision is distributed, not centralized.

*Big Tech pragmatism:* The relationship with Big Tech is modulated pragmatism rather than ideological purity. Arnau views WhatsApp integration as acceptable if it extends participation and uses open protocols: "a priori, I don't see it as bad if it can extend the levels of participation, taking into account that everyone uses WhatsApp" (IFP2, Arnau). The balancing principle: "when you have to give security guarantees, you also have to measure accessibility — if you make a very robust system but no one can vote, then you have failed" (IFP2, Arnau). Giovanni connects this to the classic commons dilemma: "radical purists say you don't talk to the market, and others say you have to maintain an open dialogue."

*Relationships with other networks:* Decidim has informal connections with OIDP, OGP, and People Powered, but no formal partnerships. The "Decidim Directors Group" facilitates coordination among governments. The platform's relationship with these networks is mediated by individual practitioners rather than institutional agreements.

### Dimension 6: Knowledge — The Tool Embodies Knowledge, the Community Negotiates It

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP2, Arnau; IPO4, Bezerra; IPO8, Bertone; IPO11, Berenguer)

*The platform as knowledge infrastructure:* Decidim's most significant knowledge contribution is the platform itself — a codified expression of participatory democracy principles in technological form. The platform embodies knowledge about how participation should work: transparent, traceable, modular, and open.

*Knowledge as methodological prescription:* The platform includes methodologies for participatory processes embedded in its modules. This is prescriptive knowledge — the tool suggests how participation should be done.

*Knowledge as negotiation — the political epistemology of core inclusion:* Arnau adds a crucial epistemological dimension. The "rule of 3" is not just a governance mechanism — it is a knowledge practice. The community negotiates what counts as shared knowledge versus particular knowledge. A feature requested by a company for a specific government client is particular knowledge — it may be valid but is not yet common knowledge. A feature needed by three platforms is emerging common knowledge. This epistemological filtering — the distinction between "my need" and "the commons' need" — is the core knowledge practice of the Decidim community.

Arnau identifies a tension: the core's defenders have been "somewhat conservative" — sometimes "strictly too technical" rather than political. Technical conservatism can mean political innovation is slowed because it does not fit existing architectural paradigms. The implication: what counts as relevant knowledge in the community may be systematically biased toward technical rather than political criteria.

*Limitation — knowledge as developer expertise:* The knowledge that matters most in the Decidim ecosystem is technical — how to install, configure, develop, and maintain the platform. The broader knowledge about what makes participation effective is secondary. This reverses People Powered's knowledge orientation (participatory methodology expertise first, tools second).

*The observatory gap:* Giulia Bertone describes Emilia-Romagna's use of Decidim as primarily "a transparency operation" rather than a participatory one. The platform's knowledge is not being used. The knowledge is in the tool; the participatory culture is absent.

*Cross-cultural knowledge exchange:* Brazil's experience generated knowledge about adapting a European-built, desktop-oriented platform for a mobile-first, high-inequality context. This knowledge has not been systematically fed back into the community. Arnau's "artisan" framing of Global South inclusion suggests the community knows this gap exists but has not institutionalised the knowledge flow.

### Dimension 7: Adaptation — From Barcelona to a Multi-Scalar Model

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP2, Arnau; IPO4, Bezerra; IPO11, Berenguer; IPO8, Bertone)

*Brazil as adaptation engine:* Brasil Participativo forced adaptations the Barcelona-centred process had not prioritised: mobile-first design, simplified interface, WhatsApp integration. These adaptations were developed independently and need to be contributed back as proper plugins.

*The Swiss chapter as governance adaptation:* The Swiss chapter required "many years of rejection" before negotiation produced an agreement. This became a template for national autonomy under governance conditions, driven by the funding reality that Swiss money cannot flow to a Catalan organisation.

*Local chapters as territorial evolution:* Arnau frames local chapters as an intentional adaptation: "an opportunity and it is good news that these cooperation mechanisms are built" (IFP2, Arnau). The goals are to "energize local ecosystems, strengthen the companies that provide services, and extend the models of participation at the local level" (IFP2, Arnau). The adaptation is deliberately designed not to replicate the old model at a smaller scale but to create a distributed governance architecture.

*The Italian multi-tenant gap:* Giulia Bertone's frustration — Emilia-Romagna has Decidim, has the need, has the multi-tenant capability, but lacks political will — illustrates a different adaptation challenge. The technological adaptation exists; the political and cultural adaptation does not.

*AI as emerging challenge:* Arnau confirms that AI is on the community's agenda — the research working group is focused on AI, and the social contract may need to evolve or a specific AI statement may be needed. The debate is about both how AI transforms participation and how to prevent AI from being adopted without democratic principles.

*Design justice as unfinished adaptation:* The "artisan" approach to Global South inclusion is an acknowledgement that the community's adaptation to diversity is incomplete. The Ruby training program for women developers, the gender parity in the coordination committee, and the hiring priorities in the technical office represent concrete steps — but Arnau is candid that the deeper structural adaptation (design justice — proactively incorporating subaltern perspectives into the design process) has not been achieved.

### Dimension 8: Commoning — The Foundation, the Tensions, and the Debts

**Presence: STRONGLY PRESENT** (IFP2, Arnau; IPO4, Bezerra; IPO11, Berenguer; IPO8, Bertone)

*The platform as commons:* Decidim represents the purest commons in the corpus — a shared digital infrastructure, freely available, collectively developed, and governed by its community. The open source license operationalises the commons: anyone can use, modify, and distribute.

*The commons-governance gap:* The commons of the platform is more developed than the commons of its governance. Developers who contribute to the platform's code shape its direction; governments and citizens who use it do not. "The idea of a commons — when you use them, you have to give back to the community" (Interviewer). Brazil's adaptations developed independently and not yet contributed back are a commoning debt.

*The productive tension between individual and common needs:* Arnau's most significant contribution to the commoning dimension is his framing of the tension between particular and shared needs as *productive*. The "rule of 3", the modular architecture that allows non-core features without fragmentation, and the permanent negotiation between companies and community are all mechanisms for managing the commons. This is not a failure of commoning but a mature commons governance practice — the community has built explicit systems for deciding what belongs to the commons and what remains particular.

*The anti-compete principle as commoning safeguard:* Arnau's statement that "the association is not a service provider, it does not compete with the companies that provide services" (IFP2, Arnau) is a critical commoning principle. It prevents the association that manages the commons from becoming a competitor within the ecosystem it governs — a risk that has destroyed other open source communities.

*The enterprise ecosystem as economic commons:* The ecosystem of enterprises implementing Decidim constitutes an economic commons — a shared market created by the platform. These enterprises benefit from the platform's reputation, community, and continuous development. Their economic stake aligns them with the platform's health, but their profit motive can conflict with its political values.

*Parma as commoning in practice:* Decidim transformed Parma's neighbourhood workshops from informal communication between citizens and councillors into a structured, transparent, accountable process. Giulia Bertone: "I never expected it to work this way. It has contributed not only to transparency, but also to ordering communication flows" (IPO8, Bertone). The platform created a commons of civic communication.

*Knowledge as debated commons:* Arnau's account of the "rule of 3" reveals that *what counts as common knowledge* is itself a site of community negotiation. The epistemological filtering between particular and shared needs is an act of commoning — the community collectively decides what knowledge belongs to the commons.

### Dimension 9: Impacts

**Presence: PARTIALLY PRESENT** (IPO4, Bezerra; IPO8, Bertone; IPO11, Berenguer; IFP2, Arnau)

*Scale:* 3 million users globally. Brasil Participativo alone: 1.5 million users. The platform has enabled participatory processes at a scale impossible without digital infrastructure.

*Democratic practice:* Specific innovations: participatory budgeting at national scale in Brazil, neighbourhood workshop transformation in Parma, multi-channel participation in Catalonia through AOC's multi-tenant model, New York City's participatory budgets via collaboration agreement.

*Field-level — the political alternative:* Decidim has established an ethical alternative to commercial participation platforms. Governments that adopt it are asserting a political choice — that democratic infrastructure should be publicly owned, transparent, and collectively governed. Arnau's competitive framing (Decidim vs. GoVocal/Osprey) makes explicit what other interviews imply: "Decidim is not a company that wants to make money selling decision software. Decidim is a project that wants more democracy in the world" (IFP2, Arnau). The UN Digital Public Goods Alliance recognition, the open source office in Barcelona, and the city pact for digital rights all flow from this political positioning.

*Barcelona's participatory budgets doubled:* Arnau reports concrete internal impact: two "very powerful editions" of participatory budgets in Barcelona, budget doubled for the next term, communities already organizing around new projects.

*Political agency as impact:* The social contract cases (Chile rejection, Palestine position) demonstrate a distinctive type of impact — not numerical or methodological, but political. The community has collective agency to refuse instrumental use and to take ethical positions. This is an impact on the field's political standards: governments cannot use Decidim without also accepting normative conditions about what genuine participation requires.

*Limitation — the participatory culture gap:* The platform enables participation but does not create it. Giulia Bertone: "If you imagine and understand participation only as a questionnaire about a policy you're developing, you don't even feel the need to look for tools" (IPO8, Bertone). The platform's sophistication exceeds the participatory culture of many adopting institutions. "We have these thousand functions and they use thirty of them" (interviewer's frustration).

*Limitation — Global South adaptation debt:* The platform was built by "welfare state Europeans that do not understand the big inequalities and the big challenges of accessing and using the internet" (IPO4, Bezerra). Arnau's "design justice" framing confirms that the community knows this is a debt but has not systematized the inclusion of Global South perspectives.

## 3. Cross-Cutting Analytical Themes

### Theme 1: The Tool-Community Gap and the Three-Layer Tension

Decidim's most fundamental organisational tension is the gap between the tool and the community — now articulated through Arnau's three-layer framework. The tool is globally distributed, democratically enabling, and technologically sophisticated. The community that governs the tool is developer-dominated and Barcelona-centred. The political layer (democratic ambition) and the technopolitical layer (community governance) are in constant tension with the technical layer (code architecture, sustainability concerns). Arnau's framework reveals that this is not a failure but a designed tension: the three layers are meant to pull in different directions, creating productive friction that forces negotiation. The gap between the layers is the engine of the community's governance evolution.

### Theme 2: The Social Contract as Network Constitution

Arnau's account of the Decidim social contract introduces a concept that distinguishes Decidim from every other case in the corpus. The social contract is not a license, a code of conduct, or a governance charter — it is a living constitution that the community debates, validates, and enforces. It enables political agency (Chile rejection, Palestine position) that other networks either cannot or will not exercise. It defines the boundary between acceptable and unacceptable use. And it is now under pressure to evolve for AI. The social contract represents a form of network constitutionalism — an attempt to embed normative commitments in the infrastructure of a digital commons.

### Theme 3: The Catalan Centre, the Global Periphery, and the Dignity of Difference

Decidim's Catalan-centricity is both its strength (political coherence, institutional stability, financial commitment) and its limitation (reduced responsiveness to Global South needs, governance concentration, cultural distance). The Swiss chapter, Brazil's independent development, the "artisan" approach to design justice, and the tension between European company dominance and global community membership all reflect the same structural dynamic. Arnau's openness about the limitations of current practice — "it is done in a very artisan way," "European companies end up having a very important role" — contrasts with his certainty about the governance principles that must be preserved. **Difference in use is welcome; difference in governance is not.** This tension between distributed adoption and centralised governance will define Decidim's next phase.

### Theme 4: The Political as Competitive Advantage and Constraint

Decidim's political nature — "a project that wants more democracy in the world" — is simultaneously its greatest asset and its greatest limitation. As an asset: it attracts governments seeking assurance against proprietary capture, it enables radical innovation (social contract, collective political agency), and it positions Decidim as a public good rather than a product. As a constraint: it makes neutrality impossible (partners lost over Palestine), it creates political fragility (dependency on Barcelona's government), and it generates "participatory washing" — governments adopting the tool without the political will it requires. The political DNA that distinguishes Decidim from competitors is also what makes it vulnerable. The community's challenge is to sustain political coherence without becoming politically exclusionary.

### Theme 5: The Meritocracy — Democracy Tension in Open Source Governance

Arnau's interview reveals a tension running through every dimension of the case. The open source developer culture is meritocratic: those who code most earn most voice. The Decidim community's political culture is democratic: it aspires to give voice to all members, including non-technical users. These two logics clash repeatedly — in the coordination committee (where technical leads like Carol and Andrés have disproportionate visibility), in development discussions (where European companies dominate), and in the core inclusion process (where technical conservatism can block politically desirable features). Arnau's framing — "when the technopoliticians come, the community sometimes generates a certain rejection because the technical does not always fit with the political" — captures this as an ongoing negotiation rather than a solvable problem.
