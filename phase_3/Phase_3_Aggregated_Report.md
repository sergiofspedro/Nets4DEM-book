## 1. Introduction and Methodology

### 1.1 What This Report Is

This report aggregates findings from 14 case studies of transnational networks operating at the intersection of democratic governance, civic participation, and institutional innovation. Each network was studied through 9 analytical dimensions (organisational identity, membership, governance, resources, external relations, knowledge, adaptation, commoning, and impacts) in Phase 2. Phase 3 moves from dimension-by-dimension analysis to **pattern-based analysis**: identifying recurrent structural dynamics that appear across multiple networks, regardless of their scale, geography, or primary mission.

The report asks: what do these 14 networks, despite their diversity, reveal about the nature of **democratic network governance** in an era of funding contraction, democratic backsliding, and technological disruption?

### 1.2 The Fourteen Cases

The 14 networks studied fall into five broad types, distinguished by their primary mode of operation and the core resource around which membership coheres:

| Type | Networks | Core Resource | Primary Mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Platform/Ecosystem Networks** | Decidim/MetaDecidim, PARES | Shared software infrastructure | Code contribution + implementation |
| **Methodology/Knowledge Networks** | KNOCA, IAF, OIDP, SOIF, ASVIS/Ecosistema Futuro | Shared methodology or professional practice | Peer learning + standard-setting |
| **Hybrid Networks** | People Powered, ALDA | Combined methodology + membership + project implementation | Multi-modal (advocacy, capacity-building, project delivery) |
| **Advocacy/Political Coalitions** | GPR2C, HIC, OGP, EPD, FIDE | Shared political cause or policy objective | Collective advocacy + norm-setting |
| **Juridical-Expert Networks** | FIDE (also classified as advocacy coalition) | Shared professional expertise | Standard-setting + consultation |

### 1.3 From Dimensions to Patterns: The Analytical Transition

Phase 2 analysed each case through 9 standardised dimensions. This produced rich within-case analysis but limited cross-case comparison. Phase 3 inverts the analytical lens: instead of asking "how does this dimension manifest in each network?", it asks **"what structural dynamics recur across networks, cutting across dimensions?"**

A pattern is not merely a dimension that appears in multiple networks. A pattern is a **recurrent structural dynamic** — a tension, a paradox, or a design choice — that shapes how networks govern themselves, relate to their members, secure resources, produce knowledge, and survive. Patterns are identified when the same dynamic appears in substantively different forms across networks of different types, scales, and geographies.

**Table 1.2 — The Analytical Transition**

| Phase 2 (Within-Case) | Phase 3 (Cross-Case) |
|---|---|
| 9 dimensions × 14 cases = 126 analytical cells | 9 patterns, each traced across multiple cases |
| Case is the unit of analysis | Pattern is the unit of analysis |
| "How does Decidim govern itself?" | "How do networks that were born from political movements govern differently from those that weren't?" |
| Identification: each dimension mapped per case | Identification: same dynamic observed in ≥3 cases of different types |

### 1.4 How to Read This Report

Each pattern section (A–I) follows a consistent structure:

1. **Definition**: what the pattern is and why it matters
2. **Network Mapping**: a table showing which networks exhibit the pattern strongly (✓), partially (○), or not at all (✗)
3. **Evidence and Mechanisms**: detailed analysis with interview quotations in the format **[Rede/Nome do Entrevistado, Mês 2026]**
4. **Visualization**: a diagram, spectrum, or comparative table where analytically useful
5. **Contra-Example**: one or more cases that resist or invert the pattern, and what that reveals

Quotations are sourced from the Phase 2 case reports, which in turn draw on the original interview transcripts (Phase 1). All interviews were conducted between May and July 2026.

### PATTERN A: Political Genealogy — Movement Origins and the Activist-Technocrat Tension

#### A.1 Definition

Networks do not emerge from nowhere. They are born from specific historical moments, political movements, professional traditions, or institutional initiatives. This pattern examines **how a network's origin shapes its identity, governance, and relationship to its members** — and, critically, how networks born from political movements manage (or fail to manage) the tension between activist identity and the pressures of professionalisation that come with funding, scale, and institutional recognition.

The core dynamic is the **activist-technocrat tension**: networks born from movements carry normative commitments embedded in their founding moment (the 15M indignados, the right-to-the-city movement, the participatory budgeting movement in New York). As they grow, secure funding, and professionalise, these commitments encounter pressure from donor logics, institutional requirements, and the demands of organisational sustainability. The tension is not between "good" activism and "bad" professionalisation — it is a structural feature of movement-to-organisation trajectories that every politically-born network must negotiate.

|---|---|---|

#### A.3 Evidence and Mechanisms

**The 15M-to-platform trajectory (Decidim):** Decidim is the clearest example of movement-to-network genealogy in the corpus. The platform was "politically forged" [Decidim/Carla Bezerra, Junho 2026] — "the way you build the technology, it influences the politics." The three genealogical streams converge: the 15M/indignados mobilizations of 2011, the free software movement in Spain, and a broader "collective need of democracies" [Decidim/Arnau, Junho 2026]. These origins are not historical footnotes but "inserted almost in the DNA of the community" [Decidim/Arnau, Junho 2026], expressed in the platform's open-source license, its social contract, its rejection of instrumental use by hostile governments (Chile/Piñera), and the community's collective political agency (Palestine position). The political genealogy is the foundation of Decidim's claim to legitimacy as a democratic infrastructure, not merely a software product.

**From NYC participatory budgeting to global hub (People Powered):** People Powered's origins in Josh Lerner's participatory budgeting work in New York give it a different kind of political genealogy — rooted in a specific democratic practice rather than a broad social movement. The transition from the Global Hub for Participatory Democracy to People Powered was itself a participatory process that Charlie Ngounou, a board member, participated in: "We participated to all the aspects related to the change of names, the new structure" [People Powered/Charlie Ngounou, Maio 2026]. This origin in practice — rather than ideology — helps explain People Powered's distinctive combination of strong normative commitment (*"Josh is really somebody who believes in what he says"* [People Powered/Charlie Ngounou, Maio 2026]) with pragmatic institutional adaptation (the European pivot as response to Trump-era funding cuts).

**The anti-organisation born from movements (GPR2C):** GPR2C represents the most radical expression of political genealogy in the corpus. It was deliberately created as a collective political actor that refuses to become an organisation: no legal status, no independent budget, no dedicated staff, no membership dues. This is not incapacity but design — a structural response to the observation that formal organisations in the advocacy space compete with their members for funding, develop institutional interests that diverge from their mission, and become vulnerable to co-optation. The anti-organisation form is the direct institutional expression of GPR2C's movement origins. As Lorena Zarate, a co-coordinator, explains: *"We're doing this because of commitment and conviction. Sometimes we have funding, sometimes we don't"* [GPR2C/Lorena Zarate, Junho 2026].

**The 40-year arc of activist identity (HIC):** HIC, at over 40 years old, provides the longest time-horizon for observing the activist-technocrat tension. The organisation carries the legacy of the 1976 Vancouver Declaration on Human Settlements and the global habitat movement. The triple sustainability crisis identified in its Phase 2 report — intergenerational renewal, financial precarity, and leadership ossification — is, in part, a crisis of political genealogy. The founding generation that carried the movement's radical commitments remains dominant after 30–50 years. Younger potential leaders face structural barriers: short-term project funding prevents stable livelihoods, and the founding generation's continued centrality — driven by commitment, not obstruction — creates a bottleneck that the network has not resolved.

**The expert tradition as genealogy (FIDE):** FIDE represents an alternative form of political genealogy — not born from a social movement but from a professional tradition. The network emerged from the post-WWII European legal framework, grounded in the Council of Europe's democratic standards and the professional identity of jurists and electoral experts. This genealogy shapes FIDE's distinctive mode of operation: knowledge production through manuals and standards, consultation rather than advocacy, a relationship with governments that is expert-to-client rather than movement-to-power. The transition Giovanni describes — from consultation to facilitation — represents a slow evolution of this genealogy, not a rupture with it.

**Table A.1 — How the Activist-Technocrat Tension Manifests**

| Mechanism | Networks Where Observed | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Founder centrality** | SOIF, Decidim, People Powered, HIC | The founder(s) embody the political genealogy; distributing authority beyond them is a structural challenge |
| **Professionalisation pressure** | All politically-born networks | As networks secure funding and scale, they hire professional staff who may not share the movement's political formation |

The spectrum reveals that political genealogy is not binary (movement vs. institution) but a continuum. #### A.6 Contra-Example: KNOCA — Effectiveness Without Political Genealogy

KNOCA (Knowledge Network on Climate Assemblies) demonstrates that a network can achieve significant impact without a political genealogy — and that the absence of one can be a strategic asset. KNOCA was conceived as a temporary, method-focused knowledge network around a single democratic innovation (climate assemblies). Its founder, Graham Smith, explicitly rejected expansion beyond this method: *"Once you start opening up beyond one method, it's really hard to hold the network together"* [KNOCA/Graham Smith, Junho 2026]. The network's individual-only membership — not organisations, only individuals — functions as a political neutralizer, creating spaces where Extinction Rebellion activists and conservative public officials can discuss facilitation technique together.

KNOCA's effectiveness through self-limitation challenges the assumption that political genealogy is necessary for democratic impact. The network demonstrates that a shared methodology, independent of shared political commitments, can sustain a community and generate knowledge — and that the absence of political genealogy can, paradoxically, enable the inclusion of actors who would be excluded by it. The trade-off is that KNOCA cannot take political positions (contrast Decidim's Chile/Palestine stands) and cannot mobilise its community for advocacy — but it was never designed to do so.

### PATTERN B: The Funding Trap — Dependency, Diversification, and the Price of Autonomy

#### B.1 Definition

Every network in the corpus faces the same fundamental question: how to sustain collective action without becoming captive to the sources that fund it. This pattern examines **the structural relationship between funding models and network autonomy**, identifying three dominant dependency clusters (public/institutional, foundation/EU project-based, and self-funding) and the strategies networks use to navigate between them.

The core dynamic is the **funding trap**: the sources most available to democratic networks (public institutions, foundations, EU programmes) are also the sources most likely to shape their agendas, constrain their political independence, and create dependencies that threaten survival when those sources shift. Networks that depend on a single funder or a single type of funding are structurally vulnerable. Networks that diversify face high transaction costs and potential mission drift. Networks that self-fund through membership or commercial activity face limits on scale and equity.

#### B.2 Network Mapping

| Dependency Cluster | Networks | Primary Funders | Dependency Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Public/Institutional Dependency** | Decidim (>50% Barcelona), OIDP (Barcelona+UCLG), FIDE (Council of Europe) | Municipal/national governments, intergovernmental bodies | Political transition at funding source (e.g., Barcelona en Comú→PSOE) |
| **EU/Foundation Dependency** | ALDA (EU projects), EPD (EU framework contracts), KNOCA (European Climate Foundation, single funder), GPR2C (Ford→Misereor→project-based), HIC (project-based), People Powered (USAID+foundations→EU pivot) | EU programmes, American philanthropy, project grants | Donor priority shifts, funding cycle volatility, agenda distortion |
| **Hybrid with Self-Funding Component** | SOIF (advisory surplus reinvested), People Powered (membership dues + grants), ALDA (symbolic membership dues) | Mixed: earned revenue + grants + membership | Tension between revenue-generating activities and mission |
| **Self-Funding / Independence** | IAF (100% membership+accreditation), AfroLeadership/Charlie (company→NGO cross-subsidy) | Membership dues, commercial revenue, personal investment | Limits on scale, geographic equity, Global South inclusion |

#### B.3 Evidence and Mechanisms

**The Barcelona dependency cluster:** Decidim and OIDP both depend substantially on Barcelona City Council — Decidim for over 50% of its budget, OIDP for its secretariat and core operations. This dependency is not accidental but structural: both networks were born from Barcelona's municipalist political project. The dependency survived the transition from Barcelona en Comú to PSOE — a significant resilience test — but it creates a permanent vulnerability. As Carla Bezerra notes: *"They still had more than 50 percent dependent on the Ayuntamiento de Barcelona. They have been trying very much to diversify. But they were not experts into applying to different funding in different sources"* [Decidim/Carla Bezerra, Junho 2026]. The dependency is simultaneously the network's greatest strength (stable, politically aligned funding that enables ambitious innovation) and its greatest vulnerability (a change of government in Barcelona could threaten everything).

OIDP's relationship with funding is distinctive: it has turned dependency into a strategy of "lean resilience." The budget is deliberately minimal — Adrià Duarte Griño frames this as an advantage: *"Also the good thing is that it's so small amount of money that it's probably more easy to survive"* [OIDP/Adrià Duarte Griño, Junho 2026]. The network rejected membership fees as a model, preferring to depend on Barcelona and EU/UCLG framework funding while keeping costs so low that survival through political transitions is almost guaranteed. This is the inverse of the diversification imperative — survival through minimalism rather than through multiple revenue streams.

**The EU/foundation dependency cycle:** ALDA and EPD represent the most complete form of EU project dependency. ALDA's growth has been *project-driven* rather than strategy-driven — *"the project-driven side and the membership-driven side coexist without full integration"* [ALDA/Marco Boaria, Junho 2026]. The organisation's shape reflects donor opportunities, not an internally generated strategic vision. EPD faces an additional structural tension: as both a coordinating network AND a programme-implementing body, it competes with its own member organisations for the same EU funding. This "coordinator-competitor tension" is a direct consequence of the funding ecosystem in which both coordination bodies and implementers compete for limited resources.

KNOCA represents the extreme case of single-funder dependency: the European Climate Foundation funded the entire network for 5 years. Graham Smith is candid about the implications: *"If I'm wrong, then we're kind of screwed"* [KNOCA/Graham Smith, Junho 2026]. But the network was designed to be temporary — its current downsizing is success, not failure. KNOCA demonstrates that single-funder dependency can be compatible with autonomy if the dependency is time-limited and the network's goal is achieved within that window.

People Powered's response to the Trump-era funding shock — losing "half or one third of the budget" when USAID and American foundation funding contracted — was the European pivot: creating a European legal entity to access EU grants and European foundation funding. This is a strategic diversification that simultaneously shifts the network's centre of gravity from American to European institutional logic — a funding decision with profound organisational consequences.

**The "commitment subsidy":** A distinctive funding mechanism appears across networks of all types: individuals working beyond what they are paid (or mandated) to do, filling the gap between what the network costs and what it is funded to deliver. This "commitment subsidy" is most visible in GPR2C — co-coordinators sustain the platform through personal commitment exceeding institutional mandates, combining unpaid coordination work with "small writing commitments" — but it appears in HIC, KNOCA, SOIF, OIDP, and Decidim (through Charlie's self-funded participation). The commitment subsidy is the invisible infrastructure of network sustainability: without it, many networks would cease to function. But it is also a structural vulnerability — dependent on specific individuals whose departure would leave an unfillable gap.

**The self-funding alternative — and its costs:** IAF is the only network in the corpus that is 100% self-funded through membership dues, accreditation fees, and occasional conference revenue. *"We keep quite intentionally the business out of the network"* [IAF/Gerardo, Junho 2026]. This model protects the network from donor-driven agenda distortion and preserves its peer-to-peer character. But the costs are significant: the network cannot subsidise Global South members, limiting geographic equity; it cannot grow rapidly; and it cannot fund the kind of ambitious programmes that donor-funded networks can. Self-funding is independence, but it is also a ceiling.

Charlie Ngounou's AfroLeadership represents a different self-funding model: the company-NGO hybrid. *"When you sign a contract with me to work with me in my company, you have to work for Afro Leadership. You have to give your time and resource"* [Decidim/Charlie Ngounou, Maio 2026]. The company generates revenue through public finance consulting; the NGO receives volunteered time. Grants (UNICEF, US Embassy) are welcomed but not required. This model creates a distinctive relationship with the Decidim network: Charlie is a net contributor, not a net recipient — he pays his own way to Decidim Fest, prints his own promotional materials, and maintains platform infrastructure without financial return. This creates a structural asymmetry in the funding relationship between centre and periphery (see Pattern C).

#### B.5 Contra-Example: IAF — Autonomy as Deliberate Ceiling

IAF's 100% self-funded model proves that networks can exist without donor dependency. But the model works because IAF is a professional body whose members derive direct economic value from certification and accreditation — they pay dues because membership enhances their professional credibility and income. This model would not work for advocacy networks (GPR2C, HIC), whose members are themselves resource-constrained, nor for platform networks (Decidim), whose users are governments and citizens who do not pay for platform access.

IAF's autonomy is thus contingent on its network type — it is a model for professional bodies, not a generalisable solution. The contra-example reveals that the funding trap is not escapable through a single strategy; different network types face structurally different funding constraints, and the strategies available to them reflect their position in the ecosystem.

### PATTERN C: Centre-Periphery — The Persistent Geography of Network Power

#### C.1 Definition

Transnational networks promise to transcend geography — to connect actors across borders, to distribute resources and recognition equitably, to build communities that are global in reach and local in rootedness. In practice, every network in the corpus exhibits a **centre-periphery structure**: a geographically concentrated core (typically European, often a single city) where resources, decision-making power, and cultural norms are concentrated, and a dispersed periphery where members participate on terms set by the centre.

This pattern examines the mechanisms that produce and reproduce centre-periphery dynamics, the costs they impose on peripheral members, and the strategies — both from the centre and the periphery — that attempt to redistribute power. The pattern is not about geography per se but about **the spatial distribution of power in networks that claim to be transnational and democratic**.

#### C.2 Network Mapping

| Presence | Networks | Centre | Periphery Dynamic |
|---|---|---|---|
| ■ Strong | Decidim | Barcelona (platform, governance, Decidim Fest, association) | Charlie is "the only African organization working on DECIDIM"; logo removed without notice; Catalan-Spanish language dominance; membership fee structure incompatible with non-commercial models |
| ■ Strong | OIDP | Barcelona (secretariat, award, coordination) | "Institution-of-institutions" operates as "network-of-individuals"; Nanterre exclusion example |
| ■ Strong | FIDE | Strasbourg/Council of Europe (secretariat, standards) | European legal framework applied globally; limited Global South representation in governance |
| ■ Strong | EPD | Brussels (EU policy, funding, member access) | Larger members with Brussels presence dominate; smaller members benefit from access but cannot shape direction |
| ■ Strong | ALDA | Strasbourg/Brussels (EU funding, coordination) | LDAs are locally governed but networked through European centre; project priorities set by EU donor requirements |
| ■ Strong | SOIF | UK/London (founder, advisory contracts, network weaving) | "Network of networks" with global reach but founder's Rolodex and UK-based advisory work as centre of gravity |
| ◧ Partial | People Powered | Shifting: NYC→Washington→Europe (European legal entity) | European pivot as shift in centre of gravity; "participation-extraction cycle" privileges those who can participate intensively |
| ◧ Partial | OGP | Washington/DC + multilateral structure | Governments control resources; civil society co-creates but within power asymmetries |
| ◧ Partial | HIC | Historically distributed but funding-driven concentration | Short-term project funding prevents stable presence in some regions |
| □ Weak | GPR2C | Deliberately centreless — trans-local assemblies, rotating coordination | Anti-organisation form explicitly resists spatial concentration; "commitment subsidy" distributed across co-coordinators |
| □ Weak | KNOCA | Individual membership neutralizes geography; temporary by design | Focus on method over geography; individual-only membership removes organisational hierarchies |

#### C.3 Evidence and Mechanisms

**Charlie Ngounou as the paradigmatic peripheral member:** No interview in the corpus provides richer evidence of centre-periphery dynamics than Charlie Ngounou's account of his relationship with the Decidim Association. He is the only African organisation actively working with Decidim — *"so far, I've not seen so many organization doing that within DECIDIM"* [Decidim/Charlie Ngounou, Maio 2026]. He attends Decidim Fest at his own expense, having "already booked my flight" for the next edition. He has financed and printed his own promotional brochures for Decidim in French and English. He maintains three developers who know the platform. He has approached UCLGA Africa, the WHO, and municipalities across Cameroon and Central Africa.

For this investment, the institutional reciprocity is minimal. When the Decidim Association restructured its membership model, Charlie's logo was removed from the website without notice: *"One day I opened the CDEM platform and I didn't see my logo. And I said, how come? And then they said, okay, the thing has changed"* [Decidim/Charlie Ngounou, Maio 2026]. The new membership fee — 1.5% of turnover — assumes members generate revenue from Decidim. Charlie does not: *"We don't sell the CDM. We are using the CDM to help participation in our context"* [Decidim/Charlie Ngounou, Maio 2026]. The fee structure was designed for European enterprises that profit from Decidim implementations, not for African civic organisations that spend their own money to promote the platform.

The cultural dimension reinforces the structural. At Decidim Fest, Charlie notes: *"We are in a space where they speak Spanish for some of them... you are one or two blacks with people that are really white"* [Decidim/Charlie Ngounou, Maio 2026]. He compares the atmosphere to Helsinki — another European city where homogeneity is structural, not individual. This is not a complaint about racism but an observation about the cultural costs of participating in a community whose norms, language, and social dynamics were formed in a specific European context.

**The individual-institution paradox (OIDP):** OIDP exhibits a distinctive form of centre-periphery dynamic: the network is formally an institution-of-institutions (cities are members), but it operates as a network-of-individuals. The coordinators in Barcelona — Adrià Duarte Griño and his team — function as the network's memory and connective tissue. When individual practitioners leave their institutions, the network's relationship with those institutions often dissolves. *"That's what Giovanni Allegretti does, not the Pepito Institute; it's you, as an individual"* [OIDP/Entrevistado, Junho 2026]. This personalisation of network ties means that the centre's continuity depends on specific individuals, and that peripheral members whose institutional position changes may lose their network connection.

**The language and cost barriers:** The centre-periphery dynamic has concrete material dimensions that are often invisible to the centre. Language: Decidim Fest operates in Spanish and English, privileging European participants. OIDP's primary working language reflects its Barcelona base. FIDE's standards are produced in European languages. Cost: attending network events requires flights, lodging, and registration fees — costs that are proportionally far higher for members from the Global South. Charlie self-funds his attendance at Decidim Fest; peripheral members who cannot afford this are structurally excluded from the spaces where network relationships are built and decisions are shaped.

**The specificity of Barcelona:** Barcelona's role as a centre for two major networks (Decidim and OIDP) is not coincidental. The city's municipalist political project, initiated by Barcelona en Comú, created a deliberate strategy of using city resources to incubate democratic infrastructure. The result is a concentration of network power in a single city that is simultaneously a source of stability (political commitment, institutional continuity, financial resources) and a source of structural inequality (Catalan-Spanish language dominance, European cultural norms, physical distance from Global South members).

#### C.5 Strategies of Response

Peripheral actors and centre actors deploy different strategies in response to centre-periphery dynamics:

**From the periphery — South-to-North agency:** Charlie Ngounou exemplifies a strategy of proactive engagement that inverts the expected direction of outreach. *"The CDIM, actually, people didn't come towards me or towards Afro Leadership. We were the one going towards the CDIM"* [Decidim/Charlie Ngounou, Maio 2026]. This is not passive reception of Northern initiatives but active seeking — Charlie researched Decidim vs. Consul, chose the more vibrant community, and then invested his own resources to build a presence. Giovanni identifies this as an "elective affinity" between Charlie's tech-social hybrid identity and Decidim's technical community.

**From the centre — design justice as aspiration:** Arnau acknowledges the centre-periphery gap and frames the response in terms of "design justice": *"How you incorporate into the community in a proactive way this look, if you want, of colonial, of the subglobal in the design process — this is done in a very artisan way"* [Decidim/Arnau, Junho 2026]. The honesty of this admission — that inclusion of Global South perspectives is "artisan," not systemic — is analytically valuable. It reveals that the centre knows the gap exists but has not yet institutionalised the mechanisms to close it. Concrete steps include the Ruby training program for women developers, gender parity in the coordination committee, and hiring priorities in the technical office — but these address gender and European diversity, not the structural exclusion of African and Global South actors.

**From the periphery — building parallel infrastructure:** Charlie's partnership with Octree/VOCA (Switzerland) to develop the WhatsApp-to-Decidim API represents a strategy that bypasses the centre entirely. Rather than waiting for Barcelona to develop African-relevant features, Charlie has built his own technical infrastructure in collaboration with a European enterprise. The API has been tested in Tanzania and Brazil — creating direct peripheral-peripheral connections that do not route through Barcelona. This is a form of centre-periphery negotiation that operates through technical innovation rather than governance reform.

#### C.6 Contra-Example: GPR2C — Trans-Local by Design

GPR2C is the only network in the corpus that was deliberately designed to resist centre-periphery dynamics. Its anti-organisation form — no headquarters, no secretariat, no dedicated staff, no independent budget — eliminates the institutional infrastructure that typically concentrates power in a single location. Coordination rotates among co-coordinators in different regions. Assemblies are held in different cities. Funding flows through member organisations, not through a central body.

But GPR2C's trans-local design is not cost-free. The "commitment subsidy" that sustains it (see Pattern B) falls disproportionately on co-coordinators who have the personal capacity, institutional backing, and financial resources to work beyond their mandates. The network's ability to act quickly, to speak with a single voice, and to build institutional memory is constrained by its distributed structure. GPR2C demonstrates that centre-periphery dynamics can be resisted — but that resistance has its own costs, and that even genuinely trans-local networks depend on individuals whose personal circumstances enable them to carry a disproportionate share of the work.

### PATTERN D: Architecture of Belonging — Membership, the "Human Touch," and Who Counts

#### D.1 Definition

How does a network make its members feel that they belong? This pattern examines the gap between **formal membership** (the legal, procedural, and financial structures that define who is in the network and what rights they have) and **substantive belonging** (the felt experience of being part of something, of having a voice that matters, of being recognised and valued by the community). The pattern reveals that networks with sophisticated formal membership architectures can fail to generate substantive belonging, while networks with minimal formal structures can generate intense affective attachment.

The core dynamic is the tension between **structure** and **touch**: the concentric circles, voting rights, membership fees, and governance committees that constitute formal membership versus the personal relationships, shared experiences, cultural coherence, and emotional investment that constitute substantive belonging. The most successful networks achieve alignment between the two; the most troubled exhibit a gap.

#### D.2 Network Mapping

|---|---|---|---|
| ■ Strong | Decidim | Concentric circles: technical secretariat → coordination committee (8 elected) → partners → general community → service providers → government committee | Developer-dominated; Charlie's experience of distance: "you don't see that at the CDM"; self-funded persistence as substitute for institutional belonging |
| ■ Strong | People Powered | Membership + board + mentorship + governance committee; internal participatory budgeting | "Human touch" (Charlie); love-critique paradox — most loved AND most criticised; structural coherence between methodology and governance |
| ■ Strong | OIDP | Institution-of-institutions (cities) but operates as network-of-individuals | "Pollinating bees" carrying relationships across institutional boundaries; former participants (Nanterre) excluded |
| ■ Strong | IAF | Professional body: membership + accreditation + certification | "Safe space" architecture — Share-and-Learn, Marketplace, SIGs, ethics process — layered interaction zones |
| ■ Strong | ALDA | Membership association + project-implementing body + LDA network (triple identity) | "Project-driven side and membership-driven side coexist without full integration" |

#### D.3 Evidence and Mechanisms

**The concentric circles and the belonging gap (Decidim):** Decidim has the most formally sophisticated membership architecture in the corpus — Arnau's concentric circles model, the elected coordination committee, the transparent governance process on MetaDecidim, the partner tiers, and the government council. But the architecture's sophistication does not translate into equivalent levels of substantive belonging for all members. The most active participants in MetaDecidim are developers and enterprises, not the governments and citizens who use the platform. Carla Bezerra is blunt: *"It's not a community of practitioners. It's not a community of governments. It's not a community of NGOs. Or it's a community of developers"* [Decidim/Carla Bezerra, Junho 2026]. The membership architecture is democratic in design but meritocratic in practice — those who contribute code have voice; those who use the platform do not.

Charlie's experience reveals the belonging gap from the periphery. Despite years of self-funded participation, platform promotion, and technical contribution, he does not feel the same belonging at Decidim that he feels at People Powered. *"You don't see that at the CDM. Even when I get there, I know two, three people, but I'm the one to do the outreach"* [Decidim/Charlie Ngounou, Maio 2026]. The Decidim Association's membership restructuring — which removed his logo without consultation and imposed a fee structure incompatible with his model — reinforced the gap between formal membership procedures and the felt experience of being valued by the community.

**The "human touch" and structural coherence (People Powered):** Charlie's contrast between Decidim and People Powered is the clearest articulation of the difference between formal membership and substantive belonging in the corpus. At People Powered, he feels "belonging — not to belong theoretically or procedurally, but actually more concretely, because you can vote for this, vote for that" [People Powered/Charlie Ngounou, Maio 2026]. The mechanisms that produce this belonging are multiple: the internal participatory budgeting process (members collectively decide how a portion of the budget is spent), the structural coherence between the organisation's methodology and its governance ("walking the talk"), the affirmative action charter (50% of resources to the Global South, 50% of research funding to women), and what Charlie calls the "human touch" — *"Josh and Greta, they are really working to make sure things can go bottom up"* [People Powered/Charlie Ngounou, Maio 2026].

But People Powered also exhibits the **love-critique paradox**: the network that generates the strongest emotional attachment also generates the sharpest critique. The "participation-extraction cycle" — members participate in creating proposals, vote on them, capture the funding to implement them, while those outside this cycle are structurally disadvantaged — has produced what Stefano Stortone calls *"a conflict of interest as big as a house"* [People Powered/Stefano Stortone, Junho 2026]. The higher the democratic aspiration, the more visible the gap between aspiration and reality. The love-critique paradox is not a failure of People Powered's belonging architecture but a structural feature of it: the very mechanisms that generate intense belonging (participatory governance, shared resource allocation) are also the mechanisms that generate exclusion for those who cannot participate intensively.

**The "safe space" as belonging architecture (IAF):** IAF has built a distinctive architecture of belonging not through formal membership tiers but through the deliberate design of interaction spaces. The Share-and-Learn space is a "safe zone" where practitioners can present unfinished work without fear of commercial competition or professional judgment. The Marketplace space makes commercial transactions transparent and explicit, keeping business out of the network's relational spaces. Special Interest Groups are self-organised communities of practice. The ethics process uses dialogue-based dispute resolution rather than punitive mechanisms. This layered architecture of spaces creates different registers of belonging for different types of interaction — a more sophisticated approach than the single-tier membership model used by most networks.

**The individual-institution paradox (OIDP):** OIDP is formally an institution-of-institutions — cities are members, represented by their officials. But in practice, the network operates as a network-of-individuals. When a practitioner leaves their institution, the network's relationship with that institution often dissolves — even if the institution remains a formal member. Former participants (the example of Nanterre is cited) are excluded from the network's memory and activities. This paradox means that OIDP's belonging is more personal than institutional — attachment is to the community of practitioners, not to the formal membership structure. This creates agility (practitioners carry relationships across institutional boundaries, functioning as "pollinating bees") but also fragility (institutional continuity is not guaranteed).

**The anti-organisation and belonging through commitment (GPR2C):** GPR2C has no formal membership — no dues, no voting rights, no membership register. Belonging is generated entirely through shared political commitment and participation in collective action. Co-coordinators sustain the platform through personal conviction: *"We're doing this because of commitment and conviction. Sometimes we have funding, sometimes we don't"* [GPR2C/Lorena Zarate, Junho 2026]. This model produces intense belonging among the core group but risks excluding those who share the political commitment but cannot participate at the intensity the network demands. The absence of formal membership is simultaneously GPR2C's greatest protection (against co-optation and competition) and its greatest limitation (inability to scale participation beyond those who can afford to contribute unpaid labour).

KNOCA's individual-only membership model — no organisations, only individuals — functions as both a political neutralizer and a belonging mechanism. Belonging is generated through shared practice, not shared identity. The model works because KNOCA is method-focused and temporary — it does not need the durable affective bonds that sustain permanent networks. KNOCA demonstrates that substantive belonging can be generated without formal membership structures, and that the form belonging takes depends on what the network is designed to achieve. For KNOCA, belonging is a means to knowledge exchange, not an end in itself — and the membership model is designed accordingly.

### PATTERN E: Knowledge Regimes — Who Produces, Validates, and Circulates Knowledge

#### E.1 Definition

Democratic networks are knowledge-producing entities. They generate methodologies, standards, playbooks, research, case studies, and practical wisdom about how participation works. This pattern examines **who produces knowledge within networks, how that knowledge is validated, and how it circulates** — and, critically, whose knowledge counts and whose is marginalised.

The core dynamic is the tension between **centre-produced knowledge** (methodologies, standards, and manuals produced by the network's core for distribution to members) and **peripheral knowledge** (innovations, adaptations, and insights produced by members in specific contexts, which may never be systematically integrated into the network's knowledge base). Networks that only recognise centre-produced knowledge miss the innovations happening at their edges; networks that only celebrate peripheral knowledge may lack the standards and quality control that give their knowledge credibility.

#### E.2 Network Mapping

| Knowledge Regime | Networks | Mode of Production | Mode of Validation | Mode of Circulation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Centre-produced, standards-based** | FIDE (legal manuals, electoral standards), People Powered (playbooks, mentorship curriculum) | Core experts produce codified knowledge | Peer review, expert validation, governance approval | Top-down distribution to members, training programmes |
| **Peer-produced, practice-based** | IAF (Share-and-Learn, SIGs), KNOCA (practitioner exchange), OIDP (award as knowledge aggregation) | Members share practices with each other | Peer validation, community recognition | Horizontal exchange, events, awards |
| **Platform-embodied knowledge** | Decidim (platform architecture embodies democratic principles), PARES (intermediary as translator) | The tool itself is the primary knowledge artefact | Community governance ("rule of 3" for core inclusion) | Code adoption, modular architecture, documentation |
| **Peripheral innovation, not yet integrated** | Decidim (WhatsApp API, "participatory archive"), HIC (counter-data) | Members in specific contexts develop adaptations | Validation by use, not by centre | Local circulation; pathway to core integration unclear or absent |
| **Methodology-as-knowledge** | SOIF (futures literacy methodology), ASVIS/EF (futures literacy in Italy), KNOCA (climate assemblies method) | Core develops and refines methodology | Methodological rigour, practitioner feedback | Training, consultancy, network weaving |

#### E.3 Evidence and Mechanisms

**FIDE as the paradigmatic centre-produced knowledge regime:** FIDE's knowledge model is the most traditional in the corpus — legal experts produce manuals and standards on electoral processes, democratic governance, and human rights, which are then distributed to governments, courts, and civil society organisations. The knowledge is authoritative because it is produced by recognised experts and validated through peer review. The model works for legal-technical knowledge where expertise is concentrated and standards require consistency. But it creates a structural distance between knowledge producers and knowledge users — the governments and citizens who apply the standards have limited input into their development — and it does not systematically capture the adaptations and innovations that occur when standards are applied in diverse contexts.

**People Powered's playbooks and the mentorship model:** People Powered's knowledge regime combines centre-produced knowledge (playbooks, training curricula) with peer circulation (mentorship programmes, the Digital Participation Technical Committee where Charlie serves). The playbooks codify best practices in participatory budgeting and democratic innovation, drawing on the network's global experience. The mentorship model embeds knowledge in relationships rather than documents — experienced practitioners guide newer ones, and knowledge circulates through practice rather than through reading. This model is more responsive to context than FIDE's standards-based approach but depends on the availability and willingness of mentors, creating potential bottlenecks.

**Decidim's three knowledge layers:** Decidim's knowledge regime operates at three levels. First, the platform itself embodies knowledge about democratic participation in its architecture — the platform is a codified expression of democratic principles in technological form. Second, the community negotiates what counts as shared knowledge through the "rule of 3" — a feature is not core knowledge until it is needed by at least three platforms. Third, peripheral innovations (Brazil's mobile-first adaptation, Charlie's WhatsApp API) represent knowledge produced outside the centre's knowledge regime, which has not yet been systematically integrated. Arnau's "artisan" framing — design justice done "in a very artisan way" — acknowledges that the community knows this knowledge exists but has not institutionalised the pathway for it to enter the core.

**Charlie's WhatsApp API as peripheral knowledge innovation:** The WhatsApp-to-Decidim API, developed by Charlie in partnership with Octree/VOCA (Switzerland), is the most significant example of peripheral knowledge innovation in the corpus. The API responds to conditions the Barcelona centre does not face: phone-only digital access, prohibitive data costs ($1-2 per gigabyte), weak digital literacy, and carrier pricing that makes WhatsApp effectively free while websites incur data charges. The API enables users to submit proposals, ideas, and comments via WhatsApp, which flow directly into Decidim without the user ever opening the platform. *"If we can use WhatsApp so that someone writes a proposal on WhatsApp and then it goes directly in the platform without him opening the platform, I think we will have done a very great breakthrough"* [Decidim/Charlie Ngounou, Maio 2026]. Charlie calls this the "participatory archive" concept — using Decidim to formalise the informal participation already happening in communities.

This innovation was developed entirely outside the Barcelona-centred core development process. The Decidim Association did not commission it, fund it, or participate in its development. It is a peripheral innovation — but one that Giovanni identifies as having potential value for the entire Decidim ecosystem: *"your gift to the growth of Decidim as a global platform"* [Entrevistador/Giovanni Allegretti, Maio 2026]. The question is whether the Decidim community has the institutional mechanisms to recognise, validate, and integrate this knowledge into the core — and the evidence suggests it does not, or not yet.

**HIC's counter-data as epistemic practice:** HIC has developed a distinctive knowledge practice: producing data that the state refuses to produce. Systematic eviction monitoring, informal settlement mapping, and documentation of housing rights violations fill information vacuums created by government underreporting or deliberate opacity. *"When you produce your own data, the media actually pays attention"* [HIC/Lorena Zarate, Junho 2026]. Counter-data is simultaneously an epistemic practice (producing knowledge the powerful prefer not to exist) and a political practice (using that knowledge for advocacy and accountability). Unlike FIDE's expert-produced standards or People Powered's codified playbooks, HIC's counter-data is produced through the network's members on the ground, validated by its political utility, and circulated through media and advocacy channels rather than through formal knowledge management systems.

#### E.4 Visualization: Knowledge Production and Circulation Models

**Table E.1 — The Direction of Knowledge Flow**

| Direction | Networks | Description | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Centre → Periphery** | FIDE, People Powered, SOIF | Core produces standards, manuals, methodologies; distributes to members | Quality control, consistency, expert authority | May not fit local contexts; peripheral adaptations not captured |
| **Periphery → Centre** | HIC (counter-data), Decidim (peripheral innovations theoretically) | Members produce knowledge; centre aggregates or should aggregate | Contextual relevance, innovation from practice | Pathway to centre integration unclear; centre may lack capacity or will to integrate |

#### E.5 Charlie's Intervention in the Language-and-Literacy Debate

Charlie's analysis of digital literacy versus language translation represents a knowledge contribution that inverts the centre's assumptions about what the periphery needs. When asked about language barriers — a standard Global South technology deployment concern — Charlie dismisses the issue and reframes it: *"If you give the CDIM to me in my own language, I'm not sure I will be able to use it because I don't write and read my language"* [Decidim/Charlie Ngounou, Maio 2026]. Colonial languages (French, English, Portuguese, Spanish) are the languages of instruction and administration across Africa. Local languages are primarily oral, not written. Translating Decidim into local languages would paradoxically make it *less* usable, because users were never taught to read those languages.

The real barriers — digital literacy, infrastructure (electricity, internet cost), and the political context ("democracy is almost inexistent") — are more fundamental and harder to address than translation. This is a knowledge contribution that the European centre, with its assumptions about what African users need, could not have produced. It demonstrates the value of peripheral knowledge — not just as adaptation of centre knowledge but as a correction to the centre's understanding of the problem.

#### E.6 Contra-Example: OGP — Co-Creation as Knowledge Production

OGP's National Action Plan co-creation model represents a distinctive knowledge regime: governments and civil society jointly produce commitments, which are then independently assessed by the Independent Reporting Mechanism (IRM). Knowledge is produced through a structured negotiation between unequal partners, validated by an independent body, and made public as a mechanism of accountability. This is neither centre-produced (the commitments are co-created, not imposed) nor purely peer-produced (the process is structured and facilitated). It is a hybrid model that embeds knowledge production in a political process — and its quality depends on the functioning of the civic space in each participating country. When that space shrinks, the knowledge regime weakens with it.

### PATTERN F: Technology as Identity — Platform-First vs. Methodology-First Networks

#### F.1 Definition

Networks in the corpus divide along a fundamental strategic axis: whether their primary identity coheres around a **shared technological platform** or a **shared methodology**. This is not a trivial distinction — it shapes how the network recruits members, how it governs itself, what knowledge it values, and how it relates to its users. Platform-first networks (Decidim, and within its ecosystem, PARES) are built around code: the platform is the network's centre of gravity, and the community forms around the shared task of developing, maintaining, and deploying it. Methodology-first networks (People Powered, KNOCA, IAF, SOIF, OIDP) are built around practice: a way of doing participation (participatory budgeting, climate assemblies, facilitation, futures literacy) is the centre of gravity, and the community forms around learning, applying, and refining that practice.

The core dynamic is the **tool-culture gap**: the observation, made independently by multiple interviewees, that the technological or methodological infrastructure networks provide does not, by itself, create the participatory culture needed to use it meaningfully. This gap appears in both platform-first and methodology-first networks, though in different forms.

#### F.2 Network Mapping

| Identity Type | Networks | Core Resource | How Community Coheres |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Platform-First** | Decidim, PARES | Shared software (the Decidim platform) | Code contribution, platform deployment, developer community |
| **Methodology-First** | People Powered, KNOCA, IAF, SOIF, OIDP, ASVIS/EF | Shared methodology or professional practice | Peer learning, standard-setting, methodology refinement |
| **Hybrid/Neither Dominant** | ALDA, FIDE, GPR2C, HIC, OGP, EPD | Political cause, professional expertise, or institutional mandate | Advocacy, project delivery, norm-setting — technology and methodology are tools, not identity |

#### F.3 Evidence and Mechanisms

**Decidim: the platform IS the community:** Decidim is the purest expression of platform-as-identity in the corpus. The platform is not a tool the community uses; it is the reason the community exists. Arnau describes how code adoption becomes the basis for community formation: *"This adoption of the code is the excuse to make the link for where to start weaving a community"* [Decidim/Arnau, Junho 2026]. The platform's open-source license is not just a legal instrument but an identity statement — it operationalises the political commitment to transparency, auditability, and collective ownership that defines the community. The three-layer model (political, technical, technopolitical) embeds democratic principles in the platform's architecture: open code enables trust, trust enables adoption, adoption extends the community, the community improves the code.

But platform-as-identity has structural consequences. The community that governs the platform is predominantly a developer community — those who write code have voice; those who use the platform do not. Carla Bezerra's observation — *"It's not a community of practitioners. It's not a community of governments. It's not a community of NGOs. Or it's a community of developers"* [Decidim/Carla Bezerra, Junho 2026] — identifies the core tension. The platform enables democratic participation for millions of citizens, but the governance of the platform itself is shaped by a technically-skilled minority. The meritocratic culture of open source development and the democratic culture of participatory governance are in permanent tension.

**People Powered: the methodology IS the community:** People Powered coheres around participatory budgeting and related democratic innovations — not around a specific technological platform. The network's knowledge production (playbooks, mentorship), its governance innovation (internal participatory budgeting for resource allocation), its membership structure, and its advocacy all flow from the shared commitment to a set of democratic practices. Charlie's comparison between Decidim and People Powered illuminates the difference: at Decidim, the shared resource is code; at People Powered, the shared resource is a way of working — and People Powered's structural coherence (its internal governance mirrors its external methodology) makes the belonging feel more concrete.

**The tool-culture gap:** The single most recurrent observation about technology in the corpus is that the tool — whether a software platform or a participatory methodology — does not create the culture to use it. Giulia Bertone, describing her experience implementing Decidim in Emilia-Romagna, captures this precisely: *"It's a bit like the idea that the tool alone — boom! — will create who knows what"* [Decidim/Giulia Bertone, Junho 2026]. The platform has "a thousand functions and they use thirty of them." The knowledge is in the tool; the participatory culture is absent.

This gap appears across network types. In PARES's work with Italian municipalities, the gap is between the platform's sophistication and the administrations' capacity to use it for genuine participation rather than as "a transparency operation." In OGP, the gap is between the co-creation mechanism and the political will to implement commitments. In OIDP, the gap is between the award's recognition of best practices and the ability of other cities to replicate them. The tool-culture gap is not a failure of technology or methodology — it is a structural feature of networks that provide infrastructure without being able to provide the political and cultural conditions for its use.

**The digital divide as exclusion mechanism:** Charlie's analysis of digital literacy and infrastructure reveals a deeper dimension of the tool-culture gap. In contexts where computers are prohibitively expensive, electricity is unreliable, internet costs $1-2 per gigabyte, and most citizens' only digital device is a phone with "weak digital literacy," a platform designed for desktop use by "welfare state Europeans" [Decidim/Carla Bezerra, Junho 2026] is structurally exclusive. The tool-culture gap in Africa is not primarily about participatory culture but about basic access. The WhatsApp API is an attempt to bridge this gap by routing participation through a channel that people already use — but it reveals that the platform-first model, however open-source and democratic in intent, embeds assumptions about infrastructure, literacy, and access that exclude the very populations democratic participation most needs to reach.

The spectrum reveals that most networks cluster toward methodology-first. Only Decidim and PARES are genuinely platform-first. OGP occupies an intermediate position — its platform (the OGP website, the IRM database) is a mechanism for accountability, not an identity. The six networks on the right (GPR2C, HIC, FIDE, ALDA, EPD, and to some degree OIDP) are neither platform-first nor methodology-first — their identity coheres around political causes, professional expertise, or institutional mandates. This suggests that the platform-methodology axis is relevant for only a subset of democratic networks — those whose primary value proposition is a tool (digital or methodological) for participation, rather than a political or professional identity.

#### F.5 Contra-Example: GPR2C — Network Without Technology

GPR2C is a transnational advocacy network that operates without a technological platform and without a codified methodology in the sense that People Powered or KNOCA have one. Its tools are political: campaigns, declarations, assemblies, advocacy. Its knowledge is produced through collective political action. It demonstrates that networks can achieve significant impact without either a shared platform or a shared methodology — through shared political commitment alone. The cost is that GPR2C cannot scale through technology the way Decidim can (3 million users), and it cannot standardise practice the way People Powered can (global playbooks) — but it also avoids the tool-culture gap entirely, because it does not provide a tool.

### PATTERN G: Governance Innovation — Contracts, Budgets, Substitutes, and the Architecture of Decision

#### G.1 Definition

Democratic networks face a distinctive governance challenge: they must govern themselves democratically while pursuing democratic goals in the world. A network that advocates for participatory democracy but governs itself through opaque, unaccountable, or oligarchic mechanisms faces a legitimacy crisis. This pattern examines **the governance innovations that networks have developed to align their internal governance with their external mission**, and the tensions that arise when this alignment is incomplete.

The core dynamic is **performative governance vs. structural governance**: do the network's governance mechanisms genuinely distribute power and enable participation, or do they provide the appearance of democratic governance while power remains concentrated? The most innovative governance mechanisms in the corpus — Decidim's social contract, People Powered's internal participatory budgeting, OGP's co-creation model, IAF's safe space architecture — are attempts to close this gap.

#### G.2 Network Mapping

| Governance Innovation | Networks | Mechanism | Distinctive Feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Social contract as network constitution** | Decidim | Community-validated social contract that enables collective political agency | Network can refuse instrumental use (Chile/Piñera: "Decidim is not good for anything if there is no real participation"); network takes political positions (Palestine); binding on all members |
| **Internal participatory budgeting** | People Powered | Members collectively decide how a portion of budget is spent | "Walking the talk" — the network uses the methodology it advocates externally for its own governance |
| **The "Rule of 3"** | Decidim | Feature needs must be shared by ≥3 platforms for core inclusion | Distinguishes "my need" from "the commons' need"; epistemological filtering of what counts as shared knowledge |
| **Co-creation + independent monitoring** | OGP | Governments and civil society jointly produce commitments; IRM independently assesses | Embeds accountability in the governance mechanism; governments cannot control assessment |
| **Governance by substitution** | OIDP | Award functions as substitute for observatory function the name implies but funding doesn't support | Name outlived meaning; function re-grounded through substitute practice |
| **Safe space architecture** | IAF | Layered interaction zones (Share-and-Learn, Marketplace, SIGs, ethics process) | Separates networking from commercial activity; creates different registers for different interaction types |
| **Anti-organisation form** | GPR2C | No legal status, no budget, no staff, no dues — governance through rotating coordination and assemblies | Governance through refusal to govern formally; protection against co-optation and competition |
| **Individual-only membership** | KNOCA | Only individuals, not organisations; temporary by design | Neutralizes political identities; creates space for practice-focused exchange across political divides |
| **Open-use logo policy** | ASVIS/EF | Any participant can use the visual identity without permission; Steering Committee right to withdraw is primary (unused) lever | Distributed ownership enacted through a small, concrete mechanism |
| **Self-funding as governance choice** | IAF | Refusal to accept external funding | Protects from donor-driven agenda distortion; preserves peer-to-peer character |
| **Managed openness** | OIDP | Deliberate inclusion of non-democratic cities | "Better to have them with us hearing our narrative"; ethical tensions resolved through distributed decision-making (Tehran case) |
| **LDA federated model** | ALDA | Locally governed, multi-stakeholder agencies networked through coordinating structure | Local ownership + shared infrastructure; coordination costs and variable quality as trade-off |

#### G.3 Evidence and Mechanisms

**The social contract as network constitutionalism (Decidim):** Decidim's social contract is the most ambitious governance innovation in the corpus. It is not a license, a code of conduct, or a governance charter — it is a living constitution, *"an instrument validated by the community, discussed by the community, and approved by the community"* [Decidim/Arnau, Junho 2026]. It enables collective political agency that no other network in the corpus exercises. When Piñera's right-wing government in Chile sought to use Decidim for a constitutional process, *"a part of the community said no to our name"* — and after intense debate, the community issued a formal rejection: *"Decidim is not good for anything if there is no real participation — we reject the use of Decidim"* [Decidim/Arnau, Junho 2026]. The community took a collective position on Palestine, and "lost some partners" as a result. This is network constitutionalism — the capacity to define, collectively, the normative boundaries of acceptable use and to enforce them, even at material cost.

The social contract is now under pressure to evolve. Arnau notes that a statement on AI is needed — *"not just any AI is worth"* — and the research working group is focused on this question. The evolution of the social contract is itself a governance process, one that tests whether the community can adapt its constitution to new technological and political conditions without losing its normative coherence.

**Two implications for the social contract:** First, Charlie's experience reveals that the social contract, for all its sophistication, does not yet address the relationship between the association and its peripheral members. The membership restructuring, the logo removal, and the turnover-based fee model all occurred without consultation with the African partner. The social contract governs the community's relationship to external political forces (Chile, Palestine, AI) but does not yet fully govern the community's relationship to its own peripheral members. Second, the social contract's political agency depends on the community's willingness to exercise it — and willingness may vary across issues. The Chile and Palestine cases demonstrate capacity; the absence of collective action on centre-periphery governance suggests that capacity is selective.

**The participation-extraction cycle as governance failure (People Powered):** People Powered's internal participatory budgeting is a governance innovation that embodies structural coherence between methodology and governance. But the "participation-extraction cycle" — members participate in creating proposals, vote on them, then capture the funding to implement them — reveals a structural flaw in the design. Those outside the cycle (small organisations, Global South practitioners without the time or resources for intensive participation) are structurally disadvantaged. The very mechanism that generates belonging and legitimacy for active participants generates exclusion for those who cannot participate at the required intensity. Stefano Stortone's observation — *"A conflict of interest as big as a house was created"* [People Powered/Stefano Stortone, Junho 2026] — captures the tension between the democratic aspiration and the practical outcome.

**Self-limitation as governance strategy:** Several networks have adopted governance strategies based on limitation rather than expansion. KNOCA refused to broaden beyond climate assemblies: *"Once you start opening up beyond one method, it's really hard to hold the network together"* [KNOCA/Graham Smith, Junho 2026]. IAF refuses external funding to preserve its peer-to-peer character. OIDP keeps its budget minimal to ensure political survival. These are not failures of ambition but deliberate governance choices — decisions about what the network is *not* that are as important as decisions about what it is. Self-limitation as governance is the inverse of the growth imperative that dominates most organisational thinking.

**Governance by substitution (OIDP):** OIDP's award mechanism is a governance innovation of a different kind — not a formal governance structure but a practice that substitutes for one. The network's name — International *Observatory* of Participatory Democracy — implies a systematic observation function that the network's minimal budget cannot support. The Best Practice Award fills this gap: it generates data, recognition, and recruitment that the name "Observatory" implies but the network cannot deliver through dedicated research. This is governance by substitution — a mechanism that performs the function of a formal structure through a different, lighter practice. OIDP's lean resilience (Pattern I) and governance by substitution are complementary: the network survives by doing less, and what it does is a substitute for what it cannot afford.

### PATTERN H: Scale and Impact — The Tension Between Reach and Depth

#### H.1 Definition

Every network in the corpus makes claims about its impact. But what counts as impact — and how it is measured, demonstrated, and attributed to the network — varies dramatically. This pattern examines the tension between **scale** (quantitative reach — numbers of users, members, countries, installations) and **depth** (qualitative transformation — changed practices, deepened democratic culture, shifts in power relations). It also identifies a distinctive strategic fork: networks that seek **permanence** (growth, sustainability, institutionalisation) versus those designed to be **temporary** (achieving a specific goal and then dissolving or downsizing).

The core dynamic is that scale and depth are often in tension — the networks with the greatest quantitative reach (Decidim's 3 million users, OGP's 75+ countries) are not necessarily those with the deepest qualitative impact, and vice versa. The measurement problem — the difficulty of attributing democratic impact to network activity — compounds this tension.

#### H.2 Network Mapping

| Impact Profile | Networks | Scale Indicators | Depth Indicators | Measurement Approach |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Scale-Driven** | Decidim (3M users, 1.5M Brasil, 400+ installations), OGP (75+ countries, 4,000+ commitments), People Powered (50+ member organisations globally) | Quantitative metrics (users, countries, commitments) | Variable; depth documented in some cases (Parma, Brasil Participativo) but not systematically | Platform analytics, IRM reports, member reporting |
| **Depth-Driven** | HIC (counter-data, eviction monitoring), GPR2C (policy influence, norm-shifting), KNOCA (deep expertise in one method), PARES (Parma's neighbourhood workshop transformation) | Limited quantitative scale | Documented qualitative transformation in specific contexts | Case studies, policy change tracking, practitioner testimony |
| **Self-Limiting / Temporary** | KNOCA (designed to end), OIDP (lean resilience through smallness) | Deliberately limited | Depth achieved through focus and self-limitation | Success defined by obsolescence (KNOCA) or survival (OIDP) |
| **Ambition Without Systematic Measurement** | ALDA, EPD, FIDE, SOIF, ASVIS/EF, IAF | Claims of impact made | Evidence variable; systematic impact assessment not institutionalised | Anecdotal, case-based, or not systematically collected |

#### H.3 Evidence and Mechanisms

**The scale-depth trade-off:** The networks with the greatest quantitative reach achieve it through mechanisms that standardise and replicate — Decidim's open-source code, OGP's National Action Plan template, People Powered's playbooks. Standardisation enables scale but limits depth, because deep democratic transformation is context-specific and resists standardisation. The networks with the deepest documented qualitative impact — Parma's neighbourhood workshop transformation through Decidim, HIC's counter-data-driven advocacy, GPR2C's norm-shifting campaigns — achieve it through intensive, context-specific work that cannot be easily replicated at scale. This is not a failure but a structural feature: the mechanisms that produce scale and the mechanisms that produce depth operate on different logics.

**Decidim: the scale paradox:** Decidim is simultaneously the most scaled network in the corpus (3 million users) and a network whose most compelling impact stories are about depth, not scale. Parma's transformation of neighbourhood workshops from informal communication into structured, transparent, accountable processes. The 25 municipalities in Cameroon where Charlie has pre-installed the platform — but where activation requires the "gatekeeper" condition of genuine political commitment. The platform enables scale; the participatory culture (or its absence) determines depth. Decidim's scale is not evidence of democratic impact — it is evidence of platform adoption. The gap between adoption and impact is the space where the tool-culture gap operates.

**KNOCA: temporary by design:** KNOCA is the only network in the corpus that was conceived as temporary — a catalyst, not a permanent institution. The European Climate Foundation funded it for 5 years to build a knowledge community around climate assemblies. That community now exists and can function without KNOCA. The network's current downsizing is success, not failure — it has achieved its purpose. KNOCA challenges the assumption, embedded in all other networks, that permanence is the measure of success. It demonstrates that a network can be effective precisely because it is temporary: the time limit focuses effort, prevents mission drift, and avoids the institutional self-preservation that diverts resources from purpose. *"Once you start opening up beyond one method, it's really hard to hold the network together"* [KNOCA/Graham Smith, Junho 2026] — the refusal to grow is not a limitation but the condition of effectiveness.

**The measurement problem:** Most networks in the corpus cannot systematically demonstrate their impact. They claim it — in annual reports, funding applications, and advocacy materials — but the claims rest on anecdotal evidence, selected case studies, or output metrics (number of trainings delivered, number of participants) that do not establish causal links between network activity and democratic outcomes. This is not unique to democratic networks — impact measurement is difficult in all complex social interventions — but it creates a structural vulnerability. Networks that cannot demonstrate impact are less competitive for funding, less persuasive in advocacy, and less able to learn from their own experience.

**Table H.1 — What Networks Measure (and What They Don't)**

| What Is Measured | Networks That Measure It | What Is NOT Measured |
|---|---|---|
| Platform adoption (installations, users) | Decidim | Democratic culture change attributable to platform use |
| Government commitments made | OGP | Whether commitments are implemented and change practice |
| Training/event participants | People Powered, IAF, OIDP, FIDE | Whether training changes behaviour or outcomes |
| Policy changes influenced | GPR2C, HIC | Attribution: was the network's advocacy decisive? |
| Membership growth | ALDA, EPD, People Powered | Quality of member engagement beyond formal membership status |
| Counter-data published | HIC | Whether data leads to accountability outcomes |
| Awards given / recognised | OIDP | Whether recognition leads to replication or improvement |

#### H.4 The Scale-Impact Paradox

The networks that are most effective at demonstrating scale (Decidim, OGP) are not necessarily those with the deepest democratic impact, and the networks with the most compelling depth stories (Parma through PARES, Cameroon through Charlie, HIC's counter-data) often lack systematic evidence of their impact. This is not because they are ineffective — it is because the most important democratic impacts (shifts in power relations, changes in political culture, deepened citizen agency) are the hardest to measure and attribute. The measurement problem is not a technical challenge awaiting a better methodology — it is a structural feature of democratic work, which operates on long time horizons, through indirect causal pathways, and in complex political systems where attribution is inherently uncertain.

#### H.5 Contra-Example: KNOCA — Success as Obsolescence

KNOCA's definition of success — the network becomes unnecessary because the community it built can function without it — inverts the standard logic of organisational impact. Most networks measure success by growth: more members, more funding, more programmes, more countries. KNOCA measures success by its own disappearance. This is the most radical impact model in the corpus, and it is also the least replicable — KNOCA could only be temporary because it was focused on a single method, funded by a single funder for a defined period, and led by an individual (Graham Smith) whose academic position provided the stability the network itself did not have. KNOCA demonstrates that temporary-by-design is possible, but also that it depends on specific conditions that most networks cannot replicate.

### PATTERN I: Resilience — How Networks Survive Political Shifts, Funding Crises, and Democratic Backsliding

#### I.1 Definition

Democratic networks operate in hostile environments. The political conditions that enabled their creation — progressive municipal governments, open civic spaces, available funding — are not permanent. Governments change, funding dries up, civic spaces shrink, and democratic backsliding accelerates. This pattern examines **how networks survive (or fail to survive) these shocks**, identifying the strategies that confer resilience and the structural vulnerabilities that threaten it.

The core dynamic is the tension between **adaptation** (changing in response to shocks) and **identity preservation** (maintaining the normative commitments that define the network). Networks that adapt too much risk losing their political DNA; networks that refuse to adapt risk irrelevance or extinction.

#### I.2 Network Mapping

| Resilience Profile | Networks | Shocks Survived | Resilience Strategy | Vulnerability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Political Transition Survivors** | Decidim, OIDP | Barcelona en Comú → PSOE (both survived) | Institutionalisation (Decidim: Metadecidim association 2019, reducing Barcelona dependency); lean resilience (OIDP: minimal budget enables survival through any government) | Decidim still >50% Barcelona-dependent; OIDP depends on Barcelona secretariat continuity |
| **Funding Shock Survivors** | People Powered | Trump-era cuts ("half or one third of the budget") | European pivot (new legal entity, EU grants, European foundations); membership dues diversification | Shift in centre of gravity from US to European logic; participation-extraction cycle may intensify |
| **Existential Crisis Networks** | HIC | Funding precarity, intergenerational gap, leadership ossification (mutually reinforcing) | Counter-data practice sustains relevance; founding generation commitment sustains operations | Triple crisis unresolved; no succession plan for founding generation |
| **Lean Resilience Networks** | OIDP, GPR2C, KNOCA | Multiple shocks through minimalism | Keeping costs so low that survival is almost guaranteed (OIDP); no formal structure to collapse (GPR2C); designed to end (KNOCA) | Cannot scale; cannot respond to opportunities requiring resources |
| **Founder-Dependent Networks** | SOIF, KNOCA, People Powered | Transition from founder-centric to distributed model | SOIF: deliberate 6-year founder exit journey; KNOCA: designed to end with founder; People Powered: European pivot distributes leadership | Founder exit may be impossible to execute (SOIF); network may not survive founder departure (KNOCA already designed to end) |
| **Absorbed/Eliminated** | Open Source Politics (OSP) | Absorbed by GoVocal (commercial competitor) | N/A — did not survive | Commercial consolidation in open-source ecosystem; for-profit actors acquiring non-profit infrastructure |

#### I.3 Evidence and Mechanisms

**The Barcelona transition as resilience test:** The transition from Barcelona en Comú (the progressive municipalist government that incubated Decidim and OIDP) to PSOE (a mainstream social-democratic party) was the most significant political shock any network in the corpus has survived. Both Decidim and OIDP survived — a significant resilience achievement. The mechanisms differed. Decidim had anticipated this vulnerability: the 2019 constitution of the Metadecidim association was driven by the insight that *"the project could not have the exclusive anchorage of Barcelona. A change of government could threaten everything"* [Decidim/Arnau, Junho 2026]. The association, modelled on Wikipedia, Mozilla, and Drupal, created an institutional vehicle independent of any single government. This was resilience through institutionalisation — creating structures that could survive the political project that created them.

OIDP's survival strategy was different: lean resilience through minimalism. *"Also the good thing is that it's so small amount of money that it's probably more easy to survive"* [OIDP/Adrià Duarte Griño, Junho 2026]. The network's budget is so small that any government can afford it; the political cost of cutting it exceeds the financial saving. This is resilience through insignificance — the inverse of the institutionalisation strategy. Both worked, but they represent fundamentally different theories of how networks survive political change.

**The Trump funding shock and the European pivot (People Powered):** The loss of "half or one third of the budget" when the first Trump administration cut USAID and American foundation funding was an existential threat to People Powered. The response — creating a European legal entity to access EU grants and European foundation funding — was a strategic adaptation that preserved the network but transformed it. The European pivot is not just a funding decision; it represents a shift in the network's centre of gravity from American to European institutional logic, with implications for governance culture, funding requirements, and political positioning. This is resilience through transformation — the network survived, but it is not the same network.

**The triple sustainability crisis (HIC):** HIC demonstrates that resilience vulnerabilities can be mutually reinforcing. The intergenerational gap (founding generation remains dominant after 30-50 years) is reinforced by financial precarity (short-term project funding prevents stable livelihoods for younger potential leaders) and leadership ossification (the founding generation's continued centrality is driven by commitment, not obstruction, but creates a bottleneck). These three dynamics form a vicious cycle: financial precarity prevents generational renewal, which perpetuates leadership concentration, which limits the network's capacity to diversify funding. Breaking any one of these cycles requires resources the network does not have — a classic resilience trap.

**The founder's paradox as resilience challenge (SOIF):** SOIF's resilience depends on resolving what its Phase 2 report calls the "founder's paradox": Cat Tully's vision, Rolodex, and relentless work ethic built a "network of networks" with global reach, but the next phase requires distributing that power. The six-year exit journey is an attempt to resolve this — but the path remains uncertain. The paradox is structural: the very qualities that enabled the network's creation (founder's vision, personal relationships, individual credibility) become barriers to its institutionalisation. The founder cannot simply leave — the network's infrastructure is personal, not institutional. But the founder cannot stay forever — the network's next phase requires distribution of the power concentrated in a single individual.

This paradox appears in attenuated form across multiple networks: Graham Smith and KNOCA, Josh Lerner and People Powered, the co-coordinators of GPR2C. In each case, the network's functioning depends on specific individuals whose departure would create an unfillable gap. The "commitment subsidy" (Pattern B) is the mechanism through which this dependence operates: individuals work beyond what they are paid or mandated to do, and the network would collapse without them.

**Network mortality: Open Source Politics absorbed by GoVocal:** The absorption of Open Source Politics (a European Decidim enterprise) by GoVocal (a commercial competitor) is the only documented case of network mortality in the corpus. It demonstrates a specific resilience threat: the commercial consolidation of the open-source ecosystem. When for-profit actors acquire non-profit infrastructure, the values embedded in that infrastructure (open source, democratic governance, community ownership) are at risk. Decidim's anti-compete principle — the association does not compete with service providers — is a resilience strategy designed to prevent this dynamic from affecting the core platform. But it cannot prevent individual enterprises within the ecosystem from being acquired.

#### I.4 Visualization: Resilience Strategies and Their Trade-Offs

**Table I.1 — Resilience Strategies Compared**

| Strategy | Networks | How It Works | Trade-Off |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Institutionalisation** | Decidim (Metadecidim association, 2019) | Create formal structures independent of any single political actor | Structures develop their own interests; institutionalisation can dilute political DNA |
| **Lean resilience** | OIDP, GPR2C | Minimise costs so survival is almost guaranteed | Cannot scale; cannot respond to opportunities requiring resources |

| **Commitment subsidy** | GPR2C, HIC, KNOCA, SOIF, OIDP | Individuals work beyond what they are paid, filling the gap between resources and needs | Structural vulnerability; network collapses if key individuals leave; burns out committed members |

#### I.5 Contra-Example: KNOCA — Resilience Through Refusing to Survive

KNOCA is the only network in the corpus that does not seek resilience. It was designed to end. Its current downsizing is success, not failure — the community it built can now function without it. KNOCA's approach to resilience is to refuse the question entirely: the network does not need to survive because its purpose is to make itself unnecessary. This is the most radical resilience strategy in the corpus, and it is also the least replicable. KNOCA could be temporary because it was method-focused, time-limited, and funded by a single donor for a defined period. Most networks have ongoing missions that cannot be completed within a defined timeframe — they must survive to continue their work. But KNOCA's example challenges the assumption, embedded in every other network's strategy, that survival is the measure of success.

## 4. Cross-Cutting Synthesis: Three Network Types and the Unresolved Tensions

### 4.1 Three Types of Democratic Network

The pattern analysis reveals that the 14 networks cluster into three broad types, distinguished not by their formal characteristics (size, age, geography) but by **the core resource around which the network coheres** and **the primary mechanism through which it creates value for its members**:

**Type 1: Infrastructure Networks** — Networks whose primary value proposition is a shared resource (a platform, a methodology, a standard) that members use, contribute to, and build upon. The network's governance is organised around the stewardship of this resource. Decidim (platform), People Powered (participatory budgeting methodology), KNOCA (climate assemblies method), IAF (facilitation standards), and SOIF (futures literacy methodology) belong to this type — though they differ on whether the resource is technological or methodological.

**Type 2: Advocacy Coalitions** — Networks whose primary value proposition is collective political action. The network enables members to achieve advocacy outcomes they cannot achieve individually — influencing policy, shifting norms, holding power to account. GPR2C, HIC, OGP, EPD, and FIDE belong to this type, though they differ in their political genealogy (movement-born vs. institution-initiated) and their relationship to state power (confrontational vs. collaborative).

**Type 3: Hybrid Organisations** — Networks that combine infrastructure provision with advocacy, and that operate simultaneously as membership associations, project-implementing bodies, and network coordinators. ALDA is the purest example, but People Powered (which provides methodology infrastructure AND engages in advocacy) and OIDP (which provides an award infrastructure AND advocates for participatory democracy) exhibit hybrid characteristics.

**Table 4.1 — Three Network Types and Their Dominant Patterns**

| Pattern | Infrastructure Networks | Advocacy Coalitions | Hybrid Organisations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A: Political Genealogy** | Variable (strong for Decidim/PP, absent for KNOCA) | Strong for movement-born (GPR2C, HIC); weak for institution-initiated (OGP, EPD) | Variable |
| **B: Funding Trap** | Dependency on platform/methodology funders | Dependency on advocacy funders; commitment subsidy | EU project dependency dominant |
| **C: Centre-Periphery** | Strong for platform networks (Decidim); weaker for methodology networks | Variable; GPR2C resists, HIC partially, EPD strong | Strong (European centre) |
| **D: Architecture of Belonging** | Formal membership architectures; belonging gap for non-contributors (Decidim) or non-participants (PP) | Belonging through political commitment; minimal formal membership (GPR2C) or asymmetric membership (EPD) | Triple identity creates belonging fragmentation (ALDA) |
| **E: Knowledge Regimes** | Centre-produced or peer-produced knowledge; tool embodies knowledge (Decidim) | Peripheral knowledge (counter-data); knowledge as advocacy tool | Mixed |
| **F: Technology as Identity** | Platform-first (Decidim) or methodology-first (PP, KNOCA, IAF) | Neither; tech and method are tools, not identity | Neither dominant |
| **G: Governance Innovation** | High (social contract, internal PB, self-limitation, safe spaces) | Variable (anti-organisation, co-creation, traditional) | Moderate |
| **H: Scale & Impact** | Scale-driven (Decidim, OGP) or depth-driven (KNOCA) | Depth-driven; scale limited by advocacy model | Scale limited by project-based model |
| **I: Resilience** | Institutionalisation or lean resilience or temporary-by-design | Commitment subsidy + lean resilience | EU dependency = structural vulnerability |

### 4.2 Convergences: What All Networks Share

Despite their diversity, five dynamics appear across all three types:

1. **The commitment subsidy is universal.** Every network depends on individuals who work beyond what they are paid or mandated to do. The form varies — founder labour (SOIF, People Powered), co-coordinator unpaid work (GPR2C, HIC), self-funded participation (Charlie/Decidim), voluntary governance service (IAF, OIDP) — but the mechanism is the same. The commitment subsidy is the invisible infrastructure of network sustainability; without it, most networks would not function.

2. **The funding trap is structural, not contingent.** No network has solved the tension between financial sustainability and political autonomy. The networks that are most autonomous (IAF, GPR2C) are also those with the most limited scale. The networks with the greatest scale (Decidim, OGP) are those most dependent on institutional funders. This suggests that the funding trap may be a structural feature of democratic network governance, not a solvable problem — the conditions for scale and the conditions for autonomy may be incompatible under current political-economic arrangements.

3. **Centre-periphery dynamics are persistent, even in networks that resist them.** GPR2C, the network most deliberately designed to avoid centre-periphery concentration, depends on co-coordinators whose personal circumstances enable them to carry disproportionate work. The commitment subsidy reproduces centre-periphery dynamics in a different form — not through formal governance structures but through differential capacity to contribute unpaid labour.

4. **The gap between formal governance and substantive belonging appears across types.** Networks with sophisticated membership architectures (Decidim, People Powered) struggle to generate belonging for all members. Networks without formal membership (GPR2C) generate intense belonging among core participants but cannot scale it. The belonging problem is not solved by better governance design alone.

5. **Knowledge flows are asymmetrical.** In every network, some knowledge is recognised as authoritative and some is not. The centre's knowledge (standards, methodologies, platform architecture) is systematically integrated into the network's knowledge base. Peripheral knowledge (adaptations, innovations, contextual insights) may be celebrated but is rarely systematically integrated. The WhatsApp API is a gift from the periphery that the centre does not yet know how to receive.

### 4.3 Divergences: What Distinguishes the Networks

The fundamental divergence is captured by two axes:

**Axis 1: Resource Type — Platform/Methodology vs. Political Cause.** Networks that cohere around a shared resource (platform or methodology) face governance challenges related to the stewardship of that resource (who contributes, who decides what goes into the core, how the resource relates to members who use but do not contribute). Networks that cohere around a political cause face governance challenges related to collective action (how to mobilise, how to speak with one voice, how to maintain commitment over time). These are different governance problems, and they produce different governance innovations.

**Axis 2: Ambition — Permanence vs. Obsolescence.** Most networks seek permanence; KNOCA sought obsolescence. This is not a continuum but a strategic fork. Permanence-seeking networks must solve the funding trap, the founder's paradox, and the intergenerational renewal problem. Obsolescence-seeking networks must solve the problem of how to achieve their goal within a defined timeframe and then exit. Both strategies are valid; neither is universally applicable.

### 4.4 The Unresolved Tensions

Three tensions cut across all types and all patterns, and they are unresolved — not because networks have failed to address them but because they may be structural features of democratic network governance:

1. **Scale vs. Depth**: The mechanisms that produce scale (standardisation, replication, platformisation) are in tension with the mechanisms that produce depth (contextualisation, relationship-building, political commitment). Networks that maximise one tend to minimise the other. No network in the corpus has achieved both at high levels simultaneously.

2. **Autonomy vs. Sustainability**: The funding sources that enable sustainability (government budgets, foundation grants, EU programmes) are also the sources that constrain autonomy. The networks that are most autonomous (IAF, GPR2C) are least sustainable at scale. The networks that are most sustainable (Decidim, OGP) are most dependent. This may not be a solvable problem but a permanent condition of democratic network governance under current political-economic arrangements.

3. **Inclusion vs. Coherence**: Networks that maximise inclusion (open membership, diverse participation, broad scope) risk losing the coherence that makes them effective. Networks that maximise coherence (focused mission, selective membership, strong normative boundaries) risk becoming insular. KNOCA's self-limitation and Decidim's social contract are different strategies for navigating this tension; neither fully resolves it.

## 5. Conclusions and Recommendations

### 5.1 For Network Practitioners

**1. The commitment subsidy is your greatest asset and your greatest vulnerability.** It enables networks to function beyond their formal resource base, but it depends on specific individuals whose departure would leave an unfillable gap. Networks should map their commitment subsidy — who is working beyond what they are paid, and what would happen if they stopped — and develop strategies to reduce dependence on it.

**2. Self-limitation is an under-valued strategy.** KNOCA, IAF, and OIDP demonstrate that networks can be effective precisely because they limit their scope, their funding sources, or their ambitions. The growth imperative that dominates organisational thinking is not a law of nature. Networks should ask not just "what should we become?" but "what should we refuse to become?"

**3. Membership models designed for revenue-generating members exclude non-commercial participants.** Charlie's experience with Decidim's 1.5% turnover fee reveals a structural flaw in membership design: models that assume members profit from the network exclude civic organisations that contribute labour, time, and resources without financial return. Networks should audit their membership models for this exclusion and develop tiered structures that recognise different modes of contribution.

**4. The founder's paradox requires planned resolution.** Networks whose functioning depends on a founder's vision, relationships, and labour need a strategy for distributing that power that does not depend on the founder's continued presence. SOIF's six-year exit journey is one model; KNOCA's temporary-by-design is another. The strategy must be tailored to the network type, but the need for one is universal.

**5. Peripheral knowledge is an under-utilised resource.** The most innovative adaptations in the corpus — the WhatsApp API, counter-data, the participatory archive concept — were produced at the periphery. Networks should build institutional mechanisms to identify, validate, and integrate peripheral knowledge into their core knowledge base. The pathway from peripheral innovation to core integration should be as well-designed as the pathway from core production to peripheral distribution.

### 5.2 For Funders

**1. The single-funder model is high-risk but can work if time-limited.** KNOCA demonstrates that single-funder dependency can be compatible with network effectiveness and autonomy — provided the funding is for a defined period, the network's goal is achievable within that period, and the funder does not attempt to shape the network's agenda. This model is appropriate for time-limited, method-focused knowledge networks; it is not appropriate for ongoing advocacy or infrastructure networks.

**2. Funding models that assume "turnover" exclude the most innovative actors.** Charlie Ngounou's AfroLeadership spends money to participate in the Decidim ecosystem; it does not generate revenue from it. Funding models — including membership fees, grant eligibility criteria, and partnership requirements — that assume participants profit from network participation structurally exclude the civic organisations whose contributions are most needed for the network's democratic mission.

**3. The commitment subsidy is not free labour — it is deferred fragility.** When funders rely on networks whose functioning depends on individuals working beyond what they are paid, they are building on unstable foundations. Funding that covers the real cost of network operations — including the coordination, relationship-building, and knowledge management work that the commitment subsidy currently absorbs — would increase network resilience without increasing dependency.

**4. Impact measurement requirements should match network type.** Requiring scale metrics from depth-focused networks (HIC, GPR2C) or depth metrics from scale-focused networks (Decidim, OGP) produces data that satisfies reporting requirements but does not capture the network's actual contribution. Funders should work with networks to develop impact frameworks appropriate to their type, rather than imposing standardised metrics.

### 5.3 For Researchers

**1. The tool-culture gap deserves dedicated investigation.** The observation that infrastructure (technological or methodological) does not create the culture to use it appears across multiple networks. Why does this gap persist, what conditions enable it to be bridged, and what role can networks play in bridging it beyond providing the infrastructure? These questions are both theoretically significant and practically urgent.

**2. Network mortality is under-studied.** The corpus contains only one documented case of network mortality (Open Source Politics absorbed by GoVocal). Understanding why networks fail — and what distinguishes those that survive from those that do not — requires studying networks that no longer exist, which the Nets4DEM sampling frame did not capture. A dedicated study of democratic network mortality would complement the survival-focused analysis presented here.

**3. The relationship between governance innovation and democratic impact is not established.** Networks with innovative governance (Decidim's social contract, People Powered's internal PB) are assumed to have greater democratic legitimacy and impact than networks with traditional governance (EPD, FIDE). But this assumption has not been empirically tested. Does governance innovation produce better democratic outcomes, or does it primarily produce legitimacy for the network itself?

**4. The centre-periphery pattern may be irreducible under current conditions.** The persistence of centre-periphery dynamics across networks of all types, including those deliberately designed to resist them, suggests that these dynamics may be structural features of transnational network governance under current political-economic conditions — not failures of specific networks. Research should investigate whether centre-periphery dynamics can be eliminated or only managed, and what conditions would be required for genuinely distributed transnational network governance.

## Appendix B: Methodological Note — From Phase 2 to Phase 3

### B.1 The Analytical Transition

Phase 2 of the Nets4DEM project analysed each of the 14 networks through 9 standardised dimensions: Organisational Identity, Membership, Governance, Resources, External Relations, Knowledge, Adaptation, Commoning, and Impacts. This produced 14 detailed case reports, each providing within-case analysis structured around these dimensions. Phase 3 inverts the analytical lens: instead of analysing each case dimension by dimension, it identifies **cross-cutting patterns** — recurrent structural dynamics that appear across multiple cases, regardless of network type, scale, or geography.

### B.2 Pattern Identification Method

Patterns were identified through an iterative process:

1. **Extraction**: The cross-cutting analytical themes from each Phase 2 report (typically 2–5 themes per report, located at the end of each report) were extracted and compiled. These themes represent the case analysts' own identification of dynamics that cut across the 9 dimensions within their case.

2. **Clustering**: Themes from all 14 reports were clustered by similarity of the underlying dynamic. For example, themes related to funding dependency (identified in ALDA, EPD, Decidim, KNOCA, and others) were grouped; themes related to geographical power concentration (identified in Decidim, OIDP, FIDE, EPD, and others) were grouped.

3. **Pattern Formulation**: Each cluster was formulated as a pattern — a named, defined structural dynamic with a clear mechanism. The formulation was tested against the evidence: does the pattern actually appear in the cases where the cluster suggests it should? Are there cases where it should appear but doesn't (candidates for contra-examples)?

4. **Refinement**: The initial set of 12 candidate patterns was refined to 9 through consolidation (merging overlapping patterns), elimination (removing patterns supported by insufficient evidence), and sharpening (clarifying the mechanism and distinguishing the pattern from related but distinct dynamics).

### B.3 Limitations

1. **Sampling**: The 14 networks were selected for their relevance to democratic participation and governance innovation. They are not a representative sample of transnational networks in general. Patterns identified here may not generalise to networks in other domains.

2. **Interview coverage**: The number of interviews per case varies (from 1 to 4+). Cases with more interviews (Decidim: 9; People Powered: 3+; FIDE: 2) have richer evidence than cases with fewer interviews. Patterns supported primarily by single-interview cases should be treated as more tentative.

3. **Temporal scope**: All interviews were conducted in a narrow window (May–July 2026). The analysis captures networks at a specific moment; it does not capture longitudinal change. The resilience pattern (I) attempts to address this by examining networks' responses to past shocks, but the analysis is retrospective, not prospective.

4. **Researcher positionality**: The pattern identification was conducted by the Nets4DEM research team, whose analytical frameworks and theoretical commitments shaped what was visible as a pattern. Alternative analytical frameworks might identify different patterns or interpret the same evidence differently.
